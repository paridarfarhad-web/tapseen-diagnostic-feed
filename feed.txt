Batgirl: Stephanie Brown happened at an interesting point in Batman continuity. Bruce Wayne was “dead”, Dick Grayson was wearing the Batman suit in his place, and Cassandra Cain gave up her Batgirl costume to Stephanie Brown. Pretty quickly, both Dick Grayson and Barbara Gordon realize it isn’t Cass wearing the Batgirl costume anymore, and they try to stop Stephanie Brown. Wendy not only provides tech assistance but also crashes a jet to save Stephanie’s life.

Batman as a comic is full of passed-down mantles. There've been numerous Robins and numerous Batgirls, with each iteration finding a way to make the mantle their own. Oracle may have been formed out of Barbara Gordon’s specific journey, but Wendy Harris showed that it no longer needed her. It’s a running gag through the second half of the run that when Steph gets tech help, she assumes it’s Barbara talking to her, only to realize she’s talking to Wendy seconds later. Proxy gets the job done, and when Barbara is occupied either with Birds of Prey business or simply off the clock, it’s no longer as intense as it once was.

Batgirl: Stephanie Brown set Wendy up with a new status quo, much like the stories that followed The Killing Joke did with Barbara. The comic leaves Wendy there, starting another journey, one that audiences don’t get to see since the New 52 happened a month later, and Wendy Harris as a character hasn’t been revisited since. Still, her trajectory shows the importance of Oracle. Oracle not only saves the lives of the heroes she helps but also gives purpose to the person donning the moniker, even if it doesn’t “fix” them. Given the prevalence of the person in the chair as a trope and how much the heroes of today rely on technology, Oracle’s always going to be useful to have. It doesn’t have to be Proxy — that specific codename has been given to a New Teen Titan anyway — but it’s important that superheroes aren’t just one thing.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google Dune has the potential to sit among the greatest movie trilogies of all time , but it all depends on how Dune: Part Three brings the story to an end. Director Denis Villeneuve is returning for the third film, and with it, he will have successfully adapted the events of both Frank Herbert's original Dune (1965) novel and the sequel book, Dune: Messiah (1969.

However, Villeneuve has made no secret of the fact that he has approached Dune from a different angle than Herbert took with his novels. After all, modern times call for modern themes and updates to keep the story relevant to modern audiences; Villeneuve has highlighted some aspects of Dune 's world, lore, and story, while shaving off entire other chunks of Herbert's narrative. It seems to have worked: the two Dune movies, combined, have earned over a billion dollars and eight wins at the Oscars.

Dune 3 will also be making some major changes to the original text of Dune: Messiah , and Villeneueve is speaking up about some of them in a new interview.

In a new interview with TIME , Denis Villeneuve explained that the biggest change he made to the original text was with Paul Atreides' (Timothée Chalamet) love interest, the Fremen warrior Chani (Zendaya). After barely appearing in the first Dune (2021), Chani becomes one of the lead characters of Dune: Part Two . To update the original story and character, Villeneuve re-imagined Chani as someone who is far more skeptical and vocally critical of a foreigner from another world being crowned messiah of the Fremen people.

"It all started with the adaptation for Dune: Part Two . I was facing the challenge of trying to embrace Herbert’s idea to create a story that would be a warning, not a celebration. At the end of the first book, Paul wins the battle, marries a princess, still gets Chani, and becomes emperor. When you read the book, you’re like, 'This is a celebration of a hero,'" Villeneuve explained. "I had the idea to make Chani more of a critic regarding the idea of religious colonization. It’s not in the book, the idea that the older generations are contaminated by the beliefs of the Bene Gesserit, by the colonialist religious poison. And the new generation doesn’t believe in the messiah. They want Fremen to be freed by the Fremen themselves. I thought it was a dynamic idea, Chani being the representation of this."

In the first book, Chani starts as a warrior but quickly becomes a tragic, subjugated shell of herself. She and Paul lose a child, which is a major reason Paul takes the Water of Life that finally unlocks his full powers – and violent ambition. In Dune: Messiah, Chani is kept as Paul's "concubine," while she and Princess Irulan fight a battle through competing doses of poison and spice as Chani tries to secure her place by birthing Paul's heirs, and does so at the cost of her life. If you've seen the trailers for Dune: Part Three , you know that while certain story elements (like Chani birthing and raising Paul's children) may be retained, Denis Villeneuve is going a different route with his themes and Chani's arc – in many ways making her the protagonist battling Paul, who has become the antagonist of the story (you either die a hero, or…).

"In the third movie, Chani is still a critic of the choices Paul has made. She believes the Fremen should free themselves. She doesn’t want to harm Paul, but she does want to harm his regime," the Villeneuve revealed. "I would have loved to have taken Frank Herbert for a coffee about these ideas because I think he could have liked them. In his book, Chani is there to give Paul foot massages. It’s a character that has not a lot of firepower."

The trailers for Dune: Part Three have teased that the emotional climax of the film will be Paul having to confront Chani and reconcile how everything he's done as a warlord has also made him fail his true love as a man. What Villeneuve doesn't reveal is whether or not Chani gets any kind of happier ending (or at least justice) than she does in the books.

We'll find out when Dune: Part Three is in theaters on Decemmber 18th.
