He owns a couple theaters, co-hosts a movie podcast, and wrote a great book of film criticism. Still, deep down, I wonder if Quentin Tarantino is more of a TV guy. Still his cleanest screenplay, especially when things get messy. A wild bunch of color-coded badmen congregate in a safehouse. 1978’s The Inglorious Bastards was a rude romp about semi-psychotic soldiers in Occupied France. It’s the Inglourious experience in micro, full of vivid characters and fatal consequences.

Tarantino was casting Pam Grier for talent, not just stuntcasting her ‘70s fireball persona. When Jackie lands in jail, the soundtrack cranks the song Grier sang for the 1971 prison-hottie wackfest The Big Doll House — but the look on her face tells you the wild days were a long time ago. She finds an unlikely ally in Robert Forster’s Max Cherry, a bond agent weathered by years of scum and bureaucracy. Jackie’s big problem (and biggest opportunity) is Ordell Robbie, a gun dealer astounded at his own cleverness. Jackie Brown is the director at his least Tarantinoesque and most Zastoupilian, uncovering surprising soul in a landscape of food courts, airplane-hotel bars, beach pads, delusional small time crooks, and the occasional oil derrick. Transplanting Elmore Leonard’s novel from Miami, Tarantino chopped a gunfight, unrequited the romance, turned a white woman Black — and added one last ambiguous kiss.

What’s your favorite chapter? The buzz in ‘94 fixated on Vincent Vega and Mia Wallace at Jack Rabbit Slim’s, that binary-star supernova of John Travolta’s comeback and Uma Thurman’s arrival. Me and my teen buddies, scared or baffled by love, preferred the middle segment, with a katana-wielding Bruce Willis. The final act of corpse disposal has the most questionable rep, depending whether you think Tarantino’s acting is serviceable or unforgivable. (He’s fine.) Yet it’s the last bit that reveals Jackson’s Jules Winnfield as Pulp ’s moral core. Tarantino characters rarely change in the screenwriting-101 sense — he keeps casting Brad Pitt to specifically not do an emotional arc — but Jules is transformed after six point-blank bullets miraculously don’t execute him. In a moment of clarity, he starts wondering over Ezekiel 25:17, the Bible verse he’s been misquoting. Has he been his brother’s keeper? Or the tyranny of evil men? Heavy themes for a sensational quotefest, but Tarantino was always after more than his bratty referential imitators. His characters dress cool, talk cool, murder cool, and know cool things. It’s not enough to just be cool. So Jules becomes a killer who doesn’t kill — a Bad Mother Fucker trying real hard to be the shepherd.

Brad Pitt exudes folkloric chill as convertible cowboy Cliff Booth. Whereas Leonardo DiCaprio’s Rick Dalton is all hat and no cattle, a whiskey-soured mess with a nervous stutter. They’re near-nobodies in Hollywood ’s Hollywood: A stuntman who can’t get stunt jobs, a fading TV star clutching guest parts. One memorable day, they both go to different fake Wests. Rick’s on a backlot doing a cowboy TV pilot. Cliff returns to the defunct western town at Spahn Ranch, where lurks doom. A fatal mood lingers, even if you don’t know the history. Rick got famous on Bounty Law for never bringing back his targets alive. Cliff maybe-definitely killed his wife. Zoë Bell is the stuntlady who refuses to work with a spouse-killer — hilarious, since Kurt Russell plays her stuntman husband, and they were the stuntpeople trying to kill each other four movies ago.

That’s just one way Tarantino Voltrons together his whole filmography into his magnum opus. Here’s a Los Angeles thriller, a meta-western, and an alternate history, with Grindhouse -worthy re-enactments and a whole society incapable of turning off the radio or the television. There’s even — call Oliver Stone! — a meditation on screen violence, with one wannabe murderer declaring “If you grew up watching TV, that means you grew up watching murder!” Is it weird the heroes are older men, while the Mansonites (Margaret Qualley, Sydney Sweeney, Mikey Madison, Austin Butler, Maya Hawke) turned out to be Gen Z’s top stars this decade? I think Tarantino was being honest about late midlife fears, with Rick in particular worrying that some whole rising generation of longhairs is coming for his ass. There’s always been so much you don’t see in Tarantino’s movies, though, like Dogs ’ jewelry store or Pulp ’s briefcase. The climactic awe in Hollywood is how magnificently one big nothing happens. Margot Robbie’s Sharon Tate is a luminous mystery, worshipped yet unknowable. Manson himself barely appears. Tarantino resurrects an era that finished horrifically in the true 1969, then merrily twists the facts. Sadness embeds in that fantasy. We know the end came and will come again. Celluloid projection won’t return to the global multiplex, no matter how many repertory theaters Tarantino runs. Nobody lasts forever, in Hollywood or anywhere else. Good thing art is death proof.
