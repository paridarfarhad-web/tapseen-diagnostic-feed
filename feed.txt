The connection to GTA 6 isn't explicitly stated in the pop-up, though EA has already made its awareness of Rockstar's enormous upcoming release abundantly clear. On November 10, the official Madden NFL account posted , &#8220;Don't let 210GB get between you and Your League,&#8221; directly referencing the storage space players may need for GTA 6. Rockstar has confirmed that the game's pre-load can require up to 212GB, depending on the platform. Culture Crave shared the in-game message on X , showing the rather unusual request players are now encountering in Madden NFL 27

The presentation is particularly funny because EA has seemingly embraced the comparison rather than simply letting its earlier social media post do the talking. The pop-up's language and visual presentation evoke GTA 6's criminal escapades, while the request itself is about as straightforward as marketing gets. Players are being asked to keep Madden installed, and the publisher has even given them a date to look forward to before Rockstar's game arrives.

It's also a rather unusual thing to see inside a game. Publishers routinely promote upcoming updates and seasonal content, but an explicit &#8220;Don't Delete!&#8221; message is a considerably more memorable way to do it. There is no shortage of games competing for players' time, but GTA 6's reported storage requirements add another dimension to that competition. Some players will inevitably have to make room for the new release, and Madden NFL 27 is making a playful case for why it shouldn't be among the games they remove.

Games come and go. Players finish a release, move on to something else, and occasionally circle back when new content arrives. That's an entirely normal part of the medium, particularly when another major release is about to consume everyone's attention. Yet the idea that a publisher might need to remind its audience not to delete a game is a pretty remarkable reflection of how competitive the modern live-service environment has become.

Of course, EA is clearly having some fun with the situation, and the November 17 teaser gives the message a purpose beyond simply acknowledging GTA 6's looming arrival. Still, it's hard not to appreciate the absurdity of a major publisher effectively asking players to save some space for its game when one of the industry's biggest releases is about to arrive. GTA 6 hasn't even launched yet, and it's already prompting some unexpected marketing from elsewhere in the industry.

Mattel's unique release strategy for M.U.S.C.L.E . made it easier for kids and challenging for completionist adults to collect. The figurines were primarily sold in completely randomized packages of four or ten, as well as larger (loosely) themed twenty-eight packs. The four-packs retailed for a lower cost than the average action figure in 1985 or '86, making it easier for a kid to amass an impressive collection of M.U.S.C.L.E . figures on a budget of spare change and their weekly allowance. Owing to their low price point, the figures are plentiful, seeming to make their way into virtually every toy collection of the era, although the first law of toy collecting dictates that as a collector edges closer to having them all, they will inevitably never come across those last five or ten missing characters. That's why auctions like this upcoming lot at Heritage advertise and emphasize having key characters like &#8220;the Claw,&#8221; a giant disembodied hand and a cult favorite of M.U.S.C.L.E . collectors, included. Given the aforementioned random release strategy, coming across the exact fan favorite character mint-on-card is exceptionally rare.

Kinnikuman was created in 1979 by Yoshinori Nakai and Takashi Shimada, and was designed by the pair as a loose parody of Ultraman . The plot followed the masked wrestler Kinnikuman (translated roughly as &#8220;Muscleman&#8221;), deposed prince of the planet Kinniku, as he competed against the wrestlers of the wicked Chojin in a quest to prove himself worthy of Kinniku's throne. Terri-Bull / Buffaloman was one of many villains Kinnikuman faced in the ring, and would become an ally to Kinnikuman after being defeated in combat. Such was also the case for Ramenman , Ashuraman , Robin Mask , and numerous other characters who appeared in the Kinkeshi (and subsequently M.U.S.C.L.E .) toylines. The Japanese name for the line, &#8220;kinkeshi,&#8221; is a portmanteau of Kinnikuman and &#8220;keshi,&#8221; a type of soft rubber, eraser-like figurine popular in Japan. Though a popular toy in Japan, keshi figures lack the same cache in the American market, meaning that when M.U.S.C.L.E . debuted, there was not another toyline like it. Its scale, designs, and open-ended world were unlike virtually anything else on the market.

It's not unusual for an American toy company to take an existing product from Japan and rebrand it for Western audiences. Most famously, this was the origin story of the Transformers , but this practice also spawned lines like Mego's Micronauts, Mattel's Shogun Warriors , and even Tonka's Go-Bots . Where M.U.S.C.L.E . varies is that it eschewed the tie-in comic book or animated series that was used by these other properties to help familiarize kids with the line and its characters. M.U.S.C.L.E , for better or for worse, was a blank slate. Mattel's marketing materials give a little background, specifying that &#8220;Muscleman and his heroic Tag Team, the Thug Busters, are ready for all challengers. The terrible Terri-Bull and his evil cohorts [sic], the Cosmic Crunchers, want to take the world to the mat.&#8221; For the most part, which characters fit into each faction was left in the hands of kids, outside of the twenty-eight designated &#8220;Thug Busters&#8221; and &#8220;Cosmic Crunchers&#8221; included in the faction-themed packs Mattel sold. Two additional twenty-eight packs, the &#8220;Mighty Maulers&#8221; and the &#8220;Cosmic Showdown&#8221; were also available.

These twenty-eight packs were less popular than the modestly-priced four-packs, and are difficult to locate on the secondary market. More common are the ten-packs, which were sold in plastic garbage cans; a fair number of kids held onto these and used them to store their M.U.S.C.L.E . collections. Additional expressions for the line enhanced the wrestling theme, with a &#8220;Hard Knockin' Wrestling Ring&#8221; playset, a wearable &#8220;Battlin' Belt&#8221; that could house a small number of M.U.S.C.L.E . figures, and the &#8220;Mega Match&#8221; game, which created turn-based gameplay for wrestling matches between the figurines. The &#8220;Hard Knockin' Wrestling Ring&#8221; had play mechanics similar to Rock &#8216;Em, Sock &#8216;Em Robots : the players clipped one figure each into a plastic stand controlled by a joystick, enabling them to &#8220;fight&#8221; until one figure was knocked loose from the plastic clip. The playset and game are relatively rare, and command triple-digits price-points mint in box.

A promotional mail-away from Mattel threw down the gauntlet for collectors: a poster featured all two hundred and thirty-three M.U.S.C.L.E . figures, emblazoned with the rhetorical question of &#8220;Can you collect them all?&#8221; As mentioned in the introduction, the randomized nature of M.U.S.C.L.E &#8216;s distribution meant that the answer to this was &#8220;no&#8221; for all but the most diligent, obsessive fan &#8212; and that doesn't become much easier for them, even as an adult. The lack of agreed upon names adds another degree of difficulty, as seeking out individual characters on auction sites is hampered by trying to figure out which name the seller might have the character listed under. Some American M.U.S.C.L.E . collectors have taken to using the Japanese names, while other stick with the informal nicknames adopted by the American collecting community. As an example, &#8220;the Claw&#8221; advertised by Heritage's M.U.S.C.L.E . auction listing is a collector nickname for a character known in Kinnikuman as &#8220; Sunigator Shotai .&#8221; Some eBay listings for the figure use both names, some one or the other, some use neither.

Beyond the incredibly wide character selection, Mattel introduced another challenge to assembling a complete set of M.U.S.C.L.E . figures: different colored rubber. Later waves of M.U.S.C.L.E . came in eye-catching blues, reds, oranges, and greens. Including color variants, there are more than a thousand M.U.S.C.L.E . figures, though most collectors content themselves with trying to assemble a complete set of characters. M.U.S.C.L.E . is an easy line to collect, and a tough line to complete; each of these characteristics makes it attractive to different kinds of collectors. For some, M.U.S.C.L.E . is a fun line to informally gather a handful of from conventions, flea markets, and collectible stores. They're cheap, readily available, and full of creative, strange designs like the Moai-inspired &#8220; Amoiman &#8221; or the shark-headed &#8220; Jawsman .&#8221; Patience and research might be needed to track down the line's most elusive releases, and there is nothing hardcore collectors love more than a challenge. To a lesser extent, that's also the appeal of mint-on-card auctions like the Heritage listing: it captures some of the line's best characters in a rare state of preservation, creating a unique display piece that emphasizes just what makes M.U.S.C.L.E . unique.
