Games outside of the Rockstar bubble have always been influential to the GTA series, such as how the Max Payne , Saints Row , or Mafia series all had elements of gameplay Rockstar adopted into their flagship franchise. One such unlikely source of inspiration for GTA 6 is Kingdom Come: Deliverance , as well as its sequel. While Kingdom Come: Deliverance 2 came out in 2025, only a year before GTA 6 , its predecessor established much of that series signature immersive RPG depth, within a historical fantasy setting often used to tell a fantastical story of some kind. Almost taking the direct opposite route, the Kingdom Come: Deliverance games started in 2018 with a title that almost took the term "grounded approach" to an extreme. The famous part of the Kingdom Come: Deliverance games is how every action has some sort of consequence, whether for better or worse. For example, you could choose to engage with a particular quest, but if you forget about it, the people involved in it could die off-screen in a few days. Similarly, you have to eat, drink, sleep, and perform basic physical health activities to keep your stats up, requiring a level of maintenance for your character that most RPGs fear doing. You may notice that a version of a physical health mechanic in GTA 6 does practically the same thing, revealing this inspiration in an obvious fashion.

Most of Kingdom Come: Deliverance is non-linear as well, with players have to go out into the world and figure out what they're going to do as they travel. Much like GTA games, players who commit crimes in certain regions will be hunted, making that non-linear travel more complicated. Reputation plays a big factor in this RPG series, and it is something players might notice is more emphasized in GTA 6 than it has been in past games. The Heat system in particular from GTA 6 reflects the design of Kingdom Come: Deliverance in some ways, creating a greater sense of immersion by having the player's impact on the world be felt at every step.

The map design of GTA 6 is by far the most detailed in the series, with players still discovering secrets in the days after the game's launch. "What is Shenmue ?" is a question I still ask myself, as the strange but beloved series is something of an enigma even to those who played it. Essentially, Shenmue is a series that is initially about a standard martial arts revenge story, but blossoms into a fully detailed simulation of daily life that many players might find tedious. While 2019's Shemue 3 may be controversial for its lack of modern design features with this concept, the earlier Shenmue games are truly innovative for being the first games to ever immerse players in a "life-sim" approach.

Everything about a Shenmue game is a test of patience, as players have to wait until certain hours of the day to do quests, sometimes participate in getting various chores done, or literally interact with everything in the world. Working for Atlas Comics in the '50s, he would become an important part of Marvel's return to superheroes. He's most known as the co-creator of Spider-Man and Doctor Strange , working with writer Stan Lee, but he also had quite a career outside the House of Ideas. Over at Charlton Comics, he created characters like Ted Kord, the second Blue Beetle, Captain Atom, and the Question and would also have a pretty fruitful tenure with DC Comics as well. He created the Creeper, a psychotic television host vigilante, the teen duo Hawk and Dove, and one more character you've probably never head of – Shade the Changing Man.

Ditko characters often went in certain directions – many of them shared his Randian belief system (it's always fun to read early libertarian Peter Parker) or were all about finding the "truth" like the Question and the Creeper. Shade, though, was quite different from all of the rest. Rac Shade was an alien from the planet Meta (unrelated to the Zuck), on the run from the militant authorities and armed with the M-Vest. Shade was a rare foray into sci-fi for Ditko and while the hero never really caught on back in the day, he's had a rich second life, playing a bigger role in the history of the comic industry than most fans realize.

Ditko's characters may not have all been Objectivist (a libertarian belief system that posits that the individual's job isn't to help the collective, but just themselves), but all of them had a certain "fight the power" feel to them. Shade the Changing Man is a perfect example of this. Rac came from a Meta, a world where the rules were followed to a militant degree. He was a secret agent who was eventually framed for treason, stealing the M-Vest and heading to Earth where he could be free.

The M-Vest allowed Shade to create powerful forcefields and make himself into a bigger, more monstrous fighter. The character first debuted in 1977, representing the first major superhero Ditko had created in years, but wasn't a massive hit. The first series ran for eight issues before folding, with Shade joining the army of obscure DC characters. In the '80s, he would end up showing up as a member of the Suicide Squad, which lead to him going back to Meta. However, things were about to look all the way up.

Sidetrack time! In 1982, British writer Alan Moore was given the reins of Saga of the Swamp Thing after years of success in British comic magazines like 2000 A.D. and Warrior . Moore's Swamp Thing took superhero comics in a more mature direction , starting a revolution that would lead DC to decide that the British comic scene was home to a lot of untapped talent. As the '80s went on, DC bigwigs Jenette Kahn, Paul Levitz, and Dick Giordano would meet with British talent, leading to the British Invasion of 1989.

Writers Neil Gaiman, Grant Morrison, and Peter Milligan were the first big gets of this program and each of them were given their choice of obscure DC character to write. Gaiman chose Black Orchid (with his accent, the editor thought he said "Black Hawk Kid", as related in The Sandman Companion ; I was once a massive fan of the writer before the allegations came out), Morrison Animal Man, and Milligan would go with Shade. It's weird to think that the entire comic industry was changed forever because of Animal Man, Black Orchid, and Shade the Changing Man

Shade the Changing Man (Vol. 2) dropped in 1990, from Milligan and future X-Men superstar artist Chris Bachalo. Instead of being a secret agent, this new Rac Shade was a poet sent from Meta, now more hippie than militant, to save Earth from a tide of madness that threatened to destroy the planet. The M-Vest was now the Madness Vest, which could warp reality and his first major enemy was the American Scream, the living embodiment of the darkness that Rac was sent to fight. The secret agent was gone, replaced by a lovelorn poet.

It's always interesting to see how creators take old ideas and remake them. Ditko's beliefs usually got left out of the characters he created as the years went by, the new Rac Shade was basically the completely different from the old one; in fact, you could say that he was meant to be a critique on Ditko and his belief system (Milligan retconned Ditko's Meta by having Rac say it was all stuff he made up to entertain himself while he was on Earth). Meta was remade from the Cold War nightmare into something more akin to the Summer of Love (the one in the '90s, not the one in the '60s; some of you know what I'm talking about and some of you are much too American to know the pop culture of other countries) and suddenly, Rac was all about helping people and making their lives better. Much like Morrison, Milligan was plugged into the rave culture of the time and Shade the Changing Man played into this.

The book dealt with subjects like the JFK assassination (we were obsessed with the JFK assassination in the '90s) and transgender issues (Milligan's Enigma is a lost queer history gem), and its subject matter and the more mature way it and the other British Invasion comics were written led to DC creating the Vertigo imprint in 1993. Shade never reached the level of popularity or The Sandman, Hellblazer, Animal Man, Swamp Thing or Doom Patrol , but it had a cult following, the book running for 70 issues.

DC tried to bring Shade back as a superhero character several times over the years after the end of the Vertigo series to no avail, which would lead to another modification of the character. In 2016, DC announced the Young Animal imprint from My Chemical Romance singer/comic mega-fan Gerard Way and one of the inaugural books was Shade the Changing Girl . This version combined aspects of both – Meta was a world where emotion was punished and poet Loma Shade stole the Madness Vest and went to Earth – and ran through two series, the first for 12 issues and the second, titled Shade the Changing Woman , running for six.

Steve Ditko's place as a Marvel legend is cemented, but his time outside that publisher doesn't get as much mainstream recognition. His DC creations are some of his most interesting and Shade the Changing Man was a cool idea that proved to have longer legs than his initial eight-issue offering led readers to believe. His return in the late '80s as a part of the British Invasion proved that failures didn't always happen because the ideas are bad – they just need a little push (to the left; there's a moral there).

He clearly had a sci-fi angle on how the robotic beings interpreted the fight between light and dark side Force users like the Jedi and Sith, or the geopolitics that pushed things towards multiple cycles of galactic war. Fincher's film would apparently have examined a record of the Skywalker Saga and Galactic Civil War, which was stored inside a droid like C-3PO, and offer a new artificial intelligence perspective on it all.

Ironically, Star Wars comics would repurpose the idea of how droids view being servants to the warring and self-destructive cycles of organic lifeforms in the 2023 story arc, "Dark Droids". A droid A.I. and Sith relic known as the "Spark Eternal" combined to create "The Scourge," a self-aware AI able to possess other droids and machines as part of its hive consciousness. C-3PO was one of the first and primary host bodies Scourge used to conduct its experiments with possessing organics with its machine consciousness. The villain gave hell to both the Rebellion and Empire before finally being defeated. It was a compelling story, which lends credit to the fact that Fincher's film would've been one, as well. But fate had other plans:

"So that's what we had started talking about, and then I said to her [Kathleen Kennedy], 'I kind of pictured this idea.' I said, 'Let's take this thing to Wall Street and let the market decide.
