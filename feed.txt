Back in 1988, in Japan — and then in 1992 in the United States — developer Chunsoft and publisher Enix, before the latter merged with Square Soft to become known as Square Enix, released Dragon Quest III: The Seeds of Salvation . If this name doesn't sound familiar at all, it may be because in the United States the game was known as Dragon Warrior III . In 1996, the game was remade for the SNES, and then remade again in 2001 for the Game Boy Color. More than two decades after this, a third remake was released in 2024 called Dragon Quest III HD-2D Remake . It's this remake that has been made free via PS Plus Extra and Plus Premium.

The original game was well received back in 1988. This is most evident by the fact that it was the best-selling game of the late 1980s, at least in Japan. In the United States, its footprint was smaller, just like the series as a whole. While it didn't sell as well as it did in Japan, it did receive a warm reception among Western consumers. And the same is true of Dragon Quest III HD-2D Remake , which hit an 84 on Metacritic . Meanwhile, it has a similar user review score on the PlayStation Store. To this end, it has a 4.24 out of 5 stars on the digital PlayStation Store, which is roughly an 85 on a 100-point scale.

A Classic RPG Reborn With a New Look Dragon Quest III HD-2D Remake is specifically a remake of the classic RPG done in the art style of Octopath Traveler , another Square Enix RPG series that debuted in 2018. And if you know your RPGs, you will know Octopath Traveler's primary claim to fame is its beautiful art style, which combines 2D traditional RPG art with a modern 3D presentation.

Dragon Quest III is a self-contained and isolated story. It's not connected to its previous two games, which it actually serves as a prequel to in terms of timeline. As for the game's story, it puts you into the shoes of the 16-year-old son of Ortega, a great hero who previously left his wife and child behind on a quest to defeat the villainous Archfiend, Baramos, but failed. Baramos still threatens the world, and now the king of Aliahan has summoned you to do what your father couldn't.

Those who end up checking out the Square Enix RPG now that it is free with two of the three tiers of PlayStation Plus should expect to sink 30 to 35 hours into the game just to mainline it. Add side content, and this range is more like 40 to 45 hours. Meanwhile, completionists will need to put more like 65 to 70 hours into the old-school RPG.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google It's no secret that Game of Thrones is one of the most successful and popular fantasy series of all time. Based on George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire novels, HBO's Game of Thrones proved to be a massive global phenomenon, quickly becoming known as one of the best fantasy TV shows of all time . While fantasy stories have long been among the most enduring and popular in fiction, Game of Thrones marked a shift in small-screen entertainment. As well as ushering in a new era of prestige television, Game of Thrones also definitively proved that, if appropriate care was taken in the adaptation process, bringing fantasy stories to life in live-action could provide compelling entertainment that would grip audiences around the world.

There can be no massive success in the entertainment industry without a wave of imitations following in its wake. The same was certainly true for the adaptations of GRRM's iconic novels, as numerous shows were made to target Game of Thrones fans following the show's unprecedented popularity. However, of all the shows that tried to become the next Game of Thrones , none really achieved it, earning mixed success that failed to live up to the series that kicked off the modern fantasy TV boom. While the following shows each have their own qualities that make them worth watching, none had the compelling stories or longevity that made Game of Thrones such a major success.

Even as Game of Thrones was approaching its peak, other networks were attempting to capture its success with their own similar projects. The mid-2010s saw a wave of medieval TV shows featuring a mix of political intrigue and brutal violence, and, considering the sheer quantity of these series that were introduced in that period, it's no surprise that some were pretty quickly forgotten. Created by Kurt Sutter of Sons of Anarchy fame, The Bastard Executioner was just one such show, airing for just one season before its cancellation.

In terms of tone, it's clear that The Bastard Executioner was angling to become the next Game of Thrones . Trying to establish an ensemble cast of morally complex characters alongside a whole medieval mythology was always going to be a huge undertaking, and it's something that The Bastard Executioner really struggled with. It proved to be a passable series in many ways, but it was never any sort of match for Game of Thrones ' sprawling universe of intricate familial hierarchies, resulting in Kurt Sutter's show failing miserably at challenging the HBO behemoth.

When it comes to fantasy TV shows cancelled too soon , it's genuinely staggering just how many can be attributed to Netflix refusing to give shows the time they need to grow. This was exactly what happened with Cursed , a series based on Frank Miller and Tom Wheeler's novel of the same name, which itself was written as a reimagining of Arthurian legend. Taking well-known fantasy source material and putting its own unique spin on the tale could have proved a route to success, and while Cursed got off to a bit of a slow start, the potential was plain to see for many fantasy fans.

While Cursed was tipped by many as Netflix's attempt at creating its own successor to Game of Thrones ' fantasy crown, the streaming service opting to axe it after just a single season was ultimately the reason it failed. It might not have been the same instant success as Game of Thrones , but Netflix choosing to swiftly cut its losses wasted a huge amount of potential. Cursed had political intrigue, eye-catching fantasy action, and a large-scale fantasy setting that could possibly have grown into a major contender in the fantasy genre, but by cancelling it prematurely, Netflix ironically secured Cursed 's status as a failed fantasy experiment.

Of all the shows that tried to follow in Game of Thrones ' footsteps, Shadow and Bone stands out as one of those that came closest to achieving comparable results. Another fantasy TV show cancelled by Netflix, Shadow and Bone aired for two seasons before its untimely end was announced. Based on Leigh Bardugo's Grishaverse novels, the series balanced faithfully adapting its source material with some original additions, a move that proved popular with fans and secured the series a considerable following.

Despite a growing fan base, Netflix's decision to cancel Shadow and Bone prevented it from securing the sort of legacy it probably deserved. In terms of pure quality and appeal, it might not have quite matched up to Game of Thrones , but it was an impressive and original fantasy show that really did deserve much better. It was a show whose unique elements and quality could easily have grown into a major fantasy success, but Netflix's hasty decision to end its run marked another instance of bizarre self-sabotage in the streaming giant's apparent intention to recreate Game of Thrones ' popularity.

NBC was another network that tried to jump on the prestige fantasy boom, only to fail miserably. 2017's Emerald City sought to reimagine the iconic fantasy world of L. Frank Baum's The Wizard of Oz , putting a unique spin on the well-loved story that would allow it to step into the shoes of Game of Thrones , whose run was beginning to approach its end at the time. Taking the familiar setting and characters and rewriting them to incorporate a more mature tone and a much darker atmosphere, Emerald City certainly seemed to have potential.

That is, however, on paper. In reality, Emerald City was incredibly disappointing and is cited by some as ranking among the worst fantasy shows ever made . For all the imaginative ideas that went into reworking the world of Oz, the show simply never did anything to make it stand out as particularly unique or innovative. Instead, it felt like a generic fantasy series desperately seeking major success by trying to replicate the ideas of much better shows. By trying too hard to be just like Game of Thrones , Emerald City only succeeded in cementing itself as a poor imitation that wasted an interesting central idea concerning its beloved source material.

Very few fantasy TV projects seemed, on paper at least, to be better positioned to become the next Game of Thrones than Amazon's adaptation of Robert Jordan's The Wheel of Time . Taking source material that is actually far more beloved than George R. R. Martin's novels and casting Rosamund Pike to lead its ensemble, The Wheel of Time seemed poised for great things. As the book the show sought to adapt was one of the most seminal, groundbreaking works of fantasy fiction out there, The Wheel of Time seemed a perfect fit to fill the Game of Thrones -shaped hole in fantasy fans' lives.

Instead, The Wheel of Time proved to be a fantasy TV show where the worldbuilding was better than the story . It painted a picture of an intriguing fantasy world that felt rich and original, but its actual narrative was a little underserved. The sheer scale of the source material worked against the adaptation in a major way, and despite the show getting consistently better as it went on, declining viewership led to it being cancelled after just three seasons. While The Wheel of Time probably deserved, on balance, to become the next Game of Thrones , the slightly botched execution ultimately prevented it from living up to that potential.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google The label “unadaptable” has been slapped onto many works of literature over the years, especially in science fiction . Since the birth of the film industry in 1895, studios, producers, and filmmakers have been trying to film the unfilmable, finding creative ways to capture complex worlds and internal struggles with cinematic visuals. Historically, when someone attempts to adapt an “unadaptable” story, detractors, fans, and sometimes even the authors of the original works come out against the project, claiming it can’t be done.

But as the following films prove, telling Hollywood it can’t do something only adds fuel to the fire. Regardless of previous stalled projects or failed attempts, a visionary filmmaker eventually steps up to the plate, ready to take on the challenge. The result, at least in these cases, is a masterful mind-bending tale or sweeping science fiction odyssey that wows audiences in theaters and becomes a home-viewing classic that stands the test of time.

Watchmen began as a 12-issue comic series from DC, written by Alan Moore and illustrated by Dave Gibbons. But Moore himself argued that Watchmen wasn’t cinematic, noting that a comic reader can flip back and connect details, while film forces you to watch at 24 frames per second. Another problem was the scope. After Moore declined to write the screenplay, screenwriter Sam Hamm attempted to squeeze the 338-page comic into a 128-page script. When the project moved to Warner Bros., it attached sci-fi master Terry Gilliam, who was unhappy with Hamm’s script, bringing in Charles McKeown to rewrite it using Rorschach’s journal as voice-over. Although it seemed they’d gotten closer, Gilliam’s film also collapsed over budget, and later drafts by David Hayter replaced it.

Warner Bros. eventually hired director Zack Snyder in 2006, and the rest is history. Snyder pulled it off by using the comic itself as his storyboard. He added a title montage to explain the alternate history, and used a simplified ending taken from Hayter. Moore himself ultimately took no credit for the film and gave his share of the income to Gibbons. Gibbons said that watching it on DVD would feel like flipping through the book, since viewers could pause and rewind, and he was right: DVD sales are where Watchmen made its money back. Today, Watchmen is considered a superhero sci-fi classic, and it even launched a Watchmen sequel series from Damon Lindelof .

Philip K. Dick’s A Scanner Darkly entranced readers with the story of an undercover cop in a near-future world who gets involved with a dangerous new drug and begins to lose his identity. However, Dick’s writing is known for being extremely internal, leading readers through the breakdown of the protagonist’s perception from the inside; something nearly impossible to show visually on screen. Director Richard Linklater, who had experimented with rotoscoping on Waking Life , was interested in adapting one of Dick’s novels. He first played around with Ubik , but eventually turned to A Scanner Darkly , deciding to direct it as an intimate animated sci-fi thriller, made for only $8.7 million. His script followed the novel closely, and when he pitched it to Dick’s estate, it was approved.

It was ultimately Linklater’s rotoscoped animation that was key to the film’s success. The technique involved filming live actors (including Keanu Reeves, Robert Downey Jr., Woody Harrelson, and Winona Ryder), then having animators paint over the footage frame by frame. The result is a shifting, dreamlike animation that conveys the character’s fragmented, drug-induced perception while still allowing the actors to give live performances. Instead of turning it into a big-budget action thriller, Linklater leaned into the story's internal nature, helming what is now considered not only a cult classic, but one of the more successful Dick adaptations and one of Linklater’s most unique films.

Frank Herbert’s space opera opus Dune built out an entire galaxy’s politics, theology, ecology, and characters. The first notable adaptation attempt came when director Alejandro Jodorowsky began plotting his film, which included music by Pink Floyd and a dream cast of Orson Welles, Salvador Dalí, and Mick Jagger. Trouble was, Jodorowsky’s script would have made a 14-hour movie, and he’d already spent $2 million of the $9 million budget on pre-production. Thus, the project stalled. Next, Ridley Scott was hired to direct and was the first to consider splitting the book into two films. However, Scott’s version focused on an incestuous relationship, which outraged Herbert, and Scott ultimately dropped the project to make Blade Runner . David Lynch then became the first to put the book on screen, though he famously didn’t get final cut, and the result was so disastrous that Lynch disowned the film.

Enter modern-day sci-fi master Denis Villeneuve. Following the idea Scott first floated, he split the story into two films. Using minimal dialogue, he relied on largely practical visual world-building and took a restrained approach to adapting the story and characters, leaving the audience to build out much of the world for themselves without over-explaining the complicated lore and terminology. His adaptations have finally proven a success, making the unfilmable filmable, with Dune: Part Two making over $714 million worldwide. His upcoming Dune: Part Three , based on the next novel, Dune Messiah , is set to open in December as the final film in the trilogy.
