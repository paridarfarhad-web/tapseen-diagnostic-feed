Harrelson discussed his initial reservations about joining the franchise in a conversation with his successor, Joseph Zada, for Vanity Fair . "I thought it was good, I read the books, really good, but I just don’t think there’s enough to do. I don’t know if there’s enough to do. It’s an arbitrary, odd way of assessing it." The director told Harrelson that he didn't have a second choice for Haymitch. After about ten minutes on the phone with Ross, Harrelson agreed to do the movie. He said, "Gary calls me, and he's like, 'Woody, you have to do this part. I don't have a second choice. You have to play Haymitch.’ And he just talked to me for about 10 minutes on the phone. I'm like, 'All right, okay, yeah, let's do it.'"

Harrelson has seen what Zada does with the character in The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping and couldn't be happier with his successor. “I really can't imagine anyone doing a better job,” Harrelson told the young actor. He was particularly impressed with how Zada develops the cynicism that would come to define Haymitch. While audiences were introduced to the character as a hard-drinking, cynical mentor to Katniss and Peeta, Zada has the opportunity to show audiences the young man Haymitch was before the Capitol robbed him of his optimism.

It's somewhat ironic that Harrelson was initially concerned about the size of the role given how much of the larger story of The Hunger Games hinges on Haymitch. He's one of the few characters who understands what Katniss is going through long before she becomes the face of the rebellion. Having survived the Second Quarter Quell , Haymitch knows that winning the Hunger Games doesn't necessarily mean you've beaten the Capitol. That informed Harrelson's performance across four movies, but audiences are only now getting to see how Haymitch learned that lesson.

Haymitch isn't even the only franchise mentor Harrelson has turned down twice. The actor was equally reluctant to play Tobias Beckett, Han Solo's mentor in Solo: A Star Wars Story . In that instance, it was producer Allison Shearmur who convinced Harrelson to take the role. Unfortunately, not every filmmaker has been as persistent with Harrelson as Ross and Shearmur were. What's remarkable about Collins' Sunrise on the Reaping is how much of the young Haymitch she introduces in the book feels consistent with Harrelson's interpretation of the character. Even without knowing the full extent of Haymitch's history, Harrelson always managed to convey how much the man cared about the kids he was being asked to send to their deaths. Now, thanks to Collins and Zada, audiences have a much better understanding of why. For a character Harrelson initially thought didn't have enough to do, Haymitch has proven to be one of the richest characters in the entire franchise. Toys and comics were further intermingled in the 1980s — a great decade for both toys and comics — when several franchises included mini-comics with the release of individual toy figures. Now seen as valuable collectibles, these mini-comics were just bonuses with the toy when they first arrived. Some lines were more successful than others, launching line after line of mini-comics while others stopped after only three issues. Although toy giant Mattel acquired it in 1996, Tyco carved out its own niche in the toy market for a time. Tyco was founded in the 1950s as a producer of toy train kits before moving on to pre-built trains, slot cars, and remote control vehicles. In December of 1987, Tyco launched a toy line known as Dino-Riders. With each dinosaur figure released, kids got a mini-comic. Since it was a new toy, Tyco figured a comic would help market the action figures . According to the comic, Dino-Riders was about the battle between the superhuman Valorians and the humanoid Rulons, set during dinosaur times on Earth.

The comic was sixteen pages long, and Tyco would release two issues of it. The second issue continued the narrative from the first one and was included in each box of the toy’s second, third, and Ice-Age series. The mini-issues were written by regular Tyco freelancer Paul Kirchner. In addition to Dino-Riders , Kirchner wrote for Tyco’s Crash Dummies and Spy-Tech toy lines. It would hardly be his only contribution to toy comics, given his work with G.I. Joe, He-Man, Thundercats, and Power Rangers in the 1980s and 1990s.

Before the company filed for bankruptcy and had its products bought out by Hasbro in 1989, Coleco Industries, known for Cabbage Patch Dolls, created a line of action figures. While each toy’s mini-comic was meant to spotlight the character or playset it came with, some comics were more common than others, such as “The World of Symbion”.

MASK had three mini-comics that came out with the Kenner toys. The comics were randomly included in 1985’s series one boxes, and they were the first comics that were produced for MASK. Masters of the Universe was a force to be reckoned with during the 1980s. Unlike the other entries on this list, Masters of the Universe as a brand only dates back to 1982. Given that the line was new, the Masters of the Universe action figures came with a mini-comic meant to introduce not only the individual toy’s character but also the larger lore around Eternia.
