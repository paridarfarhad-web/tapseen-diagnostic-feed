The Bronze Age was an era of growing pains for DC Comics, who were struggling to adapt to a changing marketplace and increasingly stiff competition from Marvel. For readers, there was one clear benefit: the publisher understood themselves to be losing their grip on what was popular or appealing to its readers, and was willing to take risks. Editors let creatives push the comic book medium and the super-hero genre in new directions, and the result was some of the strangest, most exciting stories DC ever published. Jack Kirby's epic " Fourth World " saga emerged from this era, as did Denny O'Neil and Neal Adams' character-redefining Green Lantern / Green Arrow and Batman runs. However, few Bronze Age titles are as iconic or as sought-after as the original Len Wein and Bernie Wrightson issues of Swamp Thing .

Besides Kirby's aforementioned Fourth World titles ( The New Gods especially), few Bronze Age DC titles are as collectible as Swamp Thing 's first volume. Wrightson lent interiors and stunning covers to the first ten, while Wein scripted the first thirteen. This Heritage Auctions lot assembles the first twenty-one issues of Swamp Thing in a single lot, all in high-grade condition. Changing censorship standards in the early seventies allowed for horror comics — after being functionally banned for more than a decade — to make their return to newsstands. A generation of creators who had grown up on the controversial E.C. titles of the 1950s were entering the field, and were eager to lend their talents to the genre. Anthology titles like House of Mystery and House of Secrets were converted into horror books, and strong reader response supported an explosion of new horror titles like The Witching Hour, The Phantom Stranger , and The Demon . Swamp Thing was spun-off from a short story in House of Secrets #92, also by Wein and Wrightson. For the ongoing series, the duo retold the Southern gothic origin of the character, updating it for the modern era. While House of Secrets ' Alex Olsen became Swamp Thing because of a fire set by a romantic rival, Swamp Thing 's Alec Holland was killed for refusing to give over the secrets of his world-hunger ending bio-restorative formula to a shady criminal cabal. What didn't change was Wrightson's striking design for Swamp Thing, which resembles the artist's later interpretation of the Frankenstein Monster .

A more direct take on the Frankenstein Monster would come the following issue with the "Patchwork Man," a demented experiment by the mad Anton Arcane, who revives his brother Gregori from the dead after he's blown apart by a forgotten World War-era landmine. For Len Wein's initial thirteen issues especially, Swamp Thing existed in a dark, gothic world that — but for a team-up with Batman in Swamp Thing #7 — felt totally outside the DC Universe. It was a world of witches, eldritch horrors, and clockwork men, and every issue felt like it plunged Swamp Thing into a different nightmare out of Poe, Bierce, or Lovecraft. That was, however, Swamp Thing 's failing: Wein didn't know how to break Swamp Thing out of a "monster of the week" formula, and by the end of his thirteen-issue tenure, he felt the title was growing stale. Bird singled out Nicholson's decision to spend much of Chinatown with a bandage on his nose. "He's snoopy and he's sticking the nose where it shouldn't be, and the nose actually pays the price," Bird explained. "And the lack of vanity he had of going through that movie with this just honking bandage on his nose is just… that's great filmmaking."
