The story of Iceman's homosexuality is one of the most contentious in comics for a variety of reasons. To begin with, there's this idea that you can never change the sexuality of established characters, that they can only be gay or straight or bi or ace or whatever the whole time. No one is allowed to come out of the closet for some fans, something that is honestly pretty common in the real world. These fans started an uproar over the very concept of gay Iceman. Looking at the history of the character, it honestly makes all the sense in the world that he was gay, but the problem, one that the MCU can solve easily , is the coming out moment itself.

So, to begin with, let's get this out of the way – Bobby Drake wasn't created to be gay, but then again, none of those original superheroes were. It was a different time in American pop culture and you wouldn't get a gay character in what was essentially children's media back in the day. For his early years, Iceman was a straight character. Honestly, if you know people who came out later in life, Bobby shares a lot of similarities with them.

Besides Kirby's aforementioned Fourth World titles ( The New Gods especially), few Bronze Age DC titles are as collectible as Swamp Thing 's first volume. Changing censorship standards in the early seventies allowed for horror comics — after being functionally banned for more than a decade — to make their return to newsstands. A generation of creators who had grown up on the controversial E.C. titles of the 1950s were entering the field, and were eager to lend their talents to the genre. Subsequent writers David Michelinie and Gerry Conway had the same struggles, compounded by another mounting problem: waning reader interest.

The horror comic book explosion burned bright, but it did not burn for long. By the mid-seventies, titles like Tomb of Dracula , Werewolf by Night , and Swamp Thing were struggling to attract the strong readership of just a few years prior. Creators also found themselves burnt out, finding the storytelling potential of these character surprisingly limited. Swamp Thing 's final issues (by Gerry Conway, in collaboration with David Anthony Kraft for #24) attempted to revive the title by drastically changing its status quo: Alec Holland regains his humanity at the end of issue #23, and does not appear as Swamp Thing at all in the final issue. For the rest of the decade, Swamp Thing appeared only sporadically, serving as a supporting character to the Challengers of the Unknown (again written by Gerry Conway), as well as a guest star in team-up titles The Brave and the Bold and DC Comics Presents . The early issues were well-regarded by fans, but it wasn't enough to ensure a future — at least not until one of those fans bought the film rights.

Swamp Thing was a dormant character when entertainment lawyer, academic, and comic book geek Michael Uslan bought the film rights from DC. Uslan had been stymied by a years-long effort to get a darker, more grounded Batman film off the ground, and decided it would be easier to have creative control over a less well-known DC character. He chose Swamp Thing , feeling its stories and tone would be well-suited to adaptation. With Wes Craven in the director's chair, Uslan and business partner Benjamin Melniker set Swamp Thing for a 1982 release. To capitalize, DC decided to revive the Swamp Thing comic, launching Saga of the Swamp Thing at the same time as the film hit theaters. Saga of the Swamp Thing hit the same creative speed-bumps as its predecessor, and was due for cancellation by the time writer Martin Pasko left the series. Having little to lose by the decision, DC gave Saga of the Swamp Thing to a relative industry newcomer, British writer Alan Moore. Moore was told he could do as he liked with the title, given its precarious state.

The result was one of the most critically acclaimed comics ever published, turning Swamp Thing into one of DC's most popular characters among comic readers. Succeeded by penciller and co-plotter Rick Veitch, Moore's Swamp Thing brought complex, long-form storytelling to the character, taking it in strange directions in the process. Instead of a mutated Alec Holland, Swamp Thing was a plant that believed it was Alec Holland, the latest in a mystic line of "Guardians of the Green." This Swamp Thing was instrumental in the creation of DC's adult-oriented "Vertigo" imprint, as well as the gradual abandonment of the Comics Code Authority. It's perhaps best known for introducing John Constantine , routinely upheld alongside Swamp Thing as one of DC's best characters.

Although lauded for its experimentation and innovation, Moore and Veitch's Swamp Thing stayed rooted in the work of Wein and Wrightson, not only in using characters and ideas from the original run, but evoking its mood. Reprinted as The Roots of the Swamp Thing contemporaneously to Moore's ongoing, the first volume of Swamp Thing cemented itself as one of DC's most influential comics in the 1980s. It was at this time that those early issues took on their collectible status, which has only grown through the intervening decades as demand continues to outstrip supply. Both the Wein and the Moore runs of Swamp Thing have remained constantly in print since the rise of trade paperbacks and omnibuses, leaving new readers to discover their work every year. If they're lucky, it may be one such reader who wins the twenty-one issues of Swamp Thing on auction at Heritage, which planted the seed for the decades of Swamp Thing stories to follow.
