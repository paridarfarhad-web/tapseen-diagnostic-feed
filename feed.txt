Superhero comics have changed a lot over the years and as much as I love DC, even I have to admit that Marvel played a massive role in the evolution of the medium. Marvel is all about changing the game, with numerous reveals that set the comic industry on fire.

There's nothing like picking up a comic and getting a jaw-dropping moment. It gets even better when that jaw-dropping moment ripples outward, transforming everything it touches. Captain America is Marvel's greatest soldier , having fought against evil in World War II and beyond. Cap was teamed up with Bucky, a garishly dressed young man with a smile on his face and a machine gun in his hand. Bucky'd end up dead by the end of the war, and his loss was one of the formative ones of Cap's existence. Back in the day, we used to joke about how the only characters who stayed dead in the Marvel Universe were Uncle Ben, Bucky, and Gwen Stacy. It was a fun joke, one that we were able to use until 2005, when Captain America (Vol. 5) began. Issue #6 was the one that revealed the truth about Bucky – he hadn't died when the drone bomb exploded. He was gravely injured, but found by the Soviets. They experimented on him, giving him a metal arm to replace the one he lost, the Infinity Formula to keep him hale and hearty, and brainwashed him into becoming the Winter Soldier. The return of Bucky is one of those moments that you really had to have been there for. It set the industry on fire and, because of the MCU, has become a huge moment in pop cultural history (even if we didn't know it at the time).

Silva, House of X told the story of the X-Men establishing the mutant nation of Krakoa, while Powers of X dealt with the ramifications of Krakoa across space and time. House of X #1 ended with the mutants telling humanity they had new gods and the the second issue blew the whole thing wide open.

The issue gave us her nine previous lives, showing how often mutants had lost the war against humanity. It changed everything we thought we knew about the X-Men.

"Fatal Attractions" was 1993's big X-Men event. The story brought back Magneto, thought dead since X-Men (Vol. 2) #3. The mutant master of magnetism had transformed Cable's satellite Graymalkin into Avalon and began to offer chosen mutants a place next to him. X-Men (Vol. 2) #25 revealed why when he released a world-crippling EMP. Thanks to their Shi'Ar technology, the X-Men were the only one able to answer leading to a battle on Avalon. We all know what happens next – Wolverine tried to kill Magneto (he did almost succeed), but got his adamantium skeleton ripped out because he decided to sass Mags instead of stab him in the throat, which then led to Xavier mindwiping Magneto.

Gone were the razor sharp adamantium talons and in their place are bone claws. This was the beginning of Wolverine's bone claw era, six years of stories about Wolvie learning to do more with less, including some of the best Wolverine stories ever . Remember, he was Marvel's most popular character of the day and they dropped this kind of change on him for six years. Marvel used to be something.

Given that comic book heroes are designed to star in never-ending stories, they need strong, defined starting points from which all future stories can draw inspiration. Status quo is king, after all, so origins need to serve as a thesis statement for how these characters develop into who they are. Importantly, they also need to be adaptable. Given that heroes tend not to age, they typically exist on a sliding timescale, meaning that their earliest years are repeatedly moved up in time to be closer to the present day. Every few years, origin stories tend to be reimagined and updated. All except for "Year One."

Batman's "Year One" is often cited as not just his definitive origin, but one of his greatest stories put to page . It modernized Bruce Wayne's journey from trained warrior to Batman, even adding Jim Gordon's arrival and rise in Gotham. It was a foundational tale about their partnership, and it's been treated as his definitive origin ever since, even superseeding the New 52's "Zero Year." However, it was only meant to serve as his post- Crisis reintroduction, not as his singular starting point. Every other popular hero has had their origin retold at least once by this point, so today, we're examining exactly what made "Year One" work so well, and why it still might be time for a new version.

Before diving into why we need a new story to supersede "Year One," let's talk about why it's stuck around for so long. For starters, it provided the foundation for the modern interpretation of Batman while simultaneously being vague enough in characterization to make practically any interpretation of the character work. Batman is presented as a brooding but inherently kind man, who strives to help as much as he does to inflict vengeance. It leans into every part of Batman's character like balancing on a tightrope. Beyond that, it's incredibly well-written, knowing exactly what to focus on to build the mythos, reimagining past scenes with a gravitas and seriousness that helped cement Batman as someone important.

I could rave about "Year One" all day, but suffice to say, it's a perfect starting ground for Batman because it's a short, contained story that tells you everything you need to know about how he operated in his early years and how his partnership with Gordon started. Any fan could read it in an afternoon and dive into any other modern Batman comic with at least a decent understanding of who Batman is. It's timeless yet feels distinctly grounded in its own time, which both helps sell it but also is exactly the reason that this comic needs to be reworked for the current timeline. It is, after all, a snapshot of Batman's fights against the gangsters and mobsters who ruled Gotham's underground . Just take a look at this trailer for the animated adaptation.

The point being, "Year One" has aged as well as any story could hope to, but it's simply time for new origins to explain how Batman fits into the modern DC mythos, which has significantly changed since the immediate aftermath of Crisis on Infinite Earths . DC has fully embraced its fantastic and strange roots that it tried so hard to shy away from when "Year One" released. The story it provides no longer completely reflects the DC around Batman. The question of continuity and history is constantly changing, but DC is making a substantial effort to tie its heroes to a timeline. It's no mistake that Superman's history as Superboy is being expanded on at the same time Wonder Woman received a rework of her own origin, both of which bring in modern sensibilities and smooth their ages.

Continuity has always been a messy thing in comic books, but with DC's decision to embrace its entire history and characters' overlapping lives following Dark Nights: Death Metal , it's become more essential than ever to specify what's canon and not. Batman has usually shirked this problem, with the New 52 citing that the vast majority of his famous stories took place on a hyper-condensed time scale, but with things more convoluted than ever and it having been decades since a lasting update to his first outing as Batman, there are more than enough reasons to warrant a new take on this classic tale.

A modern version of Batman's origin would definitely draw heavily on "Year One," but it would also likely steer away from the militaristic outlook that Bruce had.
