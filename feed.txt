&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google There have been rumors of an American Psycho remake coming, but according to director Luca Guadagnino, it's not happening. Scott Z. Burns was attached to write the movie as far back as 2024, and Guadagnino was in talks to direct it at the time. The original American Psycho was released in 2000, directed by Mary Harron, wth Christian Bale starring as Patrick Bateman . Based on the novel by Bret Easton Ellis, the movie follows a yuppie in the 1980s who might or might not be a serial killer. In the end, the book and the movie adapted from it were actually an indictment of the yuppie lifestyle and consumerism that was prevalent in the 1980s. That made it a perfect movie for Guadagnino.

However, Guadagnino was on Deadline 's Crew Call podcast with Anthony D’Alessandro, and he was asked about the American Psycho remake. The director said, "Scott Z. Burns wrote a fantastic script. Unfortunately for me, as we speak, it’s not happening." He didn't elaborate any further on what was happening with the project.

The novel was considered to be unfilmable before Mary Harron adapted it, and what resulted was a cult classic that offered fans a look at Christian Bale before he took on the role of Bruce Wayne, in a film that proved he could play the role. Making a remake seemed almost as unlikely as turning the Ellis book into a movie in the first place. Bale was perfect as Patrick Bateman, and Harron did a spectacular job of creating a movie that left viewers wondering if what they just watched was real or all in the mind of Patrick Bateman. It would be hard to pull that off again, but if anyone could, it is Guadagnino.

Luca Guadagnino has made his name with some bizarre and offbeat movies, especially as he started to gain more attention in North America. He already took on a risky remake with his 2018 release of Suspiria, and his later films, including Bones and All , Challengers , and Artificial , showed that he could take familiar genres and make something completely different out of them. He could easily take something like American Psycho and make it his own.

Lionsgate was the one that reached out to Guadagnino, and the studio hasn't made any comment about the movie no longer being in development. While the director didn't say much about it, his comments that the movie is not happening make it sound like he didn't just leave the role as director, but that the entire remake is off the table right now. Lionsgate has continued to hold the rights since the original movie and was considering a television series in 2013 alongside FX, something else that never came to pass.

After 26 years, the only thing that has come close to a return to the world of American Psycho was the 2002 movie The Rules of Attraction , which starred James Van Der Beek as Sean Bateman, Patrick's brother. That movie was also based on an Ellis novel. There was also a maligned sequel that was originally a script called The Girl Who Wouldn't Die , but it was rewritten to make it a sequel to capitalize on the original film's popularity.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google There are some movies that are made to be watched again and again …and again. That is particularly true of movies featuring time-travel and time-loops. In 1993, Bill Murray starred in one such movie that has the theme of repetition down to a fine art, where he played Phil Connors, a self-interested, cynical TV weatherman who discovers that sometimes you have to get things wrong a lot before you finally find the path you are meant to take. For him, all of this takes place on the same day as he finds himself caught in a time-loop that refuses to release him from living the same day repeatedly until he learns that the world does not revolve around him. Of course, the day he is stuck in is Groundhog Day , and the movie has found its way back into the Tubi streaming chart as a heartwarming, snow-covered film for the whole family to enjoy.

Under the direction of Murray's Ghostbusters costar Harold Ramis, Groundhog Day pairs Murray's narcissistic Phil with his TV producer coworker, Rita, played by Andie MacDowell. The movie is basically about Phil proving that he can love someone other than himself while being driven to madness in his attempts to break out of the loop he has found himself in. Unfortunately, his efforts don't instantly help him free himself from his curse, but naturally things all work out in the end. Murray was riding high at the time of Groundhog Day , and it became one of his highest-rated movies, still holding a 93% Rotten Tomatoes audience score. The popularity of the actor, along with the nostalgic feelings that a three-decade-ols movie brings has made sure that Groundhog Day is never far away from another watch, and there is no better time to revisit it than when it is available on free streaming.

Groundhog Day is a movie that plays its story perfectly with Ramis' direction and Murray's impeccable comic timing making the movie a timeless classic that doesn't miss a beat as it balances its humor and heart. Yet there could have been many other ways the movie could have turned out differently. Murray was not first choice for the role, as others such as Tom Hanks, Michael Keaton and Kevin Cline were all in the mix, with Keaton actually turning down the role because he didn't understand the story. Additionally, the studio wanted the reason why Phil ends up caught in his time-loop explaining in the movie. Ideas floated included a lover's curse and a science experiment gone wrong, but thanks to Ramis and producer Trevor Albert scheduling those scenes too late to be filmed, the movie had to go ahead without them - for the better.

Despite behind-the-scenes conflicts between Ramis and a mid-divorce Murray, and Murray's original claims that he hated the movie, years later he would admit that he felt it was one of his best performances and one of Ramis' best directorial efforts. That is something that almost everyone agrees with, as Murray's grumpy portrayal of Phil - partly enhanced by the personal issues he was facing at the time - is what elevates the film beyond just a run of the mill comedy. In 2006, Groundhog Day was preserved in the US National Film Registry, and its time-loop scenario is one that has often been repeated but very rarely bettered . Do yourself a favor and watch Groundhog Day while it is available to stream for free on Tubi among your festive holiday movies.

Less discussed is the even more elaborate, impressive playset that Mego released as a follow-up in 1976, finally giving kids a place to send the figures they put through the Enterprise’s transporter: the “Mission to Gamma VI.” Standing at over eighteen inches tall, the playset gave kids an exotic alien world for the Enterprise crew to explore. Gamma VI was a fully-rendered alien ecosystem, with the set’s accessories and action features representing three different species that inhabited the strange new world. It’s these bizarre action features that serve as the set’s claim to fame today: while Mego took some creative liberties with its Enterprise, they took far more with the “Mission to Gamma VI.” Gamma VI was virtually an original creation, its central stone-faced idol bearing a cursory resemblance to the one seen in season two episode “The Apple.”

There’s another critical difference between the Mego Enterprise Bridge and the “Mission to Gamma VI:” rarity. While the Enterprise was the top-selling toy of 1975, the “Mission to Gamma VI” scarcely reached retail shelves – owing in part to the box’s massive footprint – making it the single rarest and most sought-after release in Mego’s Star Trek line. Playsets are one of the challenges of Mego’s eight-inch action figures, demonstrated in sets like “Mission to Gamma VI” and the World’s Greatest Super-Heroes Wayne Foundation . To scale with Mego figures, these sets had to tower, making a significant investment of space for retailers and parents alike. Loose, complete samples of the set sell for up to $1k, with many of its action-features and accessories easily lost, damaged, or ruined by play. Boxed samples can sell for more than double that. The only sample currently on eBay sits at a buy-it-now asking price of $2.5k , owing to its incredibly rare sealed-in-box condition.

Beaming down to Gamma VI, Mego’s Enterprise crew would be met with a rocky, desolate planet surface represented through a cardboard baseplate. The only sign of life is a plastic plant, whose articulated leaves could be used to trap the unwary crewman who steps too close. The dominant feature of Gamma VI’s landscape, however, was the imposing stone face carved into a jagged cliffside. This is the aspect of the playset that bears the closest resemblance to the source material, seemingly modeled loosely off of the “Vaal” statue from the aforementioned episode “The Apple.” Given Mego’s penchant for loose adaptations of its Star Trek aliens , it feels reasonable to call Vaal the basis for “Mission to Gamma VI” – especially given that “Gamma Trianguli VI” is the name of the planet in “The Apple.”

The lizard-like face looms over a stepped platform, looking almost like a sacrificial altar. Looking closer, one would expect to find a door in the carved stone wall. From there, they can explore the secrets of Gamma VI’s temple, less detailed but just as fun as the exterior. The inside is functionally a cave, given two levels of play by a cardboard structure. More play features round out the exterior, including a movable jaw and glow-in-the-dark eyes on the “Vaal” stone face; as well as two other native alien species. A textured green glove fits through a hole in the playset, letting kids become what the box calls the “Cave Glove Creature,” pictured there snatching an unsuspecting Spock. It’s been said that Star Trek toys have almost never really been for kids: whether it was Galoob’s 1987 Star Trek: The Next Generation line, or Playmates’ epic 1990s Star Trek figures, the primary target audience was adult collectors. Catering to these adult collectors, these lines ensured their figures, characters, and accessories were coming straight from the source material. Playmates’ line especially, with its mail-away figures, ToyFare magazine exclusives, and limited-edition chases, were designed to engage grown-up Trekkies with the time and budget to obsess over the line. Mego’s line came at a completely different point in toy history, inspired directly by the overwhelming popularity of early-seventies Star Trek reruns with children. While Playmates or Galoob’s figures were collectors’ items as much as toys, Mego’s garish, pastel-colored Star Trek was firmly intended for kids.

Mego’s Star Trek line came at a time when the first and only consideration for toys was whether they would appeal to kids. Mego wasn’t beholden to screen accuracy, they were beholden to playaiblity. Designing their “Mission to Gamma VI,” the first and only question was if it would hold the interest of a child. As illustrated by the previous section, it absolutely woud: Mego designed its play features to gradually reveal themselves, playing out in a structure like an episode of Star Trek . A kid sitting down with the set and their Enterprise crew was exploring an alien planet with them, and imagining in real-time how their heroes would respond to the sudden dangers and developments posed by Gamma VI’s flora and fauna. Though it barely resembled the episode of Star Trek that it was based on, the “Mission to Gamma VI” embodied the spirit of the series perfectly, encouraging exploration, discovery, and a sense of adventure.
