&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google Few people would argue that the 1985 film The Breakfast Club isn't a classic. It’s considered one of the best films of the 1980s, and some would go as far as to say of all time. John Hughes wrote, produced, and directed it, making it part of his series of movies set in a suburb outside Chicago. The original film starred Emilio Estevez, Anthony Michael Hall, Judd Nelson, Molly Ringwald, and Ally Sheedy as the titular Breakfast Club, with Paul Gleason cast in the role of the authoritarian vice principal, Mr. Vernon. The film’s main five actors were referred to as the "Brat Pack" by the media and would go on to star in a variety of films throughout the 1980s.

Given how universally beloved of a film it is, it seems almost disrespectful to consider remaking it, even if John Hughes had once considered the idea of a sequel. Hughes died before the idea of the teens serving detention in university came to fruition, and no adaptation or continuation of the film has ever been made. Still, if somebody was brave enough to do a remake of The Breakfast Club, we know who should be cast for it.&nbsp;

To replace Molly Ringwald, you need star power and a little bit of sass. In The Breakfast Club, Claire Standish strikes the perfect balance between uptight and reasonably guarded, especially in response to John Bender’s overtures. The perfect Claire needs to be able to get called out by the characters for being a “princess” but should nevertheless charm the audience.

There’s nobody who’d be a better fit for that role than Stranger Things and Marvel Cinematic Universe star Sadie Sink. During her tenure as Max Mayfield, Sadie Sink never shied away from a challenge, playing to her full skill set. Max started out as a comedic character, albeit with insecurities and depth, before turning into more of a dramatic powerhouse through her struggles with trauma and Vecna in seasons four and five. Although Sink has only played Jean Grey in Spider-Man: Brand New Day so far, there’s no underselling her performance in the role, and despite the dubious morality of her actions in the film , the audience can’t help but root for her, at least a little bit.

Replacing Ally Sheedy as Allison Reynolds is Geena Meszaros. Meszaros is arguably less known than our other picks for this list, but her performance in Netflix’s The Body is a standout. Meszaros portrayed weird girl Lisbeth Anderson — a character that would likely freak out the so-called weird girl in The Breakfast Club — and embodied a character that viewers loved to hate. Meszaros stole the spotlight in every scene that she was in and possessed an off-kilter magnetism that would be perfect for a role in The Breakfast Club.&nbsp;

Making The Breakfast Club today also begs the question of how closely to follow the original script, especially with a character like Allison Reynolds. The original film has Claire Standish give her a makeover with makeup to soften Allison’s look, moving her appearance away from her “basket case”. The makeover leads Andrew to express interest in Allison, causing those two to pair up. The connotations around movie makeovers have definitely shifted with time, and it’d be curious to see how a remake of The Breakfast Club would approach the material today.

In 1985, Judd Nelson played John Bender, the so-called delinquent student. Bender spends the majority of The Breakfast Club defying Mr. Vernon and the rules he set for detention, along with antagonizing the other students. After being berated by Mr. Vernon — during which Bender stands frozen still — the audience finds out that Bender and his mother have been physically abused by his father, and Bender’s currently serving detention for pulling the fire alarm.&nbsp;

Our 2026 pick to replace Judd Nelson is Michael Cimino. Cimino gained popularity as an actor following his lead role in Love, Victor — a loose spin-off of the popular 2018 film Love, Simon — as Victor Salazar, and then a recurring role in the final season of Never Have I Ever as rebellious stoner love interest Ethan Morales. Cimino definitely has the chops to pull off antagonism while concealing a softer underbelly of vulnerability.

Our second Stranger Things star on this list — because, to be real, if we had to call a group of actors in the late 2010s to early 2020s a new version of the Brat Pack, it would be the actors from Stranger Things — Gaten Matazarro would be excellent as Brian Johnson. Matazarro definitely has the nerdy charm and comedic timing needed for Brian. Audiences have seen him play socially awkward for years in his role as Dustin in Stranger Things , and he’s battled academic pressure — and it changed him for the worse — in the 2022 film Honor Society. Really, this feels like a no-brainer.

This was a tough one, and all in all, we may be playing it safe here. In 1985, Andrew Clark was brought to life by Emilio Estevez, even though he was originally cast as John Bender. Estevez was only recast because John Hughes struggled to find the perfect actor for Andrew Clark, and thus the role switched up. As a character, Clark isn’t too much of a stretch from what we’ve seen from Kit Connor before.&nbsp;

Clark is a jock, influenced both by peer pressure from other jocks and by wanting to gain his father’s approval. Kit Connor rose to fame for his role in Heartstopper as Nick Nelson, the rugby lad with the personality of a golden retriever, and is set to play Cyclops in the MCU. Clark may be rougher around the edges than Nelson, but there’s no reason to believe that Connor wouldn’t do the role justice.

There’s no The Breakfast Club without the authoritarian vice-principal to rebel against — the main adult in the movie, and who, unlike the other characters, has few to no redeeming qualities. He talks down to students, forcefully flaunts authority, and is aggressive to any who stand against him. Whether you agree with his interpretation or not, original actor Paul Gleason saw hidden depths in the character — he felt that when the character read the ending essay, it led him to realize how he’d let the kids down and that he hadn’t understood them at all. Still, for most of the movie, Vernon is that authoritative jerk, even if he might come around at the end. It’s a role somebody has to play.

Our pick for a 2026 Mr. Vernon is Pedro Pascal. Pascal has exuded authority in all of his roles, even if it is usually a lovable one. The one that most immediately comes to mind is his current role as Reed Richards / Mister Fantastic in the MCU . As Reed Richards, Pascal shows he can play a borderline obsessed character, one focused on results and isn’t as skilled with the social aspects of life. There’s a reason Mister Fantastic is worried about being a father, and about the kid turning out like him — he knows that he is the way he hurts other people. A 2026 remake might soften Mr. Vernon through a performance by Pedro Pascal, or it might give the actor a chance to play a truly villainous role.

A lot of the answer can actually be found in horror movies that don't follow the genre's more traditional formulas, such as slashers and monster stories . Ever heard of psychological horror? These are the kind of films that leave us uneasy about something that's either difficult to explain or so deeply rooted in human experiences that it gets under our skin on a personal level.

If you enjoy psychological horror, this list features some of the best examples of how this genre can explain, in its own way, why we love feeling disturbed and terrified.

What The Babadook does best is make you feel afraid of something that goes beyond finding a monster inside your own home (which is already pretty terrifying on its own). In fact, this movie is more concerned with the feelings its protagonist tries to hide than with the creature itself. The story follows Amelia (Essie Davis), a widowed mother who's exhausted from trying to raise her son, Samuel (Noah Wiseman), a difficult child who still hasn't come to terms with his father's death. And when the two come across a children's book about a frightening creature called the Babadook , Samuel's behavior becomes a little more concerning, while Amelia also notices strange things happening around her.

The psychological suspense works because, as things get worse, it's difficult to separate the supernatural from Amelia's emotional struggles. She's not a mother who knows how to handle every challenge; she's tired, frustrated, and has a hard time showing affection toward her own son. So, when Babadook enters the picture, it brings all those feelings to the surface, including the ones many people would rather not admit they have. And this approach does a great job of explaining our fascination with fear because, deep down, the movie is dealing with very real problems. The Babadook is frightening in its own right , but its message also touches on something personal. The difference is that, because we're watching it unfold on screen, we don't have to deal with the consequences ourselves, which allows us to enjoy it.

Considered by many to be the best horror movie of the century , Get Out has more than enough reason to be on this list. Its premise is pretty effective since it takes a situation most people would already find uncomfortable and turns it into a nightmare. The story follows Chris (Daniel Kaluuya) , a Black photographer who travels with his white girlfriend, Rose (Allison Williams), to spend a few days at her family's estate. At first, her parents seem friendly, even if they make some awkward comments and show a few strange behaviors. But Chris soon realizes that something is wrong not only with them, but also with the Black employees working on the property. And the social commentary here is so powerful that the way Jordan Peele handles it is a masterclass in filmmaking.

Chris is surrounded by people who say they're not racist, but whose actions show that's not exactly the case. Horror becomes a way to explore the objectification of Black people and racism disguised as admiration. The Sunken Place sequence, for example, is amazing because it turns the feeling of not being heard or having no control over your own situation into a literal experience—it's surreal. And as you follow Chris trying to figure out what's going on, you also see that he's in a position where fighting back could make things worse. Overall, it's about the fear of being judged, manipulated, or put in danger by those who pretend to be something they're not . And it's even scarier when you remember that Get Out deals with a reality many people actually experience.

Maybe you've felt this way yourself, or you know someone who believes they need to be perfect to have any worth. Well, Black Swan explores this idea so well that you'll find yourself disturbed as you watch what someone is willing to do to reach the top. The movie introduces us to Nina Sayers (Natalie Portman), a ballerina who lands the lead role in a production of Swan Lake, where she has to portray both the White Swan and the Black Swan. The problem is that she's perfectly suited to the character's delicate and disciplined side, but struggles to show the sensuality and spontaneity required for the other role. Soon, she's dealing with pressure from the director, competition with a new dancer, and also a controlling mother.

So you watch all of this, following a protagonist who can't separate her personal identity from what she does professionally, making any sign that someone else might be better than her feel like the worst threat ever. Black Swan even adds elements of body horror to help you understand what it's like to be consumed by an obsession, and it does brilliantly. But more importantly, as suggested earlier, this is a story people can relate to because it deals with familiar situations like the pressure to deliver results, comparing yourself to others, and always feeling like you're not doing enough. And by taking these everyday struggles to extreme levels, you get curious to see just how far Nina will go.

Rosemary's Baby is one of the classics of psychological horror , and this reputation comes from exploring something that can naturally make us feel vulnerable: helplessness. Rosemary Woodhouse (Mia Farrow) is a woman who moves into an apartment in New York with her husband, Guy (John Cassavetes), and becomes pregnant shortly afterward. At first, the couple's life seems to be going just fine, but then their elderly neighbors start getting a little too close, and people around Rosemary begin acting strangely—something only she seems to notice. So, she realizes that something is going on, but her husband and even the doctors keep trying to convince her that everything is fine. Ultimately, you watch someone being pushed closer to the edge because nobody is willing to listen.

And that's without even getting into the pregnancy, because Rosemary isn't just worried about her own safety, but also about what might be happening to her child. The movie does a great job of exploring this feeling of helplessness, especially by showing how the choices being made about her own body aren't really hers to make. Rosemary's Baby is specifically a story about Satanism and conspiracy , but it's also about manipulation and control. And even after all these decades, this approach still works, as it makes you just as paranoid and suspicious of everyone as the main character. In other words, the movie succeeds at making the audience experience the effects of psychological horror, and you keep watching because you need to know how everything will turn out, even when you have a feeling it's not going to end well.

Stephen King is the king of horror stories, and many of his books have already been adapted over the years . And one of them, despite being quite different from the source material ( something that bothered the author ), is still a great example of how to mess with an audience's mind. The Shining centers on Jack Torrance (Jack Nicholson), a writer who accepts a job as the winter caretaker of the Overlook Hotel, bringing his wife, Wendy (Shelley Duvall), and his son, Danny (Danny Lloyd), with him. The idea is to take advantage of the isolation to work on his book, but the supernatural presence inside the hotel starts affecting the family, mostly Jack. His transformation doesn't take long, but it's still terrifying to watch (especially thanks to Nicholson's performance).

This is a character who becomes a threat to his own family, and even though the hotel and its occurrences are important, the possibility of a father losing control is what gives the story its weight. Besides, The Shining has many scenes that emphasize isolation, endless hallways, and some kind of oppressive atmosphere— some sublime direction from Stanley Kubrick . So, as you watch, you get the feeling that there's something seriously weird with the place (and in the worst way possible). Basically, the movie combines the fear of the unknown with the experience of living with someone familiar whom you can no longer recognize. And what's fascinating is that you can never really explain how or why everything happens the way it does.
