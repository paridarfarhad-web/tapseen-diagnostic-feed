The movie also changed how studios thought about comic book adaptations and the business of selling a blockbuster. We've had superhero movies dominating Hollywood for years now, but there's a good argument that none of it looks quite the same without Burton's Batman .

Getting Batman made wasn't exactly easy. Producer Michael Uslan had spent years trying to convince Hollywood that the character deserved a serious movie. The problem was that executives still associated Batman with the campy television series starring Adam West. That show was a huge hit in the 1960s, but it also left the character with a rather unusual reputation. Hollywood took notice. The success helped make serious superhero adaptations easier to sell, paving the way for movies like Blade , X-Men , and Sam Raimi's Spider-Man . Those films took different approaches, but they benefited from an industry that had started taking comic book characters seriously. Batman helped open that door.

The movie's marketing campaign was almost as important as the film itself. Warner Bros. didn't need to explain the plot on every billboard. The studio turned the character into a brand before audiences had even seen Burton's movie. T-shirts, toys, cereal boxes, and countless other products followed. You couldn't escape Batman in the summer of 1989. There was even a Prince soundtrack album that spent six weeks at the top of the Billboard 200. Not bad for a movie about a billionaire who dresses like a flying mammal.

The numbers were enormous, too. Batman earned around $411 million worldwide and became the highest-grossing movie of 1989. Its merchandise business was just as remarkable. The success showed Hollywood that a blockbuster could sell an entire entertainment experience rather than simply tickets to a movie. That's something studios have been chasing ever since. Of course, modern franchises have taken that idea much further with cinematic universes, endless merchandise, and marketing campaigns that seem to begin about three years before a movie arrives. But there's something different about what happened in 1989. Batman became a cultural obsession almost overnight. Today, audiences have trailers, social media, leaks, and months of online arguments before a big movie opens. Back then, a mysterious symbol on a black poster could get an entire country excited. I can't imagine Hollywood recreating that particular kind of movie madness. Even with all the superhero blockbusters we've had since, there's still only one Batmania.

Each girl has a secret—as is typical Shepard fare—that they need to figure out how far they’ll go to keep it that way as strange things start happening to their fellow students.

The mystery story is not only right in Shepard’s wheelhouse, but also keeps the main spirit of the Birds of Prey comics without the baggage of continuity. Birds of Prey , since its inception, has been about clashing values and putting differences aside.

The team originally started with a one-shot by Chuck Dixon, featuring a post- Killing Joke Barbara Gordon acting as the woman in the chair, with Dinah Lance as a field operative. Writer Gail Simone added Bertinelli’s Huntress to the lineup in the 2000s, while Harley Quinn wouldn't be added until much later.

Some of DC’s most iconic stories have been graphic novels — they’re longer than typical comics, and unlike the usual fare, they are self-contained and can be understood without knowing the larger context. In 1987, DC put out their first standalone graphic novel for Batman, titled Batman: Son of the Demon. Son of the Demon was immensely popular and was beloved by fans. The comic was quickly declared out of continuity, and by the ‘90s, editorial decided that the matter of Talia and Bruce’s son — whom Grant Morrison would revisit and name Damian Wayne — was off the table and not to be touched.

Batman: Son of the Demon , along with other successful Batman graphic novels, paired with the success of DC’s Black Label line — established in 2018 — makes out-of-continuity graphic novels a no-brainer. They sell, and they can push the character in a new direction while still allowing for the toys to be reset in the sandbox by the end of the story. New readers can get to know beloved characters beyond what they’re seen in TV and film adaptations.

Despite being standalone stories for the most part, DC Graphics for Young Adults has still been able to launch interconnected graphic novels. Starting as a carryover from DC Ink, Kami Garcia’s Teen Titans series includes not only the 2018 Teen Titans: Raven but five other graphic novels, with the last installment, Teen Titans: Together, published in 2026. The series proved sufficiently popular with readers to launch a sequel series about the Young Justice Team, with the first installment in that series set to feature Kid Flash and Blue Beetle.

One of the two main stars of DC’s Pride Summer event, Galaxy was first introduced in Galaxy: The Prettiest Star back in May 2022. By April 2023, the graphic novel had nearly sold out, and DC sent it for a second printing. Galaxy later joined Axelrod’s run on Hawkgirl and was a major character in the graphic novel Bad Dream: A Dreamer Story before later joining the Justice League.

DC ’s Young Adult comics are not afraid of the teen coming-of-age genre. It has embraced it for heroes and villains alike, spotlighting Pamela Isley in Poison Ivy: Thorns and the much underappreciated but totally awesome twin sister of Wonder Woman in Nubia: Real One. Mister Miracle: The Great Escape reimagines Scott Free’s romance with Big Barda, and Constantine: Distorted Illusions finds the skilled magician as the lead singer of a punk band.

Birds of Prey: The Origins is undoubtedly a coming-of-age story, but its gestures towards mystery could very well be indicative of where the imprint should go next. Literature aimed at teenagers often goes a coming-of-age route, but after a while, it can feel formulaic. It can be successful, but also get creatively stale.

Leaning into mystery, horror, or other genres outside the typical teen coming-of-age could be the next phase for DC Graphics for Young Adults. Birds of Prey , in the tradition of Teen Titans and the upcoming Young Justice books, has the makings of a team book, one that could spin off into a series. Gotham, even in the world of Birds of Prey: The Origins , is not without its secrets and intrigue.

In this list, you'll find some of these movies that have found their own ways of mixing magical and supernatural elements with horror and some disturbing ideas, which are very much at the heart of dark fantasy.

Remember Little Red Riding Hood ? When we think of this fairy tale, we obviously picture the child-friendly version in which a girl walks through the forest and comes across a wolf . However, there's a much darker version of the story (released long before the better-known Red Riding Hood from 2011) that explores what the tale might represent rather than just reimagining it. The Company of Wolves blends werewolves, nightmares, and themes of coming of age and sexuality as it follows Rosaleen (Sarah Patterson), a teenage girl who starts dreaming about a mysterious forest while listening to her grandmother's stories about men who hide their true nature and women who end up paying the price for trusting them.

And as these stories begin to overlap with events in the girl's own life, it's hard to tell what's a dream, what's imagination, and what's reality. So it's a more horror-driven take on the classic tale. The werewolves aren't monsters that need to be defeated , but rather a way to explore the fears and discoveries of a young girl who's starting to understand the adult world. The Company of Wolves definitely isn't for anyone looking for something straightforward or clear explanations for everything that happens, but it's certainly one of the best examples of what dark fantasy can mean.

If you're the kind of person who enjoys fantasy films that think outside the box, you may have heard of The City of Lost Children. Its only real flaw is how easily it's been forgotten, because this is a masterpiece that actually gets what fantasy filmmaking can be . Set in a strange universe, the story revolves around Krank (Daniel Emilfork), a scientist who kidnaps children to steal their dreams in the hopes of preventing himself from aging. When Denree (Joseph Lucien), the younger brother of One (Ron Perlman), a strong man, is taken, One teams up with a girl named Miette (Judith Vittet) to try to get him back. But hold on, because the movie goes beyond being just a rescue adventure.

Everything in this universe is eccentric, from its machines to the criminals who inhabit an industrial city that looks nothing like what you'd expect from a more conventional fantasy story ( it also plays around quite a bit with sci-fi elements for that very reason). However, the most important thing is the idea behind Krank's obsession: children's dreams represent something he has lost and is desperately trying to get back at any cost. It's an unusual but fascinating way to explore the fear of aging and the importance of childhood. And well, the narrative can be a little confusing at times, but The City of Lost Children is exactly the kind of movie that reminds you how much we're missing films with this level of creativity.

It's unlikely that you've heard of The Reflecting Skin , but it's a truly intriguing movie since it tells its story through the way a child interprets things he can't quite understand. Here, we meet Seth Dove (Jeremy Cooper), an eight-year-old boy living in a small rural community in the United States during the '50s. After hearing stories about vampires and witnessing violent events, he starts to suspect that his neighbor, Dolphin Blue (Lindsay Duncan), is a creature responsible for the disappearance of children. But things get a little more complicated when his older brother, Cameron (Viggo Mortensen), returns home and becomes involved with the woman, leading Seth to try to convince him that she's dangerous.

Well, it's basically a vampire movie , but it's worth it because, at the same time, it doesn't try to be what you'd expect: there's actually no clear supernatural threat for you to follow, and you have to deal with the same confusion as Seth throughout the story, never really knowing where his imagination ends. So vampirism is actually more of a part of Seth's interpretation of the world, and what really sticks with you after watching The Reflecting Skin is the uncomfortable take on childhood. And there's a bonus: you get to see one of Mortensen's first major roles before he became Aragorn in The Lord of the Rings .

November is an Estonian production with an 81% audience score on Rotten Tomatoes. And if there's one movie on this list that's dark fantasy through and through, it's this one. The story takes place in the 19th century, with Liina (Rea Lest) at the center of it all. She's a young woman in love with Hans (Jörgen Liik), who's interested in the daughter of a German baron. As she struggles with this unrequited love, she lives in a community where people will do anything to survive the winter, including making deals with the Devil. And since the movie is based on Estonian folklore, some of the creatures are extremely interesting, such as the kratts: servants made from discarded objects and animal remains that come to life after their creators strike a deal with evil forces.

And the people in this community are always trying to find ways to take advantage of these beings and even trick the Devil himself. So, it's dark fantasy, but also with romance, horror, and humor (the really absurd kind). Still, what's so good about November is that even though it doesn't follow a more common structure and its black-and-white cinematography fits everything perfectly, the combination of strange creatures and the unexpected makes the experience unique. Those who've seen it have nothing but praise. Everything about it is crazy and feels like something straight out of a fairy tale, but one meant for adults in the most brilliant way.
