&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google It’s easy to get nostalgic about 2016, especially when you realize that was 10 whole years ago. Beyond being the final year of “millennial optimism,” it was also a fantastic year for film lovers. Masterpieces like Barry Jenkins’s Moonlight , Denis Villenueve’s Arrival , and Damien Chazelle’s La La Land graced our screens, while even disappointments like Jared Leto’s Joker in Suicide Squad became hilarious fodder for internet memes—back when the internet was still fun.

However, if you look past the best and worst movies of 2016, you’ll find a plethora of films that were critically panned at the time but still have a lot of merit. One is a classic case of so bad it's good. A few are misunderstood masterpieces from filmmakers unfairly considered past their prime. And another is a brilliant early work from a filmmaking duo who hadn’t quite reached their peak yet. Regardless of the reason, this handful of 2016 films, while hated by critics when they came out, are absolutely worth watching today.

A psychological horror thriller from Drive director Nicolas Winding Refn , The Neon Demon holds a “Rotten” score on Rotten Tomatoes and was slammed for being all style and no substance. Critics at the time weren’t necessarily wrong about the film prioritizing its glossy visuals, but many failed to appreciate that, here, the style is the substance. On rewatch, it becomes clear that the arguably shallow characters and stilted, artificial dialogue were deliberate choices meant to mirror the vapid culture of the Los Angeles modeling scene the film is directly critiquing.&nbsp;

Elle Fanning's fantastic lead performance is reminiscent of Naomi Watts in Mulholland Drive and shows the protagonist’s transformation from a bright-eyed, bushy-tailed dreamer into a narcissist. Natasha Braier's cinematography makes every frame look like a high-fashion editorial, while Cliff Martinez’s electronic score drives the tension. It may read as surface-level satire on first watch, but the film has retained some devoted fans for good reason.

The most objectively hated film on this list is Kevin Smith’s 2016 horror-comedy Yoga Hosers , which currently holds 26% on Rotten Tomatoes, although legend says it had 0% when it first came out. The main issues plaguing it are a messy screenplay, a home-movie structure, and low-effort practical effects. Yet despite the initial negativity, the film has developed a niche following, driven mostly by the chemistry between stars Lily-Rose Depp and Harley Quinn Smith, not to mention the B-movie camp.&nbsp;

The plot follows two yoga-loving 15-year-olds working at a convenience store, but when an ancient evil awakens beneath the shop and threatens their plans to attend a party, they use yoga to battle an army of mini Nazi bratwursts known as the Bratzis. The absurdity is exactly why this film is worth a watch. It is by no means a “good” movie, but it’s so bad and so bizarre that it ends up being entertaining, fitting squarely into the “so bad it’s good” category. Alongside the two teen stars, Johnny Depp reprises his role as an eccentric French Canadian manhunter. The rest of the cast is equally stacked, featuring Justin Long as a yoga guru and Austin Butler as the party host, plus Natasha Lyonne, Haley Joel Osment, Vanessa Paradis, and a famous cameo from Stan Lee . At only 88 minutes long, the film is worth watching today if you love camp, B-horror, or teen-girl action flicks.

Written and directed by legendary filmmaker Terrence Malick, shot by three-time Academy Award winner Emmanuel Lubezki, and starring Hollywood A-lister Christian Bale, Knight of Cups was teed up to be a masterpiece. However, the film, about a disillusioned Hollywood screenwriter seeing a spiritual breakthrough, currently holds a 47% critics’ rating on Rotten Tomatoes. Reviewers cited a lack of any narrative structure and so-called self-indulgence from Malick, who inserts his own perspective into the story with little subtlety, as reasons for the hate.

Yet, while it might not live up to the profundity of Malick’s masterpieces like The Tree of Life , it’s still worth a watch for anyone who enjoys his sensibilities as a filmmaker. The stunning wide-angle cinematography is captivating enough to lead you through the story’s allegory of modern alienation, which uses tarot archetypes. Alongside Bale, the cast features Cate Blanchett as his ex-wife, Natalie Portman as his married lover, and supporting roles from Brian Dennehy, Antonio Banderas, and Imogen Poots, with voice narration by Ben Kingsley.&nbsp; For a film initially dismissed as hollow, there’s actually much to glean from its spiritual pilgrimage.&nbsp;

Swiss Army Man was the directorial debut of Daniel Kwan and Daniel Scheinert, also known as the Daniels, who would later go on to helm the Academy Award-winning Everything Everywhere All at Once . But before they were Oscar darlings, their early A24 film Swiss Army Man polarized critics and audiences alike, triggering notorious walkouts at its festival premiere and barely earning a “Fresh” score on Rotten Tomatoes. Today, it seems to have transcended the label of cult favorite, becoming known in cinephile circles as something of an unsung masterpiece.

The premise follows Hank (Paul Dano), stranded on a remote island and on the verge of suicide, when he spots a corpse washed up on the shore. He names the body Manny (Daniel Radcliffe) and discovers it possesses strange, Swiss Army knife-like abilities. At the time, it was famously known as “the farting corpse movie,” with detractors criticizing the juvenile humor, pretentious “twee” vibe, and moral greyness involving Mary Elizabeth Winstead’s character. Yet, for audience members willing to embrace the foolishness, Swiss Army Man is a rewarding watch. Rather than using shock value for cynicism, the Daniels use bodily functions as a metaphor for shame, stripping Manny of social conditioning as the film probes themes like depression, anxiety, and social masking. Without Swiss Army Man , there would be no Everything Everywhere All at Once . To understand the creative DNA that led the Daniels to their masterpiece, you must see Swiss Army Man .

Parodied, homaged, and referenced countless times through the decades, Batman #1 is still one of the most recognizable images of the Caped Crusader ever drawn; and one of the absolute most important comics DC ever published — even putting aside the iconic pair of characters who debuted within.

Although rebooted three times and no longer retaining its original numbering, the title is still running — and this month's issue even featured the Joker, introduced eighty-six years and nine hundred-odd issues ago. There are a literal handful of books able to claim such a lineage, especially taking into account that Batman has published continuously for those eighty-six years, a testament to the character's enduring popularity. At the time, he was one of just two DC heroes given multiple monthly titles, the other being Superman . Virtually every major change and trend in the comic book medium can be traced through those eighty-six years of Batman stories, which would introduce readers to iconic characters like Ra's al Ghul and Poison Ivy, as well as stories better left in the past, like the time Batman and Robin tried to solve the "Paul is Dead" mystery .

Batman #1 opens with "The Legend of the Batman - Who He is, and How He Came to Be," a reprint from Detective Comics #33. In two pages, it provides the backstory that has come to be completely engrained in popular culture, establishing the murder of Thomas and Martha Wayne in a dark Gotham alley while returning from the movies with their son Bruce. From this origin reprint, the issue launches in its first new story, entitled simply "The Joker." Here we meet the Ace of Knaves as a violent, cunning maniac; an arrogant killer who announces his crimes before he commits them, confident he will not be stopped. Each of his victims is left with a grotesque smile on their faces, the result of a deadly dose of "Joker venom." After besting the Dynamic Duo several times, the Joker makes the fatal mistake of mentioning his next target in earshot of a captive Robin, enabling the escaped Robin and Batman to foil the robbery in-progress. The Joker is sent to prison, vowing revenge on his newfound nemesis — revenge that would come later that same issue.

"The Joker's Revenge" once again sees him outwit Batman continuously, and cements what makes him the Dark Knight's greatest foe: his madness. Unstable and erratic, the Joker's wanton violence and unpredictable derangement lend him an edge on Batman's regimented, logical thinking. In this issue, the Joker kills without pattern, leaves behind the target of a successful robbery, and fakes his own death. After the fake-out, Gotham Police (and readers) learn that the Joker is still alive, indicating that writer Bill Finger knew what he had with the character, and was planning his next move already. It would come in "The Case of the Laughing Death" in Detective Comics #45, followed by a team-up with Catwoman in Batman #2. That issue would also mark the first time the name "Catwoman" was used, as for her first appearance, Selina Kyle's alter ego is "the Cat."

Like the Joker, Catwoman emerges pretty fully formed, name non-withstanding. The story is actually a spotlight for Robin, who goes undercover on a Gotham socialite's yacht after correctly deducing her emerald necklace (worth $500,000 — and that's in 1940) will prove too tempting a target for the city's criminal element. Robin's hunch proves correct, and the thief in question is "the Cat," herself a member of Gotham's upper crust and a passenger aboard the yacht. Though the theft is foiled and she is discovered as the Cat by Batman at the story's end, Selina Kyle is able to escape from justice. How? Batman is taken in by her beauty, and momentarily stunned by her offer to switch sides and join her as the "King of Crime." She dives overboard from the yacht, and Batman lets her go, marking the beginning of one of the most iconic, tumultuous romances in comic books.

The issue is rounded out by a Hugo Strange story, rooting it firmly in its era. Prior to the debut of the Joker, Hugo Strange was the defacto nemesis of Batman as his first recurring opponent. As the decade continued, Hugo Strange would be overshadowed by other members of Batman's growing rogues gallery, returning to prominence decades later following a revival in Steve Englehart's justly-renowned Detective Comics run. Pitting Batman against Hugo Strange's mutated "Monster Men," this story provides what may actually be the issue's most-discussed moment: Batman kills. Although this is difficult to fathom today, Batman was in serious danger of cancellation in the early sixties. Neutered by the Comics Code Authority, DC's writers and artists were forced to avoid Batman's rogues' gallery, who were considered inappropriate to younger readers. The Joker and Catwoman were singled out in particular, with the Joker's gleeful violence and Catwoman's sexually-charged dynamic with Batman seen as bad influences. While Joker was toned down to emphasize his pranks and downplay his homicidal tendencies, Catwoman disappeared altogether. It would take the 1966 Batman television series to revive the character, who quickly became one of its most popular recurring villains. Played by Julie Newmar, Catwoman was one of four Batman villains to appear in the feature adaptation of the series, 1966's Batman: The Movie . After a more than decade-long absence, Catwoman returned to comics in Superman's Girl Friend, Lois Lane #70. The television series' popularity also forced editor Julius Schwartz to continue using the Joker in Batman stories despite his personal distaste for the character.

The boost in Batman readership proved temporary, and DC's response to the title's next dip in sales proved even more consequential: a return to Batman's darker roots, ushered in by creators like Denny O'Neil, Neal Adams, and Jim Aparo. While no longer an influence over the tone of the comics, the Batman television series left its permanent mark on who were considered central to the character's mythology and rogues' gallery. The Joker would be one of the first to benefit from the new direction, returning to his terrifying homicidal maniac roots in Batman #251, "The Joker's Five-Way Revenge." Catwoman evolved throughout the 1970s and 80s, with her alternate universe Earth-Two counterpart leaving behind her life of crime and marrying Bruce Wayne — suggesting that maybe the primary, Earth-One Selena Kyle could do the same. Len Wein's Batman work played with Catwoman's reform, turning her into an anti-hero, a characterization that has stuck in the decades since.

Tim Burton's Batman and Batman Returns further cemented the Joker and Catwoman's place in popular culture, as have the litany of further live-action and animated adaptations that have come in the intervening decades. Even in Batman #1, it's apparent why creators find themselves returning to the characters again and again: both provide excellent foils to Batman, challenging him in ways that speak to his beliefs, ethics, and what makes him a super-hero. Put into the context of a retelling of his origin, and the last time he ever killed without hesitation or regret, the Joker and Catwoman's relationships with Batman become even more complex — and Batman #1 even more significant. And with just three hundred and fifty-six copies on the CGC's population report, the record-setting prices that the issue can sell for are no surprise. With its rarity and significance taken into account, the copy on auction at Heritage starts to look like a bargain, even for $100k.

Today, Aquaman is recognized as the half-human King of Atlantis. However, when Aquaman first debuted during the Golden Age, he was anything but royalty. One of Alan Moore’s greatest achievements as a writer was revealing that everything we thought we knew about Swamp Thing was a lie. Swamp Thing’s rebooted origin has remained mostly unchanged because of how it challenges concepts of identity and humanity.

No two characters in comic book history have more convoluted, reworked origin stories than Hawkman and Hawkgirl. Things have gotten so complicated now that there are now two Hawkgirls, Kendra Saunders and Shayera Thal, who are reincarnations of the same person. Hawkman and Hawkgirl are the worst-case scenario of excessive retcons in DC Comics.

Based on George R. R. Martin's A Song of Ice and Fire novels, HBO's Game of Thrones proved to be a massive global phenomenon, quickly becoming known as one of the best fantasy TV shows of all time . While fantasy stories have long been among the most enduring and popular in fiction, Game of Thrones marked a shift in small-screen entertainment. As well as ushering in a new era of prestige television, Game of Thrones also definitively proved that, if appropriate care was taken in the adaptation process, bringing fantasy stories to life in live-action could provide compelling entertainment that would grip audiences around the world.

There can be no massive success in the entertainment industry without a wave of imitations following in its wake. The same was certainly true for the adaptations of GRRM's iconic novels, as numerous shows were made to target Game of Thrones fans following the show's unprecedented popularity. However, of all the shows that tried to become the next Game of Thrones , none really achieved it, earning mixed success that failed to live up to the series that kicked off the modern fantasy TV boom. While the following shows each have their own qualities that make them worth watching, none had the compelling stories or longevity that made Game of Thrones such a major success.

Even as Game of Thrones was approaching its peak, other networks were attempting to capture its success with their own similar projects. The mid-2010s saw a wave of medieval TV shows featuring a mix of political intrigue and brutal violence, and, considering the sheer quantity of these series that were introduced in that period, it's no surprise that some were pretty quickly forgotten. Created by Kurt Sutter of Sons of Anarchy fame, The Bastard Executioner was just one such show, airing for just one season before its cancellation.

In terms of tone, it's clear that The Bastard Executioner was angling to become the next Game of Thrones . Trying to establish an ensemble cast of morally complex characters alongside a whole medieval mythology was always going to be a huge undertaking, and it's something that The Bastard Executioner really struggled with. It proved to be a passable series in many ways, but it was never any sort of match for Game of Thrones ' sprawling universe of intricate familial hierarchies, resulting in Kurt Sutter's show failing miserably at challenging the HBO behemoth.

When it comes to fantasy TV shows cancelled too soon , it's genuinely staggering just how many can be attributed to Netflix refusing to give shows the time they need to grow. This was exactly what happened with Cursed , a series based on Frank Miller and Tom Wheeler's novel of the same name, which itself was written as a reimagining of Arthurian legend. Taking well-known fantasy source material and putting its own unique spin on the tale could have proved a route to success, and while Cursed got off to a bit of a slow start, the potential was plain to see for many fantasy fans.

While Cursed was tipped by many as Netflix's attempt at creating its own successor to Game of Thrones ' fantasy crown, the streaming service opting to axe it after just a single season was ultimately the reason it failed. It might not have been the same instant success as Game of Thrones , but Netflix choosing to swiftly cut its losses wasted a huge amount of potential. Cursed had political intrigue, eye-catching fantasy action, and a large-scale fantasy setting that could possibly have grown into a major contender in the fantasy genre, but by cancelling it prematurely, Netflix ironically secured Cursed 's status as a failed fantasy experiment.

Another fantasy TV show cancelled by Netflix, Shadow and Bone aired for two seasons before its untimely end was announced. Based on Leigh Bardugo's Grishaverse novels, the series balanced faithfully adapting its source material with some original additions, a move that proved popular with fans and secured the series a considerable following.

Despite a growing fan base, Netflix's decision to cancel Shadow and Bone prevented it from securing the sort of legacy it probably deserved. In terms of pure quality and appeal, it might not have quite matched up to Game of Thrones , but it was an impressive and original fantasy show that really did deserve much better. It was a show whose unique elements and quality could easily have grown into a major fantasy success, but Netflix's hasty decision to end its run marked another instance of bizarre self-sabotage in the streaming giant's apparent intention to recreate Game of Thrones ' popularity.
