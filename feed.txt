The first photobooths were located just north of Times Square and caused a sensation in 1926, with crowds lining up for a chance in the booth until 4 a.m. The next year Josepho sold the patent for the booths for $1 million to businessman Henry Morgenthau, who launched booths across the nation.

From there, photo booths popped up at army bases, allowing soldiers to send home a photo, and expanded into Europe, where they were favored among members of the surrealist movement. The automated, private nature of the booths also gave Black Americans a chance for self-expression in the Jim Crow South, and gave queer couples an opportunity to take home private keepsakes, with some photobooths stationed in men's bathrooms. Andy Warhol used photobooth strips as a basis for his pop art and silk screen painting, helping to legitimize it as a form of art.

While photobooths gradually fell into disuse, due to the rise of digital cameras and cell phones with cameras, a dedicated group of enthusiasts across the globe have worked to bring old booths back to life &#x2014; the refurbishment is pricey, but the business of booths is quickly profitable &#x2014; and to revive enthusiasm for the practice.
