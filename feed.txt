But what about his best horror performance? A case could be made for War of the Worlds , although its action-adventure elements still dominate. This leaves only Cruise’s 1994 role in Neil Jordan's adaptation of Anne Rice's novel Interview with the Vampire as his most notable horror performance. Cruise stars as the charismatic vampire Lestat alongside a young Kirsten Dunst as Claudia and Brad Pitt as Louis de Pointe du Lac, who recounts his centuries-long life as a vampire to Christian Slater’s journalist. Yet despite the stacked cast, Cruise remains the film's standout, particularly because he takes his leading-man reputation and turns it on its head.

Before Interview with the Vampire , Cruise had already established his reputation through performances in Top Gun , Rain Man , Born on the Fourth of July , A Few Good Men , and The Firm . In the early 90s, he was increasingly moving towards serious dramatic roles but still favored sympathetic leading characters. Not only was Lestat de Lioncourt a substantial departure from the good-guy image, but it didn’t fit squarely into either action or prestige drama. At the time of his casting, the disconnect was so great that author Anne Rice publicly objected to Cruise playing Lestat. She considered him an incorrect choice for the aristocratic, flamboyant vampire she’s created. She roasted the decision in the press, calling it &#8220;the worst crime in the name of casting since The Bonfire of the Vanities&#8221; and &#8220;so bizarre, it's almost impossible to imagine how it's going to work.&#8221;

Yet Cruise shocked the world by not only giving us a fantastic Lestat, but also delivering the best performance in the film. Even Rice came around after seeing the movie, placing a self-funded two-page ad in The New York Times apologizing for her earlier public criticism of Cruise's casting , stating, “I never dreamed it would turn out this way.” As Rice and many critics of the time noted, Cruise’s Lestat works so well not despite his public persona, but precisely because of it, using his leading-man charm to make Lestat more convincing to both the characters and the audience. Lestat’s handsome confidence and humor are offset beautifully when he is revealed as a remorseless killer who treats human beings as disposable, using seduction as his weapon of choice.

The complexities Cruise communicates through Lestat are incredible. When Lestat offers Louis the opportunity to become a vampire, he promises him the choice he himself was denied, and the restraint with which Cruise delivers the line lets the character's loneliness peek through the veneer. Simultaneously, the offer is manipulative: Lestat wants a companion and sees Louis as someone who can fill that role. Is the vulnerability real or part of the manipulation? Therein lies the brilliance of Cruise’s performance, which only becomes more openly cruel as Louis struggles with his new existence. Perhaps most unsettling is when Lestat turns Dunst's Claudia (another standout performance) into a vampire and presents her as their new daughter, intending the unconventional family arrangement to solve their problems. Cruise's enthusiasm and excitement at having condemned a child to an eternity is genuinely horrifying.

Though critics were initially mixed upon the film’s release, the movie received two technical Oscar nominations: Best Art Direction and Best Original Score. Interview with the Vampire has since accrued a dedicated cult following among genre movie lovers. For all its camp and melodrama, the movie remains genuinely emotionally devastating, thanks in part to the nuance Cruise brings to the screen. A satisfying mix of horror, romance, and drama with doomed blood-suckers at its center, Interview With the Vampire deserves recognition among the genre's finest films.

While Cruise's performance is a major reason Interview with the Vampire holds up, the film's timelessness also comes from its execution. Neil Jordan whips up an elaborate gothic world stretching from New Orleans to Paris with help from production designer Dante Ferretti, cinematographer Philippe Rousselot, and composer Elliot Goldenthal. As for the ensemble, Pitt gives Louis a melancholy that plays beautifully off Lestat's flamboyance, while 11-year-old Dunst delivers an extraordinary early-career performance as Claudia, even earning her a Golden Globe nomination (she apparently lost the win by a single vote). Antonio Banderas, Stephen Rea, and Christian Slater round out the stellar cast.

In 2022, the AMC TV adaptation of Interview with the Vampire also received critical acclaim. Starring Sam Reid, Jacob Anderson, and Bailey Bass, the series supposedly took substantial liberties with Rice's original story . Rice and her son Christopher were involved in the series' development. However, she died before its premiere, making it impossible to know how she would have responded to the changes or finished adaptation—or whether another smear campaign or two-page apology ad would have been in order. Both versions have their merits, but with the Rice seal of approval, Neil Jordan's 1994 film remains the definitive adaptation of Interview with the Vampire in which Tom Cruise gives one of the most distinctive performances of his career and, to date, his finest work in horror.

Mattel's unique release strategy for M.U.S.C.L.E . made it easier for kids and challenging for completionist adults to collect. The figurines were primarily sold in completely randomized packages of four or ten, as well as larger (loosely) themed twenty-eight packs. The four-packs retailed for a lower cost than the average action figure in 1985 or '86, making it easier for a kid to amass an impressive collection of M.U.S.C.L.E . figures on a budget of spare change and their weekly allowance. Owing to their low price point, the figures are plentiful, seeming to make their way into virtually every toy collection of the era, although the first law of toy collecting dictates that as a collector edges closer to having them all, they will inevitably never come across those last five or ten missing characters. That's why auctions like this upcoming lot at Heritage advertise and emphasize having key characters like &#8220;the Claw,&#8221; a giant disembodied hand and a cult favorite of M.U.S.C.L.E . collectors, included. Given the aforementioned random release strategy, coming across the exact fan favorite character mint-on-card is exceptionally rare.

Kinnikuman was created in 1979 by Yoshinori Nakai and Takashi Shimada, and was designed by the pair as a loose parody of Ultraman . The plot followed the masked wrestler Kinnikuman (translated roughly as &#8220;Muscleman&#8221;), deposed prince of the planet Kinniku, as he competed against the wrestlers of the wicked Chojin in a quest to prove himself worthy of Kinniku's throne. Terri-Bull / Buffaloman was one of many villains Kinnikuman faced in the ring, and would become an ally to Kinnikuman after being defeated in combat. Such was also the case for Ramenman , Ashuraman , Robin Mask , and numerous other characters who appeared in the Kinkeshi (and subsequently M.U.S.C.L.E .) toylines. The Japanese name for the line, &#8220;kinkeshi,&#8221; is a portmanteau of Kinnikuman and &#8220;keshi,&#8221; a type of soft rubber, eraser-like figurine popular in Japan. Though a popular toy in Japan, keshi figures lack the same cache in the American market, meaning that when M.U.S.C.L.E . debuted, there was not another toyline like it. Its scale, designs, and open-ended world were unlike virtually anything else on the market.

It's not unusual for an American toy company to take an existing product from Japan and rebrand it for Western audiences. Most famously, this was the origin story of the Transformers , but this practice also spawned lines like Mego's Micronauts, Mattel's Shogun Warriors , and even Tonka's Go-Bots . Where M.U.S.C.L.E . varies is that it eschewed the tie-in comic book or animated series that was used by these other properties to help familiarize kids with the line and its characters. M.U.S.C.L.E , for better or for worse, was a blank slate. Mattel's marketing materials give a little background, specifying that &#8220;Muscleman and his heroic Tag Team, the Thug Busters, are ready for all challengers. Two additional twenty-eight packs, the &#8220;Mighty Maulers&#8221; and the &#8220;Cosmic Showdown&#8221; were also available.

These twenty-eight packs were less popular than the modestly-priced four-packs, and are difficult to locate on the secondary market. More common are the ten-packs, which were sold in plastic garbage cans; a fair number of kids held onto these and used them to store their M.U.S.C.L.E . collections. Additional expressions for the line enhanced the wrestling theme, with a &#8220;Hard Knockin' Wrestling Ring&#8221; playset, a wearable &#8220;Battlin' Belt&#8221; that could house a small number of M.U.S.C.L.E . figures, and the &#8220;Mega Match&#8221; game, which created turn-based gameplay for wrestling matches between the figurines. The &#8220;Hard Knockin' Wrestling Ring&#8221; had play mechanics similar to Rock &#8216;Em, Sock &#8216;Em Robots : the players clipped one figure each into a plastic stand controlled by a joystick, enabling them to &#8220;fight&#8221; until one figure was knocked loose from the plastic clip. The playset and game are relatively rare, and command triple-digits price-points mint in box.

A promotional mail-away from Mattel threw down the gauntlet for collectors: a poster featured all two hundred and thirty-three M.U.S.C.L.E . figures, emblazoned with the rhetorical question of &#8220;Can you collect them all?&#8221; As mentioned in the introduction, the randomized nature of M.U.S.C.L.E &#8216;s distribution meant that the answer to this was &#8220;no&#8221; for all but the most diligent, obsessive fan &#8212; and that doesn't become much easier for them, even as an adult. The lack of agreed upon names adds another degree of difficulty, as seeking out individual characters on auction sites is hampered by trying to figure out which name the seller might have the character listed under. Some American M.U.S.C.L.E . collectors have taken to using the Japanese names, while other stick with the informal nicknames adopted by the American collecting community. As an example, &#8220;the Claw&#8221; advertised by Heritage's M.U.S.C.L.E . auction listing is a collector nickname for a character known in Kinnikuman as &#8220; Sunigator Shotai .&#8221; Some eBay listings for the figure use both names, some one or the other, some use neither.

Beyond the incredibly wide character selection, Mattel introduced another challenge to assembling a complete set of M.U.S.C.L.E . figures: different colored rubber. Later waves of M.U.S.C.L.E . came in eye-catching blues, reds, oranges, and greens. Including color variants, there are more than a thousand M.U.S.C.L.E . figures, though most collectors content themselves with trying to assemble a complete set of characters. M.U.S.C.L.E . is an easy line to collect, and a tough line to complete; each of these characteristics makes it attractive to different kinds of collectors. For some, M.U.S.C.L.E . is a fun line to informally gather a handful of from conventions, flea markets, and collectible stores.
