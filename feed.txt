It should come as no surprise that the history of comics based on Saturday morning cartoons would go back to the beginning of the programming concept itself. One of the first Saturday morning cartoons — referred to generally as the earliest original color cartoon in that category — The Ruff and Reddy Show debuted in 1957. Created by William Hanna and Joseph Barbera, the series followed a cat named Ruff and a brave but not super smart dog named Reddy who got into adventures together. The popular series paved the way for more animated series as well as the future of Hanna-Barbera, but it also led to the first Saturday morning cartoon comic book. The series got its first comic book adaptation from Dell Four-Color Comics in 1958, specifically issue #937.

Other cartoons soon followed onto the pages of comics and in the 1960s, Hanna-Barbera characters and series in particular were among those that regularly made the leap. All throughout the 1960s and 1970s, Gold Key Comics published a number of dedicated tie-in comics, especially for Hanna-Barbera shows like Space Ghost , The Flintstones , The Jetsons , Yogi Bear , and more. Many of these books functioned more as tie-ins rather than direct adaptations of the series themselves, meaning you got new stories that weren’t on the screen and, in some cases, expanded the stories of supporting characters as well.

What’s really exciting about the comics connected to Saturday morning cartoons is that even superheroes got in on the action. In 1973, Hanna-Barbera produced an animated series featuring DC Comics’ Justice League, Super Friends . The series ran for nine total seasons with the title of the series changing each seasons along with the team’s heroic lineup. But what made things even more interesting is that while the cartoon was itself based off or characters who existed in comics, the series in turn got their own comics, too. One of the most notable instances of the cartoon impacting the comics might just be the Wonder Twins. The brother-sister superhero duo was created by Norman Maurer, Bill Hanna, and Joe Barbera for The All-New Super Friends Hour in 1977 and ended up appearing in issue #7 of the Super Friends tie-in comic series in 1977 as well with the comics giving a much more detailed backstory for Zan and Jayna.

The Wonder Twins would end up becoming part of the main DC Comics continuity. The 1996 series Extreme Justice introduced the duo into DC’s post- Crisis on Infinite Earths continuity and they’ve appeared a handful of times in DC comics in the years since. But it isn’t just DC heroes who have had a lasting legacy that crosses comics and Saturday morning cartoons. One of the coolest examples of the cartoon-comics connection might just be Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles. The beloved heroes in a half shell started out in comics in 1984, got a popular action figure line, then became a beloved animated series in 1987, and continues to this day with a popular comic book series with IDW.

What’s really fun is that, while the age of Saturday morning cartoons may be over, many of the comics adventures remain. A lot of the comic book adaptations of Saturday morning comics are available in various collected editions — this includes collections of the Super Friends comics as well as many of the Hanna-Barbera comics. It’s a fun way to relieve to nostalgia of those weekend mornings when life was simpler and who knows; you might even come across a comics you never realized existed.

Lost in Space opened in theaters on April 3, 1998, and despite opening in the top spot at the box office — and dethroning Titanic which had held the spot for 15 weeks at that point — the film quickly turned into a box office disappointment, making just over $136 million globally against an $80 million budget. The film was also savaged by critics with Roger Ebert notable calling the film “dim-witted” but despite not winning the masses or critics over, the film is much sharper than you might recall, a great popcorn movie, and it has an incredible cast, including Gary Oldman who delivered one of the most unhinged performances of his career — and it’s truly great.

1998’s Lost in Space is an adaptation of the iconic 1960s television series of the same name, a series which was itself a loose adaptation of The Swiss Family Robinson just in space. It follows the Robinson family that embark on a journey to the stars, headed to a nearby star system to ready a new world for mass-emigration from an Earth that is rapidly becoming uninhabitable. The film stars Gary Oldman, William Hurt, Matt LeBlac, Mimi Rogers, Heather Graham, Lacey Chabert, and Jared Harris.

While the 1960s series was campy and fun, the movie takes the concept and goes much more serious and a bit darker with it. The film preserves the idea of sabotage sending the Robinson’s off course, but there is something more sinister about things in the movie, and something a bit creepier about Doctor Zachary Smith (Oldman), the family physician who turns out to be a spy. The darkness is also present in the film’s overall visuals and aesthetic. That is something that was really cool about the 1990s in sci-fi, especially the late 1990s. It was visually darker and an interesting mix of both slick and gritty, and it was something that Lost in Space actually did very well. The film also had genuinely great practical effects and highly detailed sets. Sure, CGI had a place and Industrial Light & Magic was behind some of the best effects of the movie, but there is nothing quite like the sets. When you look at the Jupiter II spaceship the Robinsons are on, that is something they built with a 70-foot model and the interior? Fully created on set. Even before the story starts to get weird (oh, and it gets weird), the film is a visual delight.

But while Lost in Space looks incredibly well done (and holds up surprisingly well in that regard) the thing that I think most people failed to appreciate at the time — and many people still do in retrospect — is the performances. All of the actors do a solid job in the film, giving us characters who bounce off one another in ways that seems to fit their personalities. Hurt, who plays Professor John Robinson, for instance is very stiff and serious much the way you’d expect the Professor to be, but it balances out his wife Maureen (Rogers). In his foreword, Eggers reflects on the history of werewolf movies and the difficulty of bringing the familiar monster to the screen. "Werewolf movies are a tricky genre,&#x201D; he writes, noting that the creature has long been overshadowed by clich&#xE9;s involving silver bullets, full moons and elaborate transformation sequences.

Eggers traces his own fascination with the genre back to George Waggner's 1941 film &#x201C;The Wolf Man,&#x201D; starring Lon Chaney Jr., while also recalling the memorable transformation sequence created by Rick Baker's team for the 1987 comedy &#x201C;The Monster Squad.&#x201D;

He also singles out Gary Oldman's transformation in Francis Ford Coppola's &#x201C;Bram Stoker's Dracula&#x201D; as particularly frightening, before turning to what he considers the genre's greatest achievement: John Landis' &#x201C;An American Werewolf in London.&#x201D;

&#x201C;I agree with this,&#x201D; Eggers writes of the film's reputation as the best werewolf movie of all time. &#x201C;But it's interesting that the pinnacle of the genre is a comedy, not a horror film.&#x201D;

Set in medieval England, "Werwulf" film follows a man struggling to conceal his monstrous transformations as ahunting party arrives in his village to rid the surrounding countryside of wolves. Aaron Taylor-Johnson stars alongside Lily-Rose Depp, Willem Dafoe and Ralph Ineson, reuniting Eggers with several cast members from his 2024 gothic horror hit &#x201C;Nosferatu.&#x201D;

The Focus Features release marks Eggers' latest exploration of historical folklore and supernatural terror, following &#x201C;The Witch,&#x201D; &#x201C;The Lighthouse,&#x201D; &#x201C;The Northman&#x201D; and &#x201C;Nosferatu.&#x201D; The filmmaker has previously described &#x201C;Werwulf&#x201D; as the darkest material he has written.

&#x201C;Werwulf&#x201D; is scheduled to arrive in U.S. theaters on Dec. 25, 2026, two years after &#x201C;Nosferatu&#x201D; opened on Christmas Day.
