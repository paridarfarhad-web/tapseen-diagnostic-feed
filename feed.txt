The Gothams have long been a launching pad for independent cinema, a place for films that might otherwise get flattened by the studio machine. In 2023, the organization scrapped its $35 million budget ceiling and opened more categories to international titles. The result is a more eclectic ballot, one that puts established auteurs and first-time filmmakers side by side. This year, that means 42 features and 25 performances across 11 categories.

That makes the Gothams a natural first stop for “The Invite,” which we expect to win best feature. Wilde’s ensemble piece, starring Seth Rogen, Penélope Cruz and Edward Norton, has the kind of dinner-party-argument appeal that plays well with this voting body. “Josephine” is my alternate and could follow some jaw-dropping selections like “A Different Man” two years ago. The drama took both the U.S. Grand Jury Prize and the Audience Award at Sundance in January, and the Gothams have never lost their appetite for championing a discovery.

Notably, a different set of industry professionals, five jury members each, for each category, pick the Gotham winners, while Gotham members vote on the audience award. A film can win a jury on artistic grounds while another finds its people in the wider membership.

Founded in 1935, the New York Film Critics Circle is the country’s oldest film critics organization and one of the most influential early stops on the calendar. Its history is full of choices that challenged the Academy’s conventional wisdom. Many of them went on to become landmarks, even when they diverged from the eventual Oscar winner.

This year, we’re predicting “You Can See Everything” to show its strength by winning the top prize for best film, with “The Odyssey” as the alternate. Fielder and Oppenheim’s 174-minute portrait of Elizabeth Holmes premiered as Telluride’s secret screening and has drawn the kind of critical fervor usually reserved for narrative features. A best film win from New York wouldn’t automatically make it an Oscar best picture contender, but it would move the film into a different tier of consideration. “The Odyssey” is the more traditional choice: a major filmmaker, a sweeping production and a likely force in the technical categories.

In directing, Wilde could pull off a deserved citation ahead of Nolan, who has never won from the group before.

Andrew Scott is my choice for actor, with Robert Pattinson’s combined work in “Primetime” and “The Drama” as the alternate. Julianne Moore could win actress for “The Debut,” with Sandra Hüller for “Rose” as a real threat. In supporting actor, Guitarricadelafuente could keep building momentum for “La Bola Negra,” with a surprise citation for someone like John Cho of “Your Mother Your Mother Your Mother” (much like Regina Hall or Tiffany Haddish surprised in their respective years). Kate O’Flynn (“Tender Loving Care”) and Amanda Seyfried (“You Can See Everything”) could be in a battle for supporting actress, or someone else completely.

The screenplay prize could go to Jordan Firstman for “Club Kid,” with Cristian Mungiu’s “Fjord” as the alternate. “Tangles” is my animated film pick over Laika’s “Wildwood,” an interesting split between independent animation and a more established studio shop.

Founded in 1909, the National Board of Review has named a Top 10 every year since 1929. Unlike the critics’ groups, it’s made up of New York-area film enthusiasts rather than working critics. Its selections land just as studios are deciding which campaigns deserve more fuel, which makes it one of the earliest industry signals of the season.

Variety is predicting “Project Hail Mary” for best film, with “Artificial” as the alternate. That would be a fitting choice for a group that has long rewarded accessible, ambitious filmmaking. Phil Lord and Christopher Miller could also take director, ahead of Denis Villeneuve for “Dune: Part Three.” That would follow the three-year current trend of the same film winning both prizes.

If both happen, “Project Hail Mary” has an instant entry for best picture.

The acting picks could be just as revealing, but they don’t always match up with Oscars, so you have to consciously pick an acting winner that probably won’t translate (i.e., Elle Fanning in “A Complete Unknown” or Will Forte in “Nebraska”). Seth Rogen is my actor prediction for “The Invite,” with Matt Damon for “The Odyssey” as the alternate. Inde Navarrette could win actress for “Obsession,” although she can win breakthrough performer. Rachel Zegler won best actress for “West Side Story” (2021) instead of breakthrough her respective year.

John Leguizamo could follow veteran winners who tend to win supporting actor with “The Odyssey.” A "no guts-no glory" pick exists with Elizabeth Debicki could taking supporting actress for “The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth,” with Samantha Morton for “The Odyssey” projected as the runner-up.

The screenplay categories offer openings for two very different films. James Gray could win original screenplay for “Paper Tiger,” ahead of Curry Barker for “Obsession.” In adapted, Sian Heder and Rebekah Taussig could be recognized for “Being Heumann,” with “La Bola Negra” as the alternate.

The specialty awards could be telling, too. I’m predicting “Wildwood” for animated feature, Anthony Ippolito for breakthrough performance in “I Play Rocky” and Barker for directorial debut with “Obsession.” “La Bola Negra” could win international film, while “You Can See Everything” is my documentary pick. Hoyte van Hoytema could take cinematography for “The Odyssey,” and “Spider-Man: Brand New Day” could win stunt artistry. “Naza” is my choice for the NBR Freedom of Expression Award.

The lists may matter as much as the prizes. For the Top 10, look for glaring omissions but don’t put too much stock in them. “The Lord of the Rings: The Return of the King” (2003) settled for the NBR’s ensemble prize without a Top 10 slot, and Guillermo del Toro’s “The Shape of Water” (2017) was left off entirely. Both won best picture. The NBR’s lists are an early endorsement, not a verdict.

The American Film Institute’s annual selections live in a different space from the critics’ prizes. There are no individual performances and no craft citations, just 10 films the institute considers culturally and artistically representative of the year. The Top 10 is restricted to American productions. Works that fall outside its criteria can receive an AFI Special Award, as Kenneth Branagh’s “Belfast” and Questlove’s documentary “Summer of Soul” did in 2021 and Jafar Panahi’s “It Was Just an Accident” did last year.

That makes the AFI list a measure of which films have entered the industry’s broader conversation. There’s no single winner, and because the list is alphabetical, it isn’t a ranking.

We’re thinking: “Artificial,” “Dune: Part Three,” “Elsinore,” “I Play Rocky,” “The Invite,” “Michael,” “The Odyssey,” “Project Hail Mary,” “Wild Horse Nine” and “Wildwood.” “Obsession” is my alternate.

“You Can See Everything” is my Special Award prediction, with “La Bola Negra” as the alternative since neither can win.

Alternate: “Obsession” (Focus Features)

AFI Special Award: “You Can See Everything” (A24) Alternate: “La Bola Negra” (Netflix)

Founded in 1975 by critics working in print, radio and television, LAFCA has become one of the most respected critics' groups in the country. It votes and announces in December, then presents its awards at a ceremony in January.

We’re seeing “The Odyssey” for best film, with “You Can See Everything” as the alternate. That would hand Nolan a major critical endorsement in a week when New York may have gone the other way. That's exactly why the opening stretch matters: different groups can watch the same year of movies and come away with very different pictures.

For director, I'm going with Andrey Zvyagintsev for “Minotaur,” ahead of Ryusuke Hamaguchi for “All of a Sudden.”

With non-gendered acting categories, the group chooses two lead and two supporting performances.

Lead performance could produce one of the more distinctive outcomes of the season, with Jeon Do-yeon for “Possible Love” and John Malkovich for “Wild Horse Nine” sharing the prize. Virginie Efira for “All of a Sudden” and Andrew Scott for “Elsinore” are the alternatives.

In supporting performance, Andrew Garfield could win for “Artificial” alongside Amanda Seyfried for “You Can See Everything.” Penélope Cruz for “The Invite” and Anne Hathaway for “The Odyssey” are the alternate selections.

The screenplay award could go to Jones and McCormack for “The Invite,” with Firstman's “Club Kid” as the alternative. Add it up: a Gotham best feature, a possible New York director win and an LAFCA screenplay prize would give “The Invite” a broad critical footprint before the guilds have said a word.

On the craft side, I'm predicting Linus Sandgren for cinematography for “Dune: Part Three,” Adam Locke-Norton for editing for “You Can See Everything” and Ludwig Göransson for music score for “The Odyssey.” “Backrooms” could surprise in production design, with Danny Vermette ahead of Donald Graham Burt for “The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth.”

If “You Can See Everything” wins both editing and documentary, pay attention. A documentary competing in a category usually owned by narrative films would strengthen the argument that it belongs in the larger best picture discussion.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google Darkseid created the Absolute DC Universe to strip away the good and make evil more pronounced. He took away advantages that some heroes had, such as Superman's earthly family, Wonder Woman being raised on Themyscira, and Batman having wealth to finance his superhero career. However, he also made the villains even more terrifying. In the Absolute Superman comics, that version of Brainiac is scarier than any version the Man of Steel has ever had to face. When looking at the Absolute Batman villains, they are even more terrifying, and some border on extreme nightmare fuel.

Scott Snyder and Nick Dragotta didn't just give Batman's rogues' gallery new costumes in Absolute Batman , but they turned the villains into body-horror monsters that make the original villains look absolutely tame in comparison. Every villain compares in some way to its original depictions, but all of them are infinitely scarier, and one of them is pure nightmare fuel.

Absolute Mr. Freeze debuted in Absolute Batman #7 (2025) by Scott Snyder and Marcos Martin. In the main DC universe, the character debuted as Mr. Zero in Batman #121 (1958) by Dave Wood, Sheldon Moldoff, and Bob Kane. He became Mr. Freeze in the comics in Detective Comics #373 (1968) after the 1966 Batman TV series used the name. He then received a sympathetic backstory thanks to Batman: The Animated Series in the 1990s.

The Absolute version is a completely different person. This is Victor Fries Jr., the son of scientists Victor and Nora Fries, who froze him as a child, and the freezing mutated him. He was horrifying, with icy blue skin, dark veins, and a hunched, unnatural way of moving. He runs V-Core, turns unwitting volunteers into ice zombies, can freeze anything he touches, and keeps his own frozen parents in search of a cure. He also works for the Joker and is in no way the sympathetic Mr. Freeze from the main Batman comics.

Absolute Clayface debuted in Absolute Batman #22 (2026) by Scott Snyder and Werther Dell'Edera. He made his full debut one issue later, and this is where he proved to be one of Batman's deadliest threats. The original Clayface, Basil Karlo, debuted in Detective Comics #40 (1940) by Bill Finger and Bob Kane as a B-list actor with no superpowers who wore a clay mask and killed people in the order their characters die in a film. It wasn't until Matt Hagen in Detective Comics #298 (1961) that he became a shape-shifter.

In the Absolute Universe , Basil Karlo is an Ark M experiment created by the Joker. He shifts between liquid mud and hardened clay, and puddles of his mud follow him like shadows. Anyone who steps in that mud delivers their template to him so he can become them. In Absolute Batman #23, he changed form to look like Batman and killed Tim Drake, making it look like Batman was now a cold-blooded murderer. The main look is a mixture of John Carpenter's The Thing and a being from House of Wax .

Batman is a monstrous human being compared to the main DC Batman, and Bane is even bigger than that. Absolute Bane first appears in Absolute Batman #9 (2025) by Scott Snyder and Nick Dragotta. The classic Bane debuted in Batman: Vengeance of Bane #1 (1992) by Chuck Dixon and Graham Nolan, and this led into the "Knightfall" storyline where Bane became an immediate threat when he broke Batman's back. The version in the Absolute DC Universe makes the original version of Bane look like nothing.

His origin is similar, but the difference is that Absolute Bane kills his own revolutionary father to accept the offer to work for the Joker. He stands about nine feet tall without Venom and can swell to the size of a small building with it. Absolute Bane broke Batman with little effort in Absolute Batman #9 before guarding Bruce in Ark M for months in the next issue. Venom also gives him precise control over his muscle cells and lets him expand his brain to hold more knowledge, so he is a strategist as well as a giant.

Scarecrow is a different kind of frightening in Absolute Batman , and instead of looking like a terrifying monster, he is a lot more cerebral and terrifying because of his more eerie appearance. Absolute Scarecrow debuted in Absolute Batman #19 (2026) by Scott Snyder and Nick Dragotta. The classic Scarecrow debuted in World's Finest Comics #3 (1941) by Bill Finger and Sheldon Moldoff, and his fear toxin never debuted until years later, when he was reintroduced in 1966.

In the Absolute Universe, Dr. Jonathan Crane is one of Ark M's most terrifying doctors, and the Joker recruits him to work for him. His origins were a mystery, as a flock of crows always follows him. He goes barefoot, his eyes and mouth are stitched shut, his speech balloons have uneven lines, and he carries a cigarette that may deliver his fear toxin. He has used his fear toxin to kill Jim Gordon and Joe Chill, both with Batman watching. The fact that Scarecrow does not look like a monster but still delivers terrifying moments speaks volumes.

Out of all the Absolute Batman villains, the Joker is the definition of nightmare fuel. The Absolute Joker first appeared briefly at the end of Absolute Batman #1 (2024) by Scott Snyder and Nick Dragotta, as a wealthy, normal-looking man with slicked-back hair who killed people who joked around him. His monster form and origin arrived in Absolute Batman #15 (2025). The classic Joker debuted in Batman #1 (1940) as a criminal, and he has changed a lot over the years. However, he has never been as terrifying as he is in Absolute Batman .

Joker's name is Joseph "Jack" Grimm V , but the Jack Grimm I-V family line is one man posing as several generations. He was a Gotham orphan who, in 1888, used laughing gas to drug the local crowds and rob them. In monster form, he stands about 15 feet tall with green horns, long claws, and rows of teeth, and #15 is the first time readers hear his laugh. He hunts and eats people on a private island, drains the blood of orphans in his charities to stay young, and funds several villains. When he and Batman finally fought, Batman never stood a chance.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google While some of the most popular romance anime that stay at the top of watchlists are fluffy, low-stakes slice-of-life romps, there is a large subsection of anime fans looking for something a little darker than the average love story. Whether it be character tropes like the infamous yandere archetype, with the timeline-hopping obsessive girlfriend Yuno Gasai from Future Diary that dominated the 2010s to rival yakuza families getting tangled into messy love affairs or even doomed soulmates whose bond goes beyond the grave, dark romance anime have gradually become more sought after every single year.

One of the most important components of these doomed romance stories, however, is their capacity to break fans' hearts. Whether it be through bittersweet reconciliation that allows the leading couple to stay together, or, in more tragic cases, end up separated despite being soulmates. There are plenty of anime who try and achieve a balance of impeccably written romance with twisted, dark themes, but very few manage to achieve it in a way that both resonates and sticks with audiences after the final episode.

Scum's Wish is an incredibly unique spin on twisted romance, as the series doesn't take its audience through steamy red light districts or introduce ravenous supernatural partners, instead, the series takes place in a very normal high school, with seemingly very normal teenagers. Utilizing the "fake dating" trope, the anime follows Hanabi Yasuraoka and Mugi Awaya as they enter a falsified relationship to emotionally cope with their feelings for other people.

Scum's Wish presents itself with contrasting color palettes and character designs much better suited for a typical slice-of-life romance that dives into some of the darker, more cynical feelings that can emerge from unrequited love; as the story progresses, the characters must combat their own self worth, grapple with intense feelings of co-dependency, and struggle with choices that don't always boil down to black and white solutions. The 12-episode anime is available on Prime Video.

A cult-classic that unfortunately flew under the radar when it originally released, Devils' Line follows a university student in her early twenties named Tsukasa Taira, a relatively normal young woman who becomes entangled, and eventually enraptured, with a half-vampire named Yuuki Anzai who works in a police task force in Tokyo tasked with hunting vampires plaguing the city. In Devils' Line , the form of vampirism in-universe doesn't require that the vampire drink blood to sustain themselves, instead, experiencing strong emotions can trigger an insatiable bloodlust and need for violence.

The anime's leading couple meet for the first time when Tsukasa learns about the existence of vampires when a longtime friend reveals his true nature and attempts to kill her. She has an unlikely encounter with Anzai, who saves her life, with the two becoming entangled as Anzai acts as her protector going forward. Given Devils' Line 's exploration into supernatural romance with a creature whose emotional regulation constantly puts Tsukasa's safety in question while obsessively acting as her protector, it's no surprise the anime has become a hit with fans of darker romance stories. Depending on region, Devils' Line can be streamed on Netflix, with the physical Blu-ray release available through online retailers.

Based on a dating sim of the same name, Diabolik Lovers is an infamous reverse harem series that places a soft-spoken human girl into a den of vampires who grow absolutely obsessed with her. Yui Komori is sent to live in a secluded mansion with six handsome and sadistic brothers who treat her as their own "sacrificial bride" and feed off her as often as they can without outright killing her. The 24-episode anime series dives into all the twisted romance narrative threads that make the genre so famous, exploring deep-rooted obsession, the effects of generational trauma, and fears of abandonment that morph into a tragic love story.

The overarching story follows Yui as she learns more about her own history and abilities as a human capable of seeing and comprehending supernatural phenomena. Considering Diabolik Lovers is based on a dating sim, the anime is relatively vague on who Yui will end up with, creating the perfect battleground for shipping wars among fans. Unfortunately, Diabolik Lovers isn't available on any major streaming platforms, however, the anime has been collected into different DVD and Blu-ray collections that can be purchased through online retailers.

Yakuza Fiancé , based on the steamy josei manga of the same title , follows Yoshino Somei, a no-nonsense heiress and granddaughter of Osaka's most prolific yakuza boss who is arranged to marry Kirishima Miyama, the grandson of a syndicate leader from Tokyo in an effort to unite the two rival families. Behind Kirishima's charming persona, the young man hides a disturbed and manipulative streak that causes him to make reckless decisions and respond to conflict in an extremely volatile manner. Yoshino, unable to stand down and be a submissive bride pushes back on his awful attitude, creating an ongoing power struggle between the couple.

The 12-episode anime beautifully depicts the mind games employed by both Kirishima and Yoshino to try and get the upper hand on the other, and the longer they pretend to despise and wish ill will on the other, the more it becomes apparent that they're absolutely obsessed with one another. Aside from exploring the main pair's romance, the series also explores many of the intricate issues plaguing areas under yakuza control, showing the negative effects of rival factions and the consequences of battling over territory. Yakuza Fiancé is currently available to stream on Crunchyroll for audiences around the world.

A major breakout hit for dark and twisted romance fans in 2026, Firefly Wedding has all the makings of a series in the genre that also interweaves the heartache of inevitable tragedy. Set in the Meiji-era, Firefly Wedding follows Satoko Kirigaya, a noblewoman with a debilitating heart condition destined to cut her life short. Wishing to wed and make her family proud of her before the inevitable comes, Satoko is abducted and nearly killed before striking a deal with one of the assassins to marry him in exchange for his protection.

Shinpei Gotou, the assassin, is an eccentric, ruthless, and stunningly handsome man who becomes smitten with Satoko right away, whisking her away to "protect" her, despite her circumstances. Satoko is a beautiful young woman who knows how to play into her feminine nature to win over the mean around her, and as she and Shinpei toy with each other's feelings to make the other jealous, Shinpei's devotion to Satoko takes the pair down an obsessive spiral of unprecedented romance.

Forty years after the release of Little Shop of Horrors , the actor has revealed that he spent some time behind the camera on the musical horror comedy. Director Frank Oz had recently welcomed a child and wanted to spend some time with his family. Unfortunately, the technically ambitious production was already running behind schedule. Moranis stepped in for Oz, inadvertently making the director look bad in the process.

In a rare interview with Empire celebrating the movie's 40th anniversary, Moranis explained that he had the luxury of shooting material that didn't involve the movie's famously elaborate man-eating plant. Oz was regularly spending entire days on just a handful of camera setups because of the technical demands of bringing Audrey II to life. Without having to contend with the enormous puppet, Moranis was able to get through considerably more material. The actor managed to get through between 12 and 14 setups while filling in for the director. Apparently, Oz still gives Moranis a hard time about how productive he was during his brief stint directing the movie. Directing wasn't the only skill Moranis had to put to use on Little Shop of Horrors . While the actor was already an accomplished comedic performer, he was determined to do his own singing in the adaptation of Howard Ashman and Alan Menken's stage musical.

Few directors in Hollywood were better equipped to tackle the technical challenges of Little Shop of Horrors than Oz. The longtime collaborator of Jim Henson had already co-directed The Dark Crystal , directed The Muppets Take Manhattan , and brought Yoda to life in two Star Wars movies. Even with his considerable experience as a puppeteer, getting Audrey II to convincingly perform musical numbers proved to be a challenge.

The larger versions of Audrey II couldn't move fast enough to keep up with the vocals provided by the Four Tops' Levi Stubbs. Oz compensated by filming the puppet at a lower frame rate, allowing its movements to be sped up when the footage was projected at a standard frame rate. Unfortunately for Moranis, that meant the actor had to sing and move in slow motion whenever he shared the frame with Audrey II during those musical numbers. Even something as simple as walking required Moranis to rethink how he was using his body.

The production would face an entirely different challenge when test audiences rejected the movie's original ending . Oz had remained relatively faithful to the stage musical, which saw Seymour and Audrey, Ellen Greene, meet their demise before Audrey II and its offspring took over the world. Audiences had grown so attached to Moranis and Greene over the course of the movie that they weren't particularly receptive to watching the pair get eaten. Oz ultimately gave audiences the happy ending they wanted, though fans can now watch the movie with its original ending restored.

Oz would reunite with Moranis just four years later for My Blue Heaven , a comedy that afforded the director the luxury of working with Moranis and Steve Martin without having to accommodate an enormous puppet. As much as Oz might still give Moranis grief about his productivity behind the camera on Little Shop of Horrors , he clearly enjoyed having him in front of it. Audiences will finally get to see Moranis back on the big screen in 2027 when he reprises his role as Dark Helmet in Spaceballs 2 . The move was, and continues to be, hotly debated amongst fans and creators alike. Barbara Gordon’s a popular character , both as Nightwing’s on-and-off girlfriend and in her own right, and it makes sense that writers would want to use an in-universe reset to make her Batgirl again. Barbara Gordon’s relationship to her disability — and how it affected not only her day-to-day life but also her relationships with her career, her father, and her love life — was compelling to read about, but could be seen as difficult when wanting more action in books.

The worst thing about losing Barbara Gordon as Oracle, though, was that right before the New 52 happened, a largely forgotten Batgirl run proved that it didn’t have to be her. Oracle, like Batgirl and many of the monikers in DC at large, could be passed down. Much like heroes could be trained in the field, so could a hero be trained to help as an Oracle figure. Bryan Q. Miller’s run on Batgirl: Stephanie Brown — which ran from 2009 to August of 2011 — not only showed Barbara Gordon stepping away but presented a character to take her place, Wendy Harris.&nbsp;

Batgirl: Stephanie Brown happened at an interesting point in Batman continuity. Bruce Wayne was “dead”, Dick Grayson was wearing the Batman suit in his place, and Cassandra Cain gave up her Batgirl costume to Stephanie Brown. Pretty quickly, both Dick Grayson and Barbara Gordon realize it isn’t Cass wearing the Batgirl costume anymore, and they try to stop Stephanie Brown. After a few missions with Barbara Gordon begrudgingly helping her, though, Steph gets the woman on her side, and they form Team Batgirl. For a while, it’s the two of them against the world.

At the same time Barbara Gordon takes Stephanie Brown under her wing, she’s also trying to reach out to Wendy Harris. Similar to Steph, Wendy’s the daughter of a criminal mastermind known as Calculator — an old foe of Barbara’s — and, similar to Barbara, a brutal attack has left Wendy without the use of her legs and angry. Leslie Thompkins wants Barbara to take the woman under her wing, believing Wendy could adjust better if she had guidance. According to Leslie, there’s no way to see your path when blinded by anger — it’s a comment not only meant for Wendy, but also Barbara.

That, in addition to Wendy stumbling upon the Oracle basement while Calculator’s on the warpath, gets her onto Team Batgirl. Wendy not only provides tech assistance but also crashes a jet to save Stephanie’s life.

Batman as a comic is full of passed-down mantles. There've been numerous Robins and numerous Batgirls, with each iteration finding a way to make the mantle their own. Oracle may have been formed out of Barbara Gordon’s specific journey, but Wendy Harris showed that it no longer needed her. It’s a running gag through the second half of the run that when Steph gets tech help, she assumes it’s Barbara talking to her, only to realize she’s talking to Wendy seconds later. Proxy gets the job done, and when Barbara is occupied either with Birds of Prey business or simply off the clock, it’s no longer as intense as it once was.

Batgirl: Stephanie Brown set Wendy up with a new status quo, much like the stories that followed The Killing Joke did with Barbara. In the latter part of the run, Wendy starts hallucinating her dead brother, with him hinting that it wasn’t just her body that broke, but her spirit. Leslie Thompkins looks at Wendy’s scans again and compares them to Barbara’s, saying that Barbara’s looked worse, with the unsaid implication that Barbara was still able to recover. Leslie suggests that a trip might fix Wendy’s spirit, suggesting a special clinic that turns out to be in Nanda Parbat. Although dangerous, Wendy insists on going by herself, telling Barbara she’s never really alone.

The comic leaves Wendy there, starting another journey, one that audiences don’t get to see since the New 52 happened a month later, and Wendy Harris as a character hasn’t been revisited since. Still, her trajectory shows the importance of Oracle. Oracle not only saves the lives of the heroes she helps but also gives purpose to the person donning the moniker, even if it doesn’t “fix” them. Given the prevalence of the person in the chair as a trope and how much the heroes of today rely on technology, Oracle’s always going to be useful to have. It doesn’t have to be Proxy — that specific codename has been given to a New Teen Titan anyway — but it’s important that superheroes aren’t just one thing.
