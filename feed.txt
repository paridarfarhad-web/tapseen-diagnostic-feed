Earth 2 's premise was pretty ambitious from the get-go. The series, which premiered on NBC on Nov. 6, 1994, took place in the year 2192, after humanity had largely abandoned an increasingly uninhabitable Earth in favor of orbital space habitats. Led by Devon Adair, a mother desperate to save her young son, Ulysses, a group of colonists traveled aboard the starship Eden Advance to the faraway planet G889. Ulysses suffered from a mysterious disease known as the Syndrome, which affected children born in humanity's orbital habitats. The expedition was supposed to establish a new settlement called New Pacifica for the 250 families who would eventually follow. It was a pretty ambitious plan, especially considering that the fate of so many children depended on it.

After a 22-year journey, the Eden Advance reaches G889, only for the spacecraft to malfunction and crash-land thousands of miles from its intended destination. The remaining group has to make a difficult trek toward New Pacifica. The end result is a group of people fighting for survival in a world they barely understand. It gave the show the chance to revisit some of the classic sci-fi tropes of colonization and survival, and humanity's relationship with the natural world. But it also presented a surprisingly well-developed story about the conflicts that come with having very different people living together in a new world. The characters weren't always working together, and their different perspectives led to plenty of tension. Instead of typical encounters, Earth 2 took an ambitious risk and explored what happens when people arrive somewhere that already has a life of its own.

By the time the colonists set foot on the planet, intelligent life had already been living there for generations. The Terrians appeared to live in close connection with the planet itself, communicating through dreams and seemingly possessing abilities the colonists could barely comprehend. Their relationship with Ulysses became especially important after they helped cure his Syndrome. However, his newfound connection to them would create complications of its own. The scavenging Grendlers and the potentially dangerous Kobas showed just how much the colonists still had to learn about the world they were trying to make their new home.

It was a rich mythology that sent Earth 2 down an interesting path. As the survivors uncovered more about G889, they found themselves with even more questions. And then there was the Council. These guys were the powerful authority that controlled much of humanity's life in the space stations. Their interest in G889 seemed to go far beyond simply providing humanity with a new home. Ulysses' growing connection to the Terrians made him particularly important to that mystery.

Very little phases a Gen Xer — and a bit reason for that are some of the weird movies we watched when maybe we shouldn’t have.

Here are five movies — some released in theaters, some aired on television because someone thought that was a good idea — that are absolutely wild that just about every Gen X kid probably saw in the 1980s and, if we really think about it, explain an awful lot about why we are the way we are now. We’re also throwing in an honorable mention that truly gives insight into just how cynical we are — and it has an absolutely stacked cast.

Let’s start off with that honorable mention because it might be the most “normal” movie on this list. Released in 1986, River’s Edge is a crime drama that has a wildly great cast, especially in retrospect. It starred Crispin Glover, Dennis Hopper, Daniel Roebuck, Keanu Reeves, and Ione Sky in her film debut (though she’s credited as Ione Sky Leitch in this film). The movie follows a group of teenagers but don’t get it twisted that this will be some sort of high school story like a John Huges film gone serious. This is the darker side of the American teen experience and these teens are completely emotionally disconnected, so much so that when one of the group murders his girlfriend, the rest have pretty much zero reaction, at least at first.

The film is a slow burn of things subtly working their way out of control, with a tight narrative that reveals how numb and directionless the teens are without the adults in their lives paying attention. Darker and darker things get revealed. Gen X is largely considered to be cynical and detached as a personality trait these days and River’s Edge is a brilliant snapshot of exactly how that played out in a way that was more “real” than the pop culture of the time would let you believe. Also, it’s abundantly clear how great of a talent Reeves is here.

Now that I’ve thoroughly bummed you out with River’s Edge , let’s get into the weird stuff. In 1985, CBS offered up a two-part, made-for-television special that on the surface sounds innocent enough: a musical adaptation of Lewis Carrol’s Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland and Through the Looking-Glass . It was broadcast just in time for the holiday season that year across December 9th and December 10th. It was absolutely stacked with its cast — we’re talking Sammy Davis Jr., Telly Savalas, Ringo Starr, Carol Channing, and even John Stamos. It was very book accurate, with the first night taking on the first book and the second taking on book two.

The special was also creepy as all get out. The entire production was live action, which meant that the fantasy characters required elaborate costumes on a 1980s scale. the hookah smoking Caterpillar (played by Davis Jr.) is a wild, just off Technicolor cousin of the Dune sandworm. Starr’s Mock Turtle looks like a cat. But the real nightmare fuel here was the weirdly wet-looking Jabberwocky . Imagine a giant sticky larvae with wings, T-rex arms, glowing eyes and horns, wandering around menacing people while, and I cannot stress this enough which is why I say it again, looking sticky and moist. And we haven’t even touched on the parts of the story where humans are turned into live animals. You might be able to still find this one on some corners of the internet if you dare but, seriously, beware the Jabberwock.

Another television moment, in 1983, the Disney Channel aired a special, Hansel and Gretel . The live-action short came from the now-legendary Tim Burton and was surprisingly faithful to the original Brother’s Grimm story, but how it made its way to the faithful nature of that story left small 80s kids pretty much scarred for life. Burton took the German fairy tale, gave it an entirely East Asian cast , incorporated a kung fu fight between Hansel and Gretel and the evil witch who uses candy canes to fight with (that part is actually very cool). It didn’t shy away from any of the darkness of the actual story, either.

But what makes this absolutely weird is just how disturbing various elements are. The special has a character, Dan Dan the Gingerbread Man, who sings a twisted cover of Rod Stewart’s “Da Ya Think I’m Sexy?” while trying to coerce Hansel into eating him piece by piece. Dan Dan is also a very demented looking, colorful puppet. There is also the fact that Vincent Price is the host. It’s twisted nightmare fueled served up as children’s entertainment and that should tell you everything you need to know about Gen X.

Now, every Gen X kid watched The Wizard of Oz on repeat thanks to it being broadcast on television pretty regularly and usually around the holidays, but it’s the 1985 Disney follow-up, Return to Oz that falls right into that sweet spot of “this is a weird movie that we all saw and probably messed us all up.” The film starred Fairuza Balk in her feature debut and gave us a faithful adaptation of L. Frank Baum’s work — more so than the OG classic movie. It picks up after Dorothy comes back from Oz and things aren’t great for her but they aren’t great in Oz either and she soon goes back to deal with a new threat there.

Return to Oz is a dark and disturbing film that borders on body horror. We start with Dorothy about to get electroshock therapy because everyone thinks she’s crazy. Once we’re in Oz, the movie’s primary villain is one who has a glass cabinet full of severed heads that she changes out like most people change outfits and she has big plans to make Dorothy’s head one of them. Then there’s the Wheelers rather than flying monkeys and they’re scarier than all get out. Even the audio is sort of messed up. Dark, twisted and twisted in a kind of familiar and oddly pretty way, that’s how you sum up Return to Oz and it’s definitely something that left a mark on Gen X.

1982’s The Dark Crystal is without question an absolute masterpiece and probably one of the most important fantasy movies of all time. From Jim Henson and Frank Oz, it was the first of kind as a major live-action movie that had no human actors and relied instead on elaborate, groundbreaking animatronics. The world was elaborate, complex, and stunning in its tale of the planet of Thra and specifically of Jen and Kira, two Gelflings on a quest to restore balance to their world and overthrow the evil rule of the Skeksis. It is a really good movie, especially in retrospect.

But try being a kid in the 1980s watching it. The Skeksis? They’re huge, disgusting buzzard-like monsters that are more than a little homicidal. Jen and Kira are small, delicate things and there’s a sequence where you see Gelflings having their essence drained from them that is absolutely horrific. Sure, these are puppets and animatronics, but you can’t unsee the violence and horror — especially when some part of you is seeing these characters as strange versions of like, Big Bird.

You can’t have a Gen X movie list without Heathers. Coming in right at the end of the 80s in 1989, Heather’s might be the funniest movie on this list and maybe the least horrifying. Consider this Gen X’s Mean Girls, just darker. Starring Winona Ryder and Christian Slater, it took the John Hughes high school world of it all and took it dark, making the quest for popularity and peer pressure actually lethal. Like, literally, someone dies by drinking a mug of drain cleaner.

If River’s Edge spoke to the alienation and disconnect of Gen X, Heather’s did the same but flipped into something a bit more palatable and added the painful parts we didn’t want to talk about: the way being alienated from mainstream teen culture as it was fed to us could feel as well as what our distrust for authority actually looked like. It is also a very quotable, quippy film and while Heather’s definitely shaped us, of all the movies on the list, this one probably best explains us. It also still holds up after all this time, kind of like Gen X itself.

But somehow, Playmates decided to turn Toxie into an action figure line with 1991's Toxic Crusaders , tying in to the just-as-inexplicable Toxic Crusaders cartoon that aired a brief thirteen episode season that same year. Who thought this was a good idea? A clue lies within the manufacturer of the Toxic Crusaders toyline: Playmates. In 1988, Playmates had a surprise monster hit with their Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles action figure line, which was still the most popular action figure line on the market in 1991. With its similar premise of "mutant hero born out of toxic waste," it wasn't that far-fetched to imagine kids having a similar response to The Toxic Avenger . And while the source-material from Troma wasn't exactly kid-friendly, neither were the original Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles comics from Mirage. The series and its toys may not have lasted long, but like everything else ever to emerge from Tromaville, they have built a dedicated cult following.

No definitive reason has ever been settled on for what happened to the Toxic Crusaders series and figures. Alternately, controversy over the R-rated source material and general marketplace oversaturation have been blamed: the early nineties were a golden age for both intellectual-property based cartoon series and tie-in toylines. The expansion of cable television meant that there were now far more networks with competing children's programming, inspiring series we're still talking about today . Most of them, however, met the same fate as Toxic Crusaders , lasting for only a season and condemning their tie-in toylines to languish on-shelves. The Playmates Toxic Crusaders line was not a strong seller, but the enduring cult popularity of the Toxic Avenger toyline has given it a second-life with action figure collectors, who are willing to pay a premium for rare, end-of-line release like the Apocalypse Attackcopter vehicle .

Nine figures and five vehicles were produced for the Toxic Crusaders line. With their neon-colors and gross-out sculpts, the resemblance to Playmates' Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles line is unmistakeable. The good guy faction was headed up by the Toxic Avenger himself, joined by Major Disaster, Nozone, Headbanger, and Junkyard. The line's four villains were led by Doctor Killemoff, joined by henchmen Psycho, Bonehead, and the Radiation Ranger. Each figure came packaged with a special glow-in-the-dark accessory, which came sealed in a plastic bag, labeled "SECRET TOXIC GLOW-IN-THE-DARK WEAPON: SO RADIOACTIVE WE'VE PUT IT UNDER WRAPS." Three to five additional weapons and accessories were included with each figure, finding them complete poses a significant challenge to collectors today. Especially sought-after is the Toxic Avenger's signature mop, without which the figure just doesn't look right.

Nozone is the most "gross-out" character in the line, featuring an exaggeratedly-giant nose (hence the name), as well as sculpted splatters of snot all over the figure. He's a former test pilot, mutated by flying through a hole in the ozone layer. After a crash-landing into a radioactive silo filled with pepper, the toxic chemicals in Nozone's body grant him the mutant ability to produce powerful sneezes. Tongue was firmly in-cheek when these characters were created, to be certain. Headbanger's origin calls DC's Firestorm to mind, while his design evokes Masters of the Universe 's Two-Bad : a scientist and a singing telegram fall into an atom-smasher, merging into a single being. Headbanger is at first one of Doctor Killemoff's henchmen, but switches sides after discovering the Toxic Crusaders get more dates (really). Junkyard is also the result of a fusion, a half-man, half-dog created by lightning-striking a toxic waste contaminated homeless man and his pet. Major Disaster is comparatively bland: he's a soldier who gains the ability to control plants from a radioactive swamp, a little like Man-Thing or Swamp Thing .

Doctor Killemoff is a native of the planet Smogula, trying to terraform (Smogulaform?) Earth into a polluted world akin to his ravished home-planet. He wears a breathing apparatus, depicted as an elaborate, multi-piece accessory, to help him survive in Earth's toxic fresh air. Psycho is his bio-mechanoid lackey, and possesses powers of prognostication that go ignored by his master: every episode, Psycho is able to accurately predict how the Toxic Crusaders will foil their plans. Bonehead is an acid-rain scarred punk and former enemy of the pre-transformation Toxic Avenger, now anointed commander of the "Radiation Rangers," Killemoff's force of mutant foot soldiers. Additional villain characters like the monstrous "Polluto" were featured in the series, though the toyline was cancelled before they could be given a plastic counterpart.

Somehow, Toxic Crusaders occupied the confluence of three trends. Most immediately, there was an early nineties trend of environmentally-conscious children's cartoons, with these themes cropping up in Captain Planet and the Planeteers and Swamp Thing as well as Toxic Crusaders . Besides Teenage Mutant Ninja Turtles, it was the prevalence of these series that convinced Troma and their partners to move ahead with Toxic Crusaders . The other trends were longer-term, and less easily explained. Beginning in the mid-eighties with series like Rambo: The Force of Freedom and RoboCop , animation studios and TV networks looked to R-rated film properties to provide recognizable franchise names for their children's series. The principle made sense: R-rated franchises and characters had the allure of "forbidden fruit" to children, and these series would let them see them in action. Other times, companies like Kenner would go ahead with a kid-ified version of a property like Aliens or Predator without a tie-in series, trading on the existing adult name of the franchise alone. There was a somehow-unforeseen drawback: parents.

The parents of these children were also familiar with properties like Aliens, RoboCop , and The Toxic Avenger, and it's been suggested they played a key role in the demise of these toylines and cartoons. Although largely apocryphal, the contention is that parents just weren't comfortable with these grown-up franchises being marketed to their kids, and wouldn't buy the toys or let them watch the tie-in. The most significant backlash was seen in the late eighties, when parent groups successfully had several companies' Nightmare on Elm Street children's toys discontinued. However, these factors also contribute to the aftermarket for these toys, drawing adult collectors them decades later. Some are just fans of the property, fascinated by the sheer weirdness of Toxic Avenger action figures. Others are those same kids whose parents wouldn't let them collect the line back in 1991, finally making good on the desires of their eight-year-old self. Either way, Toxic Crusaders has become one of the stranger footnotes of its era, forgotten by most toy collectors, but passionately collected by a dedicated cult following.

The series will offer a long-form version of what a generation of fans got from the Harry Potter films, and (hopefully) will take readers of the books on a much more complete and familiar journey. As the premiere date gets closer, we're seeing much more of what the finished production looks like, and diehard fans are spotting some pretty fine details that show the level of fidelity the show is showing to the text.

The latest set of Harry Potter promotional images showcases a milestone moment from the first novel, Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone : Harry Potter's first trip to Diagon Alley, the hidden magical market where all Wizards and Witches go to shop for their mystic essentials. However, fans are also taking note of something else in the image: the fashion Harry is wearing before he gets some proper wizard's robes.

The official Harry Potter website posted a new featured article titled "Six fashionable findings from the HBO Original Harry Potter series costume department". It helps viewers get a deeper sense of the intense level of detail that was put into the show. The first item on the list is "How to spot magical people in the Muggle world," and it features the above image of Harry Potter (actor Dominic McLaughlin) taking in the wonder of Diagon Alley, while wearing an outfit that very much looks like what a '90s kid would be wearing.

In the breakdown, the style and design team behind Harry Potter describe how they had to create a sense of fashion for the Wizarding World that felt like it could plausibly exist alongside the fashion of non-magic humans (or "Muggles") of the time. That meant "a lot of synthetics. The costume department scoured charity shops and rag-and-bone yards, consulting their family albums for unglamourous ensembles to represent early 90s fashion: unfiltered."

There's also an interesting amount of logic that went into guiding the style choices for the series: "Magical people don’t stick to trends," the site states. "They would likely ditch high street shops for jumble sales, culminating in more eclectic, unconventional wardrobes. Don’t expect all witches and wizards to look the same."

There's an even deeper dive into the fashion of Harry Potter (2026), check it out on the franchise's official site .

The Harry Potter movies that Warner Bros. put out started in 2001 with Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone , and ended in 2011 with Harry Potter and the Deathly Hallows – Part 2 . The studio chose to update the story to a modern-day setting (at that time), meaning that characters wore 2000s fashion trends to make them relatable to audience members of similar age. It raised some ire with hardcore fans of the books, as there are some deeper metaphors and symbolism about real-world politics and culture at that time, which were invariably altered by the time shift.

Now, the TV series is going the opposite route with its period piece format. It'll be interesting to see how viewers react to that, and if the book-accurate setting really does change or enhance the experience of the story, in visual form.

Harry Potter will premiere on HBO on Christmas Day. Do you like the '90s look?

As Inside the NBA ’s latest season on ESPN continues, questions continue to swirl around the show in regard to its future. Longtime co-host Ernie Johnson has officially confirmed his plans to leave the show at the end of this season, and colleague Charles Barkley apparently plans to do the same. There’s been a lot of discussion regarding who might replace the beloved sports commentators, and Barkley recently pitched his successor. While it’s a solid pick, I’d admittedly prefer to see another seasoned pundit take over Barkley’s post.

Barkley – also known as “The Round Mound of Rebound” to some – is definitely someone who loves to speak his mind. So it doesn’t surprise me at all that he’d have thoughts on who might be able to take his place on Inside . The former Phoenix Sun was recently quoted as saying he didn’t believe anyone but fellow ex-player Jalen Rose could replace him at the desk. The notion of Rose taking over for Barkley is actually quite reasonable, considering his resume as a broadcaster. He’s not only served as a fill-in analyst for Inside the NBA for years now, but he’s also taken on hosting duties for ABC/ESPN over the years. I personally enjoy seeing Rose when he appears on Inside , and his relaxed demeanor usually serves as a firm counterbalance to others on the dais like the vivacious Barkley or Shaquille O’Neal . With that said, there’s another recurring analyst I’d love to see join the show instead.

Inside has welcomed plenty of insightful and entertaining guest hosts over the years, from the cool Vince Carter to the wise-cracking Draymond Green (who has a rivalry with Charles Barkley) . Someone who, for me, has always been a welcome presence on the show is Chris Webber, who’s been appearing on the show for years. He obviously has plenty of broadcasting experience, and he’s a wealth of knowledge when it comes to basketball. I’d say the show would be lucky to have him full-time.

Again, though, this isn’t to say that Jalen Rose wouldn’t be able to do the job effectively. There may actually be a reality in which both players end up on the show full time by next season. However, that’s assuming ESPN is looking to bring on two former players and not get another non-ex-player analyst to specifically fill the void that’ll be left by Johnson.

To be honest, it may also behoove me not to get too ahead of myself right now, as the season is still young. Also, while Charles Barkley’s retirement claim is seeming more likely , he hasn’t said much more about it in the wake of Ernie Johnson’s announcement. We’ll just have to wait and see what happens and how the producers choose to proceed should Barkley leave the fold along with Johnson.
