&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google For all the memorable characters Grand Theft Auto has introduced over the years, there's always been something a little off about how the series approaches its protagonists. They're often larger-than-life personalities with enough baggage to fill an entire criminal enterprise, yet their personal stories can feel surprisingly disconnected from the chaos happening around them. GTA 5 made an ambitious attempt to address this by giving players three protagonists with wildly different personalities. It was an entertaining experiment, but it was a hard sell to get you to feel genuine compelled in their actual lives. This is especially apparent when you recall Rockstar didn't really bother expand upon their stories after the credits. That's where Grand Theft Auto 6 gets the narrative right.

Jason and Lucia are exactly the kind of protagonists I've wanted to see from this franchise. Rather than splitting attention between three people with competing ambitions, Rockstar builds its latest crime saga around a relationship that gives the entire adventure a much stronger emotional foundation. They're partners whose futures are tied together, for better or considerably worse, which means their actions take on an inherent emotional risk, adding an extra layer to literally everything they say and do. This shift makes an enormous difference, as it gives players a natural core to care about the about them, in one of the most dangerous professions: being a criminal.

One of the biggest strengths of GTA 5 is undoubtedly its three-protagonist setup. Michael, Franklin, and Trevor each bring something different to the table, and switching between them creates opportunities for comedy and conflict that wouldn't work nearly as well with a single lead. But that structure comes with a trade-off. The story constantly has to divide its attention between three separate lives, each with their own problems and motivations. Their paths intersect in entertaining ways, but the relationships between them rarely become the emotional foundation holding the entire experience together. They're memorable individuals first and a unified group second. I would not call that a flaw, as that's what GTA 5 set out to accomplish, but it does leave its narrative feeling less intimate than it could have been.

Jason and Lucia's relationship isn't some subplot that Rockstar occasionally revisits between heists. It's the connective tissue running through the entire experience. Their criminal ambitions are intertwined because neither character is simply pursuing some grand scheme in isolation. They're trying to build a future together, even when their circumstances make that prospect seem increasingly ridiculous. That gives their decisions a stronger weight. A dangerous job isn't just another opportunity to make money or create some spectacular mess. It's something that could affect the person standing beside them, that's a natural concept for any human to feel, whether its a spouse, a family member, or anything between. I find that considerably more compelling than simply having another protagonist to switch between.

Jason and Lucia don't need to constantly agree with each other to make their partnership interesting, nor does their connection depend on turning every interaction into some grand romantic declaration. Their chemistry comes through in the smaller moments, the banter, the disagreements, and the sense that these two people have developed a life together despite everything working against them. Rockstar has always been capable of writing memorable personalities, but giving those personalities a relationship worth investing in is a different challenge altogether. GTA 6 understands that distinction, and it makes Jason and Lucia considerably more interesting as a pair than either protagonist would be in isolation.

One of the things I find most interesting about Jason and Lucia is that their story doesn't have to stop whenever a mission ends. Rockstar has been building open worlds for years, where players can lose entire afternoons doing practically anything that catches their attention. Yet those diversions have often existed separately from the narratives unfolding in the main story. GTA 6 makes a more concerted effort to bring those two experiences together. Spending time with Jason and Lucia outside missions can reveal more about their personalities through conversations and interactions that respond to what's happening around them. Rather than relying entirely on cutscenes to develop its protagonists, Rockstar gives players opportunities to get to know them simply by spending time in their shoes.

This approach apparent in the smaller interactions between the two. Jason and Lucia can hold hands while exploring the world together, wait for one another when getting into a car, and even hold doors open for each other. None of these actions is particularly spectacular on its own, but that's precisely why I like them. They're the sorts of things that make a relationship feel… true, rather than something that only exists when the story needs an emotional moment. Rockstar's developers have discussed how these interactions evolved from individual mission ideas into features players could experience throughout the open world. It's a surprisingly thoughtful use of the freedom that makes GTA 6 so appealing in the first place.

I think this is an important step forward for the series because it makes the time between major story beats feel more meaningful. The best open-world games understand that their protagonists shouldn't disappear as characters whenever players decide to ignore the main objective for a while. I'd argue that's one of the greatest faults of the sub-genre, so its great to see GTA 6 take a stab at tackling this long-running problem. You aren't always watching Jason and Lucia's story happen. Instead, sometimes you're simply driving around together, and the game finds ways to make even that feel like part of it. It's ultimately a clever way to strengthen the central narrative and bring the open-world design closer together in unison. Personally, I hope Rockstar carries that philosophy into whatever comes next.

Batman is an idea born from Bruce's grief, an ideal that strives to use his pain to make the world a better place.

Bruce Wayne is Batman's heart. Bruce Wayne is the man who fills out the mask, not the other way around.

Being Batman is obviously an extremely taxing and difficult endeavor. Bruce has had to give up countless opportunities at happiness to pursue his career as the Dark Knight, and when push comes to shove, he'll always put being Batman above just about everything else in his life. Seriously, Bruce gives up being Batman constantly in those movies. Regardless, if Bruce could give up being Batman in good conscience, he would, but he also enjoys being Batman.

It gives Bruce the chance to use the agony he's felt ever since his parents' deaths for something useful. Being Batman lets Bruce fight back against the unfailling tide of darkness that Gotham is always drowning in and pull others out of it. Again, all of these misconceptions are based in truth in some capacity, and this one is so easy to see. After all, Batman proudly proclaims that he's vengeance in the night, and his rage and despair at his parents' death definitely drive him to save the day. He's a man filled with rage that he's never let go of, but that's not the real reason he pushes himself to save the day. Batman exists to save people. &nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping is in theaters at last , bringing to the big screen the story of Haymitch’s games, the Second Quarter Quell. It’s a story that fans of Suzanne Collins’ The Hunger Games series have been eager to see since the book was released in 2025 and finally helped readers understand where the true origins of what would ultimately be Katniss’ rebellion began but there was something else very specific fans were looking for with the film. Fans wanted to see the original cast of The Hunger Games film — Woody Harrelson, Jennifer Lawrence, and Josh Hutcherson — reunite as their characters. It was truly the one things that fans really wanted.

And The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping technically provided, but what should have been a triumphant moment capping of Haymitch’s story and providing a beautiful bit of closure on the saga as well as a lovely moment for fans, the scene just didn’t live up to expectations. A genuinely meaningful moment was sacrificed in the name of fan service and it’s disappointing.

In the movie, after a montage of Haymitch’s post-victory life, walking us through him being the “worst victor ever” as he promised Maysilee he would be and saw him bury Tribute after Tribute, we eventually get to the now-older Haymitch on his birthday. This time, though, there will be no reaping. The square has vines growing over the old platform and Haymitch is joined by Katniss and Peeta. It’s a very short moment with some throwaway dialogue. The trio talk about the place being peaceful, about it being a nice place to mark his birthday, a suggestion about going out to the lake that Haymitch declines to instead go to the meadow. As he goes to leave, Katniss and Peeta thank Haymitch and he smiles before going to the meadow to watch the geese.

While the scene sounds nice, it doesn’t really have that much emotional resonance to the story and it also feels wildly out of place. First, the whole scene is styled oddly. The wigs on the cast — particularly on Josh Hutcherson’s Peeta — are distractingly bad. There’s also how they character are portrayed. The scene simultaneously feels like it is happening the first reaping day after the war which we know is not true but also like it is a good bit into the future. Haymitch also comes across as too much at peace and too easily “healed”. Instead of feeling like a somber moment to sit with the journey and understand just how much has truly changed, the scene feels more like a “here is what you asked for” in the most superficial way possible. It just doesn’t work.

The book scene would have actually been a better epilogue and still would have allowed for fans to have that much desired on-screen reunion. In the book, the scene is where it is revealed that Haymitch has been telling his story to Katniss, finally opening up about his Games and everything he lost. We also see Haymitch given goose eggs and Peeta builds an incubator for him as a way to honor Lenore Dove. The framing gives space for Haymitch to finally, perhaps for the first time in decades, to sit with his grief and process everything he’s lost. It’s a moment of healing and hard-won peace that has a lot of weight. It isn’t a “happily ever after” like the movie scene frames things, but there is a hopefulness in it that is raw and beautiful.

The emotional resonance of how Collins framed things in the book would have been not only a great way to cap off the film (and would have made up for some of the things that didn’t make it to the screen ) but also would have let fans truly see Harrelson, Lawrence, and Hutcherson as the iconic characters in their true forms one more time. With Sunrise on the Reaping likely the final chapter of the story, this emotionally weighty moment is what the ending deserved. And it’s what we should have gotten.

The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping is in theaters now.
