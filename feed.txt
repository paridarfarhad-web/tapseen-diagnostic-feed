&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google People got away with a lot of nonsense in the 1980s. Racism, misogyny, and homophobia were rampant in film and television. Thankfully, the tide has since turned, and there is much less tolerance for bigotry in media today. That said, the 1980s also yielded some incredible films that tackled important subject matter. Regardless of how uncomfortable it can be, dark, potentially offensive, dark, or cynical art is sometimes the best way to probe the reasons behind our behaviors, find catharsis in our societal problems, and even understand the villains and perpetrators behind them.

Today, we’ve reached a point in culture where nearly every big studio movie gets filtered through numerous focus groups and is often sanitized of any edge that could make anyone uncomfortable. That means some of the most iconic ’80s films, particularly midnight movies and cult classics, would never have been made today unless a rogue independent studio stuck its neck out for them. Nonetheless, these films are essential not only to film history, but to pop culture and culture at large. The films on this list likely wouldn’t get the green light now, but it’s worth asking why and considering the positive impacts they’ve had.

Directed by Michael Lehmann and starring Winona Ryder and Christian Slater, Heathers follows high schooler Veronica, who is part of her school’s ruthless “Heathers” clique. As she becomes disgusted by their cruelty, she simultaneously falls for the rebel JD, and their attempts to humble the popular kids escalate into staging suicides and a plot to blow up the school. The climax centers on JD wiring the school’s boiler room with explosives to kill the entire student body during a pep rally. In a post-Columbine world, however, a comedic school bombing likely wouldn’t make it past a first draft. Additionally, the duo repeatedly murders popular kids and stages their deaths as suicides, making suicide seem cool and trendy among the teens.

Yet despite satirizing some particularly dark themes, the film has retained a devoted audience of fans who love it because it weaponizes its taboos to expose hypocrisy. Heathers relies on its shocking premise to skewer how adults, the media, and even peers might exploit tragedy for clout. It’s also important to film history because it broke John Hughes’ mold of writing teens with earnest sentimentality, moving instead into a more cynical, postmodern place. Without it, there would be no Mean Girls , no Jennifer’s Body , and no Euphoria .

Written and directed by Bruce Robinson and starring Richard E. Grant and Paul McGann, Withnail and I is a British cult classic that spins a yarn about two unemployed actors: Withnail and Marwood, as they escape their squalid flat for a weekend at a country cottage owned by Withnail’s Uncle Monty. Yet, it would never get past the censors today, primarily because its central dramatic tension relies on what would now be considered a predatory gay stereotype. Uncle Monty is an older gay man who relentlessly pursues Marwood after Withnail lies and presents Marwood as gay and interested. Modern audiences would likely spit this out, for good reason, as it portrays the older queer character as a persistent sexual predator. Additionally, its overtone of bleak nihilism amounts to essentially zero conventional moral growth by the end of the film, which is rarely seen today, particularly in major studio films.

Still, the merit of Withnail and I lies in its honest portrayal of creative failure. Whereas Hollywood stories often see struggling artists eventually getting their big break, Withnail and I captures the bottomless pit of watching talent go unrecognized. Its bittersweet ending, in which Withnail recites Hamlet to wolves in the rain, is a beautiful tableau of the lonely artist’s plight. A blueprint for British comedy, its hilarious, rapid-fire dialogue and celebration of glorious losers directly influenced modern UK comedies like Peep Show and Shaun of the Dead . Even though the Uncle Monty plot is dated, the film treats Monty’s grief and loneliness with empathy, and stereotypes aside, Withnail and I remains an essential piece of the British film canon.

Written and directed by auteur David Lynch, Blue Velvet revolves around college student Jeffrey (Kyle MacLachlan), whose amateur investigation leads him down a rabbit hole into the sadomasochistic suburban underworld controlled by Dennis Hopper’s Frank Booth, who has kidnapped the family of nightclub singer Dorothy (Isabella Rossellini). It wouldn't be made today because some of the film’s most memorable set pieces depict graphic sexual violence, with Frank assaulting Dorothy. It was a difficult watch even for its time, with Roger Ebert famously condemning Blue Velvet for how it degraded Rossellini (though she still defends it to this day). Jeffrey also spies on Dorothy, and eventually enters his own violent sexual relationship with her, stepping out of the role of hero and into something more ambiguous.

The way the violence is portrayed in Blue Velvet , and the fact that a man directed it, would seldom fly for a major studio in 2026. Yet despite being “problematic” through today’s more rigid ethical lens, it may be one of the most important pieces of cinema ever made. Its refusal to offer easy answers is what makes it a masterpiece. By forcing the audience to inhabit Jeffrey’s perspective, it explores complicity and darkness lurking in ordinary people, ultimately exposing the rot beneath the sheen of suburban America.&nbsp;

Without Blue Velvet proving that surrealist art-house horror could captivate a mainstream audience, Lynch might never have gotten the chance to make later masterworks like Twin Peaks and Mulholland Drive . His pieces have inspired everything from The Sopranos to Atlanta to Severance . While it may be easy to dismiss a film based on rigid moral rules, it’s important to remember that how characters behave in stories isn’t a reflection of the director’s own stance. Rather, these are fictional explorations of difficult topics that help us make sense of the nonsensical world around us.

In 1920, Lovecraft sat down and wrote the horror short story "From Beyond," a tale that was first published 14 years later in a magazine called The Fantasy Fan . The story was told from the first-person point of view of an unnamed narrator who had experiences with a scientist named Tillinghast, a man who created a device that helped people see outside the scope of accepted reality. The alternate dimension it reveals is full of murderous monsters that attempt to get into our world. It is the type of Lovecraftian horror the author helped create.

Director Stuart Gordon and actor Jeffrey Combs set out to turn this H.P. Lovecraft short story into a movie, and on October 24, 1986, From Beyond was released into theaters. While the movie was a box office flop, it received decent reviews and has gone on to become a beloved cult classic for horror fans.

From Beyond Delivers a Perfect Lovecraftian Horror Experience Image Courtesy of Empire Pictures In From Beyond , Ted Sorel stars as Dr. Edward Pretorius, a hedonistic and ambitious scientist who creates a machine called the Resonator that allows him to see beyond our reality. It is the same idea as in the short story, but what happens at the end of the short story, when the monsters invade our world, happens at the beginning of the movie. A creature from the alternate dimension bites his assistant, Dr. Crawford Tillinghast (Jeffrey Combs), and then Pretorius, mad with power, turns the machine up to full power and ends up dying by decapitation. Crawford is then arrested for his murder.

The movie then follows Crawford as he begins to mutate into something otherworldly. He meets a doctor named Katherine (Barbara Crampton) and is accompanied by a detective named Bubba Brownlee (Ken Foree), who is investigating Pretorius' death. They return to the lab, and when Crawford turns the machine back on, the creatures come back, including Dr. Pretorius, whose consciousness is now altered and part of the other world. From there, the movie becomes a Lovecraftian body-horror nightmare.

The movie cost Gordon $2.5 million to make, but it only brought in $1.3 million at the box office. However, it still received positive reviews, with a 73% critics score on Rotten Tomatoes and a 70% audience rating. The critics' consensus on Rotten Tomatoes reads, " From Beyond stands as a stunningly grotesque Lovecraft adaptation with a dazzling blend of chilling effects and brainy, cosmic horror."

Stuart Gordon specialized in experimental horror , and it was clear throughout his entire career that H.P. Lovecraft was his biggest influence. Just as Sam Raimi created his movies with Bruce Campbell by his side in the lead roles, Gordon had Jeffrey Combs by his side most of the time. The two are as interconnected as Raimi and Campbell, but their work was so offbeat that they never had the same crossover success as Raimi and Campbell, though their movies all stand as cult classics, especially for people who love cosmic horror.

From Beyond is the second of his collaborations with Combs and horror legend Barbara Crampton (who also starred in Re-Animator ). It was, in fact, his splatter masterpiece Re-Animator that made him a horror icon. Just like From Beyond , Re-Animator was also based on an H.P. Lovecraft story, a serial novelette called "Herbert West — Reanimator." Once again, this was a cosmic body-horror film. But this was about bringing the dead back to life, and how that was never a smart idea. Just like in From Beyond , a scientist who tried to play God ended up falling in the end.

Both movies also share the trait of using Lovecraft's stories as a launching pad rather than the template for the movies. With the foundation laid by Lovecraft in his stories, Gordon was able to create something new and fresh, while still maintaining the themes and ideas that the master sci-fi author built into his stories. From Beyond goes even further than Re-Animator . While Re-Animator was a gross-out horror tale , From Beyond just throws the goopy creatures at the audience and turns the scientist Pretorius into something beyond words. It is body horror that eclipses anything David Cronenberg was doing at the time, at least as far as gross-out effects are concerned. It is also bitingly funny, which is where Gordon was able to really shine, making his Lovecraftian horror movies unlike any other in the subgenre.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google If you've been reading modern Marvel , there's one storyline that's been recurring multiple times across numerous comics. Superheroes keep having their identities stolen in one capacity or another. This has become especially prevalent with some of the heroes' most iconic villains, with prominent examples being Norman Osborn temporarily taking over as Spider-Man without Peter's permission and the Eldest stealing the Hulk's body in the pages of Infernal Hulk . On the surface, this doesn't appear to be anything out of the ordinary, as heroes are plenty known to share similar stories across the board, but this goes far deeper than simple circular and mirrored storytelling.

This is actually a reflection of a major change in how Marvel treats its superheroes. When they were first created, the vast majority of them were meant to embody the world-outside-your-window perspective, being portrayed as more down-to-earth and less paragon-esque than DC's heroes were in the Silver and Bronze Ages. That's continued to this day, to some extent, but that barrier has been breaking down steadily over decades. Nowadays, Marvel's heroes are ideals given human form, and the reason villains keep taking their faces is to explicitly relate to how important the heroes have become to us.

Back in the 1960s, Marvel sought to differentiate itself from DC by offering a different type of hero. The vast majority of DC's catalogue at the time was taken up by paragons who could do no wrong, from Superman to Wonder Woman to Batman. Their stories were often simplistic, and thus their ideologies were often portrayed the same way. Marvel challenged that by grounding its heroes in the then-modern day with deeply personal struggles. Think of the family drama inherent to the Fantastic Four or Spider-Man's efforts to balance making rent with his superheroic duties. This resulted in heroes who felt rougher around the edges, in a way. They made more unjustifiable mistakes than DC's heroes did.

However, it didn't stay that way forever. Over time, Marvel started portraying its heroes in a more traditional DC way, while DC's own heroes took on deeper, more human flaws. Spider-Man is a perfect example . In his earliest years, Peter was a brash, abrasive young man who had a chip on his shoulder the size of Texas. Yet, as he did more and more heroics, that part naturally faded away. Modern portrayals of Peter, even when they tackle the start of his career, portray him as the wiser, more centered hero that he's become. You could accuse this of Marvel sanitizing Peter in some way, and that may be a part of it, but the reason that this happens across the board is that these heroes have come to mean something much more to fans over the years.

The moments that stick with the most fans the most are when heroes are at their most heroic. Spider-Man pushing past his limits to save Aunt May. Hulk choosing to forgive the Leader and himself. These moments are noble and heroic in their truest sense, so as the hero continues to develop, these moments and interpretations stick when other aspects don't. Repeated moments like this have elevated the heroes from characters who tried to be heroic to the very faces of what it means to embody those ideals. They've become more and more like paragons, more and more like modern myths meant to inspire. Which brings us to why so many villains want to take those heroes' identities.

The supervillain stealing a hero's identity storyline has become a true epidemic. In the past few years alone, it's happened to most of Marvel's biggest names. The Edlest has taken the Hulk's body, Norman impersonated Spider-Man, Grant Rogers is back to pretending to be Captain America, and more. This idea of someone else becoming a hero has expanded to an entire subgenre, including different takes on it like A.I.M. attempting to make their own Tony Stark, not Iron Man, and Beta Ray Bill grappling with the reality of everyone thinking he was Thor after the God of Thunder was erased from existence. It's going on across the board, and the answer isn't as simple as villains wanting to abuse everyone's faith in these heroes. Rather, it's an examination of what these heroes stand for.

This happens to Cap , Spider-Man, and Hulk more than anyone else, and there's a reason for that. The first two are the de facto paragons of Marvel, representing the peak of the heroism all others strive to emulate. The Hulk, meanwhile, has always served as a window into the inner rage and anguish that a person could go through, and how working through that or living with it could work. These three are ideas just as much as they are characters, which makes them perfect to contrast against others who steal or borrow their names.

Whenever someone steals one of these superheroic identities, the story itself serves as an argument of what it is that makes that person a hero. We look through all of their choices and actions, and come to the conclusion that these characters have risen to the ranks that they have because they always attempt to do the right thing. By juxtaposing the hero's success to how these villains fail to live up to those standards or miss the point, it highlights how special the heroes are. They have fully transitioned from being characters to being something greater, and there's no better way to show that than by direct comparison.

Marvel's writers have been exploring the place heroes have in the world not as individuals, but as ideas to strive for. Spider-Man is someone who struggles but always puts being responsible over personal gain. Captain America is a man who will never stop fighting for liberty and equality. These characters mean something more to people, and thus they are being treated more as myths and explorations of what people wish to be over standard protagonists. Simply put, Marvel's heroes have been constantly getting more heroic, and the best way to dissect that is with villains who show us everything that the heroes aren't.

Today’s world of collector-focused toylines means any property, no matter how obscure, is fair game for an action figure line. But going back a few decades, this was hardly the case: conventional wisdom held that the primary target for any toys were children, and as such, any toyline had to be something manufacturers could see kids wanting. Doctor Who , despite being a decades-running staple of British television, somehow avoided almost any toy merchandising during its original, twenty-six year lifetime. Denys-Fisher (Mego’s U.K. affiliate) released a small range of Doctor Who figures in 1977 and 1978; several companies offered Daleks; but for many collectors, the dream was an extensive, Star Wars -style line of characters from across eras and Doctors.&nbsp;

Model train manufacturer Dapol finally delivered in 1987, although with perhaps underwhelming results. That didn’t stop Doctor Who fans from turning out in force to collect the line – at least at first. The Dapol Doctor Who line was plagued with problems, both with the figures themselves and with their production. The line weathered the cancellation of the series, constant manufacturing delays, and a factory-destroying fire before Dapol lost the Doctor Who license in 2002. Their figures are badly painted, oddly articulated, and sometimes questionably accurate to the series. But like Kenner’s Star Wars line or Mego’s Star Trek , collectors look back fondly on the Dapol era, a time when there was thrill to just having a figure of a character like the Third Doctor or the Master. Today, the Dapol line may have been surpassed by Character Options’ still-running 5” Doctor Who line, but Dapol’s figures are still collected passionately by long-time Doctor Who fans, evidenced best by the nearly four-digit asking price for this 1987 TARDIS set .

Film and television tie-in toylines were largely avoided by manufacturers up until the 1970s . While this isn’t to say they didn’t happen, they usually consisted of low-cost, low-risk items, and not the kinds of elaborate action figure ranges we’ve come to expect. Doctor Who was no exception: while the series was immediately popular with children, British toy companies capitalized modestly. In the 1960s, Louis Marx manufactured several Dalek toys , but no accompanying Doctor or companions. The Marx Dalek was ubiquitous on U.K. shelves for years, and actually ended up in the series on two occasions. For the serials Evil of the Daleks and Planet of the Daleks , scores of the Louis Marx figures were put in the background of shots to make Dalek armies look larger. The Marx Dalek was the only figural Doctor Who toy produced for nearly fifteen years, with Denys Fisher providing the first-ever articulated action figure of the Doctor himself in 1978 .&nbsp;

The Doctor was based on Tom Baker’s iconic incarnation of the Time Lord, complete with his signature scarf. He was accompanied by then-current companions Leela and robot dog K-9 , still fan-favorites fifty years on. The assortment of “villain” characters included the perennial Dalek, a Cyberman, and the K-1 Robot from Baker’s inaugural episode, the creatively titled “Robot.” Boxed or loose, the Denys Fisher figures and TARDIS are incredibly rare and collectible, with the Dalek and companion Leela valued at up to $1k mint in box. The Denys-Fisher TARDIS operated under the same principle as Mego’s Star Trek transporter playset, making figures “disappear” inside with the push of a button. Around the same time, Palitoy manufactured a talking Dalek and talking K-9. K-9 is the rarer of the two, and is a surprisingly detailed and screen-accurate item for its era. Despite arriving at the show’s arguable ratings peak (as well as its high-water mark creatively, if you ask many fans), neither the Palitoy nor the Denys-Fisher range were especially popular.

Dapol inherited the license at a ratings and creative nadir. Their line came to market in 1988, just in time for the series 25th Anniversary. In celebration, Dapol incorporated not only the series’ current Doctor (Sylvester McCoy), companion (Bonnie Langford’s Mel), and adversaries, but an assortment from across its history. By 1988, the series was running on fumes, having narrowly evaded cancellation in 1985. The first wave consisted of the 7th Doctor, Mel, K-9, the bat-like alien Tetrap, and four different Daleks. Dapol’s Daleks would be the longest-running and most expansive aspect of the line, with some two dozen Dalek variations produced in the fourteen years they held the Doctor Who license. The second wave arrived in 1989, just before the cancellation of the original series. It included the 4th Doctor (sans-scarf, a complaint of collectors for thirty-seven years and counting), companion Ace, a new Cyberman, and first-ever figures of Dalek creator Davros and the 2nd and 3rd Doctor foes the Ice Warriors. Bizarrely, the Ice Warrior came with an accessory reused from Hasbro’s G.I. Joe line.&nbsp;

Two subsequent waves of new figures followed, bringing fans the 3rd Doctor (Jon Pertwee) and Roger Delgado’s incarnation of the Master, the “Melkur” statue from The Keeper of Traken , and four more aliens: a Time Lord, a Silurian, a Sea Devil, and a Sontaran. The alien sculpts are regarded as Dapol’s best work, detailed and fairly screen-accurate. Their human (and Time Lord) characters were criticized for soft likenesses and poor paint applications. Plastic quality and articulation were concerns across the line, the figures were brittle and prone to breakage. The line was rounded out by a TARDIS playset, at first just capturing the “police box” exterior of the vehicle, later resculpted to fold-out into the TARDIS’s interior, complete with plastic console.

While the fourth wave marked the end of the articulated line, a few unarticulated figures were produced in roughly the same scale as the articulated figures. These consisted of the 2nd Doctor (Patrick Troughton) and a Troughton-era Cyberman as seen in episodes The Moonbase and Tomb of the Cybermen . A planned “Whomobile” vehicle for the 3rd Doctor was cancelled following a devastating 1994 fire at their primary factory, this also ended production for most of the figures. Prototypes for a 1st Doctor figure and a Yeti (from the 2nd Doctor serial The Web of Fear ) were also cancelled; numerous rumored additional companions and aliens also failed to materialize.&nbsp; Dapol continued to sell a limited number of characters (mostly surviving product from before the fire) and new Daleks up until 2002, when they lost the license. Character Options partnered with the BBC to take over the dormant license ahead of Doctor Who ’s 2005 return to screens, and still produce Doctor Who figures to this day.&nbsp;

Character Options lived up to their name, releasing extensive product support for the 2005 season and the years after. For the first five seasons of the modern series, fans could expect that virtually every monster, alien, or adversary they saw on-screen would have an accompanying action figure; as well as a generous helping of companions, guest stars, and variants of the Doctor. In 2008, they expanded the range to include characters from the original series, and became the first-ever line to issue figures of every canon Doctor up to that point (the exception, of course, being Peter Cushing's Doctor from the 1960s theatrical films Dr. Who & The Daleks and Daleks Invasion Earth: 2150 A.D) . As Doctor Who ’s popularity has ebbed and flowed, the quantity of releases coming from Character Options has changed accordingly, but that same dedicated collector base that supported the Dapol line have kept the Character Options range running. This year, Character Options has finally issued the Yeti as a build-a-figure, making good on the promise of both Dapol’s prototype and their own long-delayed figure of the villain.

Black Widow made her debut in Tales of Suspense #52 (1964) by Stan Lee, Don Rico, and Don Heck. Her name completely ties into her role as a femme fatale, as a "black widow" is defined as a woman who murders one or more husbands or romantic partners. At the start, Natasha Romanova was a non-costumed Soviet spy sent with partner Boris to infiltrate Stark Industries and kill Russian defector Anton Vanko. Stark was her original target, but she soon became the love interest of Hawkeye.

Hawkeye and Black Widow both ended up becoming heroes, and she joined the Avengers in Avengers #111 (1973) after working with them as an ally for a few years. She slipped up sometimes, including when she was brainwashed and brought in the Swordsman and Power Man to attack the Avengers in Avengers #29 (1966). She also became Daedevil's love interest for a time.

Madame Masque debuted as Big M , a Maggia boss, in Tales of Suspense #97 (1967). Her name was revealed to be Whitney Frost in issue #98, and she became Madame Masque in Iron Man #17 (1969). She was born as Giulietta Nefaria, daughter of the criminal Count Nefaria, and raised as Whitney Frost. She took over her father's Maggia family after he was jailed. Her face was scarred in a plane crash after a failed raid on Stark Industries, and the criminal Mordecai Midas gave her the golden mask. The crash explains her hatred of Tony Stark.

She got close to Tony Stark by posing as his secretary, Krissy Longfellow, and the two became lovers. Eventually, Tony learned who she was, and she learned he was Iron Man. The affair ended in Iron Man #116 when Count Nefaria was nearly killed fighting Iron Man, and Whitney sided with her father. She has no superpowers, and one of her most powerful weapons is her charm and ability to manipulate the men around her.

Poison Ivy made her debut in Batman #181 (1966) by Robert Kanigher and Sheldon Moldoff. From her introduction, she was created to be a femme fatale. In her first appearance, she was attracted to Batman and brewed love potions to ensnare him. She eventually became a more complex character in the 1980s when her origin was rewritten in Secret Origins #36 (1988) to make her Dr. Pamela Isley, a botanist, who was injected with poisons and toxins by her mentor Jason Woodrue (the Floronic Man).

In the same story of her new origin, "Pavane," she said she moved to Gotham City to get Batman's attention. In "Hothouse" (from Batman: Legends of the Dark Knight #42-43 (1993)), she kissed Batman and tried to unmask him. In "Hush," she used pheromones to hypnotize Superman, putting DC's strongest hero under her control. This carried over to the movies, where she also used pheromones in Batman & Robin .

Felicia Hardy debuted in The Amazing Spider-Man #194 (1979) by Marv Wolfman and Dave Cockrum, and became Marvel's version of the Black Cat. While Catwoman became a femme fatale for Batman, Black Cat was the same for Spider-Man . She is the daughter of cat burglar Walter Hardy, whom she broke out of custody so he could die at home, despite Spider-Man trying to stop her. Since her introduction, her relationship with Spider-Man has defined her career, as she tries to do better for him.

In Peter Parker, the Spectacular Spider-Man #87 (1983) by Bill Mantlo and Al Milgrom, Peter took his mask off for her, and she begged him to put it back on to keep the mystery. Black Cat accepted bad-luck superpowers from a secret benefactor who turned out to be the Kingpin. Spider-Man ended the relationship when the powers endangered him, and she refused to reveal their source. She has since become more of a hero than a villain, although she still has her moments of misdeeds.

The most famous femme fatale in comic book history was also its first. Catwoman debuted in Batman #1 (1940) by Bill Finger and Bob Kane. At the time, she was known as the Cat, a jewel thief who disguised herself as an older woman. The big thing to note about this was that Batman seemed to deliberately allow her to escape rather than bring her in. Catwoman was based on classic femme fatale movie actresses like Jean Harlow and Hedy Lamarr.

She became very popular thanks to the Batman television series from the 1960s, where she was played by three different actresses. In "Hush," she and Batman became lovers, and he revealed his identity, but he ended it after suspecting Hush and the Riddler had manipulated them. She and Batman almost got married as well, but she was the one who broke off the wedding, saying it would kill the drive that made him Batman.
