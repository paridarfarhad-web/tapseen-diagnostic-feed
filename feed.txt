The action star was a phenomenon that rose up in the 1970s, and it took years for the concept of the “female action star” to seem anything more than a counterintuitive anomaly. Never a minimalist onscreen, Daniel Day-Lewis has nonetheless cultivated a lofty kind of stardom by making himself very scarce. Today, film noir might be the most popular genre of classic Hollywood, because it’s the one that seems the most “modern.” Yet it might also be the most misunderstood. “Out of the Past,” the movie that gave Robert Mitchum his greatest role, is a tangled tale of crime and fate and femme fatale treachery, yet at its heart is the portrayal of a troubled relationship that is very much a twisted kind of love . Mitchum uses his sleepy eyes and sleek cheekbones to suggest an inexorable longing and the things it will drive a man to do. A real-life renegade, Mitchum always worked the edge, and that’s what made him so captivating: the freedom with which his characters commune with the dark side. In “The Night of the Hunter,” he's an evil preacher pursuing two children (to find the money they’re hiding) in a way that’s so sinister, the film feels almost Lynchian. Gene Kelly was, undeniably, an artist of the highest caliber, but he might recoil at the idea that dancing on film was high art. A trained dancer and gifted choreographer, Kelly took as his career-long project making dance legible and accessible to the masses. Post-World War II audiences seeking a lift swooned for the “dream ballet” Kelly danced with Leslie Caron in “An American in Paris.” And in the title dance sequence of “Singin’ in the Rain,” Kelly’s own kinetic movement speaks more eloquently than do the song’s lyrics; leaping on lampposts and tapping through puddles, Kelly may be the standard-bearer for the surprisingly challenging feat of conveying joy on film. Today, those films are a reminder of the artistry of the studio era (to say nothing of the surprising sex appeal that managed to make it into movies despite the Hays Code). But in their moment, as America staggered through the Great Depression, Astaire and Rogers were something more. They projected not merely athleticism but glamour and class — an escapist tonic for a dark historical moment. Together, the pair were fluid and joyful, eschewing the grandeur-for-grandeur’s-sake spectacles of Busby Berkeley musicals in favor of the simple elegance of two bodies moving in complement. Both performers had triumphs on their own (he in films from “Funny Face” to “The Towering Inferno,” she in her Oscar-winning role in “Kitty Foyle”), but it was their ability, together, to tell stories through movement that we remember today. — D.D.

Between her activism, her workouts, her marriages and her famous family, one could certainly argue that Jane Fonda’s renown comes largely from outside acting. She can elevate sci-fi schlock like “Barbarella,” dial up the tension in a textbook paranoia thriller like “Klute” and, devastatingly, remind us all that beauty and humor are not mutually exclusive in comedies like “9 to 5.” The same steeliness that’s led Fonda to take principled stands in her actual life is always evident in her characters. Fonda may be funny, vulnerable or sad, but there’s never any doubt that she’s fully in possession of herself. — A.H.

Her exit from the industry for a different kind of public life, and her tragic death at 52, have burnished the sense of Kelly as ethereal — a star whose soft glow warmed each of her films. — D.D.

So much of what we think of as contemporary stardom was effectively invented by Elizabeth Taylor. The pairing of celebrity with charitable work? Taylor was an early, dogged advocate for AIDS research, lending a globally known name to the cause. The concept of an actress going “deglam” for the right part? She of the violet eyes and breathless voice transformed herself for first-time film director Mike Nichols — and “Who’s Afraid of Virginia Woolf?” remains a landmark of film acting. And the idea of a celebrity as someone who is, in her glamour, beauty and appetites, simply grander than us mortals? Taylor’s films attest to her gifts, but her tabloid legend was forged on the turbulent set of “Cleopatra” and on the beaches of Portofino and at the altar (eight times). Still, Taylor treated her stardom with wit and self-deprecation, even as she dramatized her way through a very public life. — D.D.
