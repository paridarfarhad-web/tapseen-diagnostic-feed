Back in 2001 and 2002, developer Volition — the studio that would go on to create Saints Row and the studio that no longer exists as of 2023 — combined with publisher THQ to release Red Faction and Red Faction 2 . Both games ended up being multi-platform, but were notably PS2 exclusives at launch. In the second half of the 2010s, the pair of sci-fi first-person shooters were re-released on the PlayStation Store with new features like trophy support and widescreen resolutions, but they are still the original PS2 versions of the games.

In 2009, a third game was released, Red Faction: Guerrilla , but the original version of this game doesn't exist on the PlayStation Store; only the 2018 remaster which costs $30 and isn't on sale. Then there was a fourth game in 2011, Red Faction: Armageddon , which is also the final game in the series, and it's not available on the PlayStation Store in any capacity.

Two Great PS2 First-Person Shooters The first Red Faction game received an 88 on Metacritic and was notably one of the best games of its year, hence why it spawned a four-game franchise, complete with a spin-off as well. The sequel wasn't received quite as well, but it still earned a very solid score of 84.

Do the pair of first-person shooters hold up? Yeah, they do, especially the first game. The first game stood out back in the early 2000s for its proprietary Geo-Mod destruction system that gave the game destructible environments and open-ended gameplay long before these things became common. And for a PS2 first-person shooter, its gunplay still holds up pretty well. By comparison, Red Faction II is a far more traditional and linear first-person shooter from this era. It has less uniqueness, but overall it still feels viable to play in 2026. If you don't have nostalgia for this era of gaming, though, it could be difficult to travel two decades back in time and experience these in their dated glory.

For those who end up checking out the pair of PS2 games, the first game is about 6 to 9 hours long, while the second is about 4 to 6 hours long. This may seem short, but it was standard for first-person shooter campaigns at the time. All three issues are highly prized by comic book collectors, and Showcase #34 can be worth a small fortune in near-mint condition like this copy up for auction through Heritage .

In the sixty years since, the Atom and Ant-Man alike have distinguished themselves as members of their respective universe's super-hero teams; Ant-Man is a charter member of the Avengers, the Atom became the second new addition to the Justice League of America in issue #14. Their solo adventures are less publicized, though both enjoyed years-running Silver Age features: Ant-Man in Tales to Astonish, the Atom in his own comic (eventually shared with Hawkman). While similar in concept, the differences in the worlds and storytelling of the Marvel and DC Universes have set the Atom and Ant-Man apart over the decades. However, there remains one startling similarity: the Atom and Ant-Man's first appearances are two of the absolute rarest comics of their era. Just fifteen copies of Tales to Astonish #27 exist in a CGC-graded 9.0 or better; and just eighteen copies of Showcase #34 in the same condition. Beginning in 1956 with the Flash, DC Comics editor Julius Schwartz worked with the company's writers and artists to revive their dormant Golden Age super-heroes, reinterpreting them for the post-World War II Atomic Age. The Golden Age "mystery men" heroes were seen as old-fashioned, and there was an interest in grounding these revivals in contemporary science and contemporary science fiction. Green Lantern's power-ring became the symbol of an intergalactic order of peace-keepers; the Flash's super-speed enabled him to travel time; and "the Atom," formerly earning the name with his diminutive stature, would become a size-changing scientist who had harnessed the energy of a white dwarf star. Ray Palmer was a scientist at Ivy University, a fictious New England university alternately (to the dismay of students of either) based on Harvard or Yale, depending on the writer. Palmer discovers a mass of energy from a collapsed white dwarf star, and invents a lens with it that enables him to shrink objects — though with massive molecular instability that causes them to explode after a short duration of time.

After being trapped in a cave with his girlfriend, Jean Loring, and his students, Palmer uses the experimental, unstable lens on himself to shrink in a last-ditch effort to save their lives. He is able to successfully free them from the cave, as well as return to normal size: the molecular instability somehow doesn't affect his body chemistry. Naturally, Palmer sees no other choice than to become a super-hero, adopting the name "the Atom." He would appear in three issues of DC's Showcase try-out comic (which earlier spawned the Silver Age Flash and Green Lantern) before spinning off into his own comic, The Atom , in April of 1962. Three months later, he joined the Justice League, remaining a regular member of the team until #220, when he had a mid-life crisis following the discovery Jean (now his wife) was having an affair. The Silver Age Atom solo comic is best remembered for its art by Gil Kane, who, along with writer Gardner Fox, worked on its first thirty-seven issues. Though less iconic than many of his Justice League teammates, the Atom's constant presence in the DC Universe has left an indelible impression on generations of readers.
