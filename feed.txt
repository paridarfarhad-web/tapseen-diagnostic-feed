Television in the 20th century was looked at as lesser than both film and the stage, and for a long time it was the home of fluff. However, as the years went by, more and more TV shows got better, more complex, more engrossing than ever before. The '90s had shows like X-Files, Oz, Star Trek: Deep Space Nine , while future classics like The Sopranos, Sex in the City , and Six Feet Under began. These shows formed the basis of what has been called the Golden Age of Television, which began in the '00s. There are some brilliant shows from the first decade of the 2000s, but there's one show deserves the title of best TV show of the '00s: Battlestar Galactica .

The original Battlestar Galactica was network TV's answer to Star Wars , a show that definitely looked and felt like it was from the late '70s in the worst ways. While it had its fans, it just never found a big enough audience for how much it cost to make and would become one of many lost sci-fi series. However, Ronald D. Moore, one of Star Trek 's best writers, and David Eick would end up bringing the show back on the then-Sci-Fi Channel in 2004, starting with a four-part miniseries. Battlestar Galactica isn't a perfect show – some fans find its last season disappointing – but it really is the best show of the decade, beating out numerous amazing shows for the title.

The early '00s were a great time to be watching TV. HBO Sundays were becoming the home of the best TV shows around, the networks were putting out shows like 24 and Lost , and Adult Swim on Cartoon Network was making adult animation into a force to be reckoned with. The Sopranos was the 500-pound gorilla in the room, but it felt like there was something amazing on TV every night. The '90s had been the decade of cable, with many new channels popping up, as well, broadening the mixture of shows.

The Sci-Fi Channel launched in 1992, playing reruns of old sci-fi and genre shows. Eventually, they started to make their own original series and Moore and Eick pitched the network the reboot. Sci-Fi wanted the show and casting would begin in Canada. The show was meant to be the focus of a new programming slate, using a recognizable old name to get fans old and new through the door.

The original Battlestar Galactica saw the titular ship escape the destruction of the Colonies at the hands of Cylons, leading a fleet of civilians ships into the stars to find the original home of humanity – Earth. The new series would copy this idea, using many of the same names of characters, ships, and places, but changed everything else. The original Battlestar Galactica was a Star Trek/Star Wars style sci-fi universe, but the BSG reboot would take place in a more grounded universe. Gone were the weird alien races, the lasers, and the bizarre clothes. In their place were the Greek Gods, a whole lot of ammunition, and fashion that looked like it stepped out of 2004 Vancouver (because it did).

Moore and Eick wanted to use the series to talk about the then-current War on Terror. The Cylons were the creations of humanity who had rebelled. The first war almost destroyed humanity, but the Cylons retreated out of nowhere leading to decades of detente. The miniseries takes a page from the 9/11 playbook, as the Cylons launch a massive sneak attack on the Colonies of Man, slaughtering billions, with only the Galactica and a fleet of civilian ships escaping. The ship rearmed and then set course past the Red Line – the edge of known space – as they searched for the homeworld of mankind, Earth.

The show starred Edward James Olmos, Katee Sackhoff, Mary McDonnell, Jamie Bamber, Michael Hogan, James Callis (Baltar is the best), Tricia Helfer, and numerous others. The original series was the brainchild of Glenn Larson and it was very Mormon. Moore and Eick went in a completely direction, with the Colonials being polytheistic worshipers of the Lords of Kobol, our Greek Gods, while the machine Cylons were monotheistic, worshiping the one "true" god. This focus on religion and culture was different from any other sci-fi show out there, giving it a unique flavor.

The show was military sci-fi with a religious edge. Back in the early '00s, the right was selling the War on Terror as a clash of civilizations and Moore and Eick took the show in that direction, while also showing how foolish that was by the end of the story. The characters were able to inhabit their roles perfectly, and as the episodes dug into them, their past, and the trauma of the destruction of mankind, we watched them change and grow. It felt like nearly every character had an arc of some kind; they all started in one place and ended up in a complete other (in more ways than one).

Of course, there are some complaints that the show can't duck. To begin with, Moore and Eick had no plan on how to end the series and when you rewatch it, it shows. The last season is an interesting beast because it does tie up all the plot threads, but the way it did it made a lot of fans angry. Without getting too into the weeds of it all, the show went to places that sci-fi fans, one of the atheist/agnostic fandoms out there, didn't really like. The final episode is one you either love completely or don't like at all (I personally love it and have watched it more than any other finale, but I get why other people don't like it). However, I think the flaws of the show make it what it is. It's one of the best rides in TV history, and was able to be more interesting than anything else in the decade.

The '00s had three of the best TV shows of all-time – The Sopranos, Mad Men, and Breaking Bad . The shows all had the same throughline – a badly behaving patriarch – and gave viewers some of the best performances imaginable. You can point to all of them as nearly flawless examples of everything that TV should be. However, I think that even though it definitely had its flaws, Battlestar Galactica was better than all of three of them.

To begin with, it became appointment TV in a way those shows didn't. Friends would get together to watch it every week. The fandom was so engaged by the show, helped along by Moore and Eick using the Internet to communicate more with them. It honestly felt like a true fan community

The show had a lot to say about the moment in time that was the '00s, letting us take a step back from the goings on of the day and see it from another perspective. It had characters that you fell in love with; sure, those other shows all had great characters, but could you love them? Most of them were monsters of some kind. The characters of BSG were flawed, stressed out, traumatized, and exhausted, but they were good people in a terrible situation. TV reboots can be all over the place , but Battlestar Galactica was able to create an amazing narrative peopled by brilliant characters in a way that no other show of the decade could.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google Mego’s Star Trek line is synonymous with its iconic Enterprise Bridge playset . Less discussed is the even more elaborate, impressive playset that Mego released as a follow-up in 1976, finally giving kids a place to send the figures they put through the Enterprise’s transporter: the “Mission to Gamma VI.” Standing at over eighteen inches tall, the playset gave kids an exotic alien world for the Enterprise crew to explore. Gamma VI was a fully-rendered alien ecosystem, with the set’s accessories and action features representing three different species that inhabited the strange new world. It’s these bizarre action features that serve as the set’s claim to fame today: while Mego took some creative liberties with its Enterprise, they took far more with the “Mission to Gamma VI.” Gamma VI was virtually an original creation, its central stone-faced idol bearing a cursory resemblance to the one seen in season two episode “The Apple.”

There’s another critical difference between the Mego Enterprise Bridge and the “Mission to Gamma VI:” rarity. While the Enterprise was the top-selling toy of 1975, the “Mission to Gamma VI” scarcely reached retail shelves – owing in part to the box’s massive footprint – making it the single rarest and most sought-after release in Mego’s Star Trek line. Playsets are one of the challenges of Mego’s eight-inch action figures, demonstrated in sets like “Mission to Gamma VI” and the World’s Greatest Super-Heroes Wayne Foundation . To scale with Mego figures, these sets had to tower, making a significant investment of space for retailers and parents alike. Loose, complete samples of the set sell for up to $1k, with many of its action-features and accessories easily lost, damaged, or ruined by play. Boxed samples can sell for more than double that. The only sample currently on eBay sits at a buy-it-now asking price of $2.5k , owing to its incredibly rare sealed-in-box condition.

Beaming down to Gamma VI, Mego’s Enterprise crew would be met with a rocky, desolate planet surface represented through a cardboard baseplate. The only sign of life is a plastic plant, whose articulated leaves could be used to trap the unwary crewman who steps too close. The dominant feature of Gamma VI’s landscape, however, was the imposing stone face carved into a jagged cliffside. This is the aspect of the playset that bears the closest resemblance to the source material, seemingly modeled loosely off of the “Vaal” statue from the aforementioned episode “The Apple.” Given Mego’s penchant for loose adaptations of its Star Trek aliens , it feels reasonable to call Vaal the basis for “Mission to Gamma VI” – especially given that “Gamma Trianguli VI” is the name of the planet in “The Apple.”

The lizard-like face looms over a stepped platform, looking almost like a sacrificial altar. Looking closer, one would expect to find a door in the carved stone wall. Instead, kids can push the platform and release a trap door, sending their Captain Kirk or Mister Spock tumbling into the temple’s interior. From there, they can explore the secrets of Gamma VI’s temple, less detailed but just as fun as the exterior. The inside is functionally a cave, given two levels of play by a cardboard structure. More play features round out the exterior, including a movable jaw and glow-in-the-dark eyes on the “Vaal” stone face; as well as two other native alien species. A textured green glove fits through a hole in the playset, letting kids become what the box calls the “Cave Glove Creature,” pictured there snatching an unsuspecting Spock. Additionally, the set included four weird, two-inch tall aliens, sculpted to cling to jutting spikes along the plastic wall. These don’t have any basis in the series, and the difference in scale makes it a bit difficult to have them interact with other figures.

It’s been said that Star Trek toys have almost never really been for kids: whether it was Galoob’s 1987 Star Trek: The Next Generation line, or Playmates’ epic 1990s Star Trek figures, the primary target audience was adult collectors. Catering to these adult collectors, these lines ensured their figures, characters, and accessories were coming straight from the source material. Playmates’ line especially, with its mail-away figures, ToyFare magazine exclusives, and limited-edition chases, were designed to engage grown-up Trekkies with the time and budget to obsess over the line. Mego’s line came at a completely different point in toy history, inspired directly by the overwhelming popularity of early-seventies Star Trek reruns with children. While Playmates or Galoob’s figures were collectors’ items as much as toys, Mego’s garish, pastel-colored Star Trek was firmly intended for kids.

Mego’s Star Trek line came at a time when the first and only consideration for toys was whether they would appeal to kids. Mego wasn’t beholden to screen accuracy, they were beholden to playaiblity. Designing their “Mission to Gamma VI,” the first and only question was if it would hold the interest of a child. As illustrated by the previous section, it absolutely woud: Mego designed its play features to gradually reveal themselves, playing out in a structure like an episode of Star Trek . A kid sitting down with the set and their Enterprise crew was exploring an alien planet with them, and imagining in real-time how their heroes would respond to the sudden dangers and developments posed by Gamma VI’s flora and fauna. Though it barely resembled the episode of Star Trek that it was based on, the “Mission to Gamma VI” embodied the spirit of the series perfectly, encouraging exploration, discovery, and a sense of adventure.

Amazing Fantasy itself has an interesting history, having started in 1961 as Amazing Adventures , but by issue #7 it had become Amazing Adult Fantas y and was an anthology book geared towards adults with short, science fiction stories and a tagline of “the magazine that respects your intelligence.” While the idea was interesting — a book tailored to the adult reader — it didn’t do well. Sales were poor and then-publisher Martin Goodman made the call to cancel the title with issue #15. Of course, Amazing Adult Fantasy coming to an end presented a different opportunity that would end up changing Marvel forever.

At the same time Amazing Adult Fantasy was faltering, as the story goes Lee was looking to introduce a completely different type of superhero, one that would appeal to a younger audience. At this point in comics, all of the heroes were adults and any teen characters were sidekicks. The character envisioned for this would be different, a regular teen with actual real-world issues and challenges: an everyday hero. It wasn’t an idea that got a lot of traction with the powers that be, but with Amazing Adult Fantasy dying, there was an opportunity. The final issue was rebranded as Amazing Fantasy , Spider-Man got the cover and became the central hero, Ditko worked his artistic magic, and that was that. If it failed, at least they tried.

Amazing Fantasy was indeed properly cancelled, but when sales figures rolled in what had been a dying book turned out to be a huge hit. The book was one of the year’s best sellers and immediately, Spider-Man got his own book , The Amazing Spider-Man #1 and it’s a book that continues even today. Spider-Man himself has gone on to be one of the most famous and beloved characters in all of comics and is easily Marvel’s biggest and most popular. Granted, Peter Parker/Spider-Man has been through a number of shifts and changes over the years and he’s not exactly the teen hero anymore, but he is still very much the everyday hero. How often do we as people find ourselves with our backs to the wall, in situations where all hope seems lost and we are down to trying just one more thing and if we fail, well, at least we tried? The fact that one of our greatest fictional characters has that as his publication origin story makes him even more endearing.

Wild how something that almost didn’t happen ended up changing everything, but boy are we glad it did.
