One thing that a lot of the best fantasy movies from the '80s share is that they likely traumatized thousands of children everywhere. There's just something about the fantasy genre that brings with it a fair bit of trauma and we mean that in a good way. Many fantasy films tend to feature elements that will stick with kids for a long time, even into adulthood, because they're haunting, a little creepy, and often include a memorable moment like a death or something along those lines. One of these '80s classics likely haunted more kids than any other and remains required viewing.

When it comes to weird fantasy stories, not many names are more associated with that concept than Jim Henson. His wacky concepts and sometimes unsettling looking puppets became a staple of the genre over the years. One of the best showcases of this is 1986's Labyrinth , which was directed by Henson and follows a teen girl as she enters the titular labyrinth to rescue her younger brother from the Goblin King.

The premise feels familiar, though it was relatively fresh when Labyrinth came out. The things that make this film truly great are Henson's surreal world-building and practical puppetry. They are the kind of aspects that age remarkably well and are still great to see today. Labyrinth also features an incredibly charismatic performance from the iconic David Bowie as Jareth. Reviews were mixed upon release, but the movie has gained more acclaim as time goes on, becoming a cult classic and something that everyone needs to see at least once.

It's important not to look past animation when it comes to the best fantasy films. Animation shouldn't be overlooked for any genre, but especially fantasy. They just work so perfectly together, which is evidenced by The Secret of NIMH , as the movie manages to craft a beautiful and unique world through its animation style. Based on the novel Mrs. Frisby and the Rats of NIMH, the story centers on a widowed mouse who moves her family to escape a farmer's plow.

We watch that harrowing journey as she faces challenge after challenge in this mythical world of animals, all while trying to keep her kids safe. The 1982 movie comes from animation legend Don Bluth and tells one of those timeless stories that anyone from any era can relate to and enjoy. Critics appreciated this, and though the box office numbers were disappointing, The Secret of NIMH found new life on home video.

As noted, among these required fantasy film viewings is one movie that could be considered the most traumatizing of the '80s. That is The Neverending Story , which is based on the first half of a 1979 novel of the same name. It's a wonderful adventure that captured the imagination of children everywhere, as they saw themselves in Bastian Balthazar Bux, a boy who discovers a magical book that tells the story of a young warrior who must stop a dark force from taking over the world of Fantasia.

Kids also may have seen themselves in Atreyu, the warrior at the heart of the story. Either way, they were locked into this 1984 classic. The film features the tragic death of Atreyu's trusty and beloved horse, Artax, in the Swamps of Sadness, which is a scene that scarred viewers everywhere. The haunting moment is probably the film's most memorable, but the overall project is a legitimate classic.

Jim Henson is so crucial to the legacy of the fantasy genre that he has two '80s flicks that are required viewing. A couple of years before Labyrinth , he and fellow iconic puppeteer Frank Oz came together for The Dark Crystal in 1982. This one featured even more puppets than Labyrinth and is set on a fictional planet. The story follows two Gelflings who embark on a quest to restore balance to their homeworld and overthrow an evil ruler.

Again, that's a story that has been told time and time again, yet the world of The Dark Crystal is just incredible to experience. To craft an entire world completely populated by puppets and featuring no humans is astonishing. The fact that the story works so well on top of those elements is what makes The Dark Crystal a true classic . It's another case of reception being stronger as time goes on and this even spawned an Emmy-winning modern prequel series.

The Princess Bride is basically everything you could want in a fantasy story. It starts innocently enough as a grandfather reads an epic novel to his grandson before bed. The story from the book is the events of The Princess Bride , which sees a swashbuckling farmhand team up with companions he meets along the way to rescue a princess from an evil prince. While that sounds like a story we've all seen a ton of times, it's the best iteration of that tale.

The 1987 film from Rob Reiner features exciting swordfights, a touching romance, fantastical characters, and a sharp script. The end result is a glorious fantasy tale filled with action, comedy, romance, and more. The Princess Bride holds an outstanding 93% rating on Rotten Tomatoes and is widely considered one of the best fantasy films ever made and one of the best movies from the '80s, regardless of genre. It has withstood the test of time and is a must-see for any fan of cinema.

Mattel's unique release strategy for M.U.S.C.L.E . made it easier for kids and challenging for completionist adults to collect. The figurines were primarily sold in completely randomized packages of four or ten, as well as larger (loosely) themed twenty-eight packs. The four-packs retailed for a lower cost than the average action figure in 1985 or '86, making it easier for a kid to amass an impressive collection of M.U.S.C.L.E . figures on a budget of spare change and their weekly allowance. Owing to their low price point, the figures are plentiful, seeming to make their way into virtually every toy collection of the era, although the first law of toy collecting dictates that as a collector edges closer to having them all, they will inevitably never come across those last five or ten missing characters. That's why auctions like this upcoming lot at Heritage advertise and emphasize having key characters like &#8220;the Claw,&#8221; a giant disembodied hand and a cult favorite of M.U.S.C.L.E . collectors, included. Given the aforementioned random release strategy, coming across the exact fan favorite character mint-on-card is exceptionally rare.

Kinnikuman was created in 1979 by Yoshinori Nakai and Takashi Shimada, and was designed by the pair as a loose parody of Ultraman . The plot followed the masked wrestler Kinnikuman (translated roughly as &#8220;Muscleman&#8221;), deposed prince of the planet Kinniku, as he competed against the wrestlers of the wicked Chojin in a quest to prove himself worthy of Kinniku's throne. Terri-Bull / Buffaloman was one of many villains Kinnikuman faced in the ring, and would become an ally to Kinnikuman after being defeated in combat. Such was also the case for Ramenman , Ashuraman , Robin Mask , and numerous other characters who appeared in the Kinkeshi (and subsequently M.U.S.C.L.E .) toylines. The Japanese name for the line, &#8220;kinkeshi,&#8221; is a portmanteau of Kinnikuman and &#8220;keshi,&#8221; a type of soft rubber, eraser-like figurine popular in Japan. Though a popular toy in Japan, keshi figures lack the same cache in the American market, meaning that when M.U.S.C.L.E . debuted, there was not another toyline like it. Its scale, designs, and open-ended world were unlike virtually anything else on the market.

It's not unusual for an American toy company to take an existing product from Japan and rebrand it for Western audiences. Most famously, this was the origin story of the Transformers , but this practice also spawned lines like Mego's Micronauts, Mattel's Shogun Warriors , and even Tonka's Go-Bots . Where M.U.S.C.L.E . varies is that it eschewed the tie-in comic book or animated series that was used by these other properties to help familiarize kids with the line and its characters. M.U.S.C.L.E , for better or for worse, was a blank slate. Mattel's marketing materials give a little background, specifying that &#8220;Muscleman and his heroic Tag Team, the Thug Busters, are ready for all challengers. Two additional twenty-eight packs, the &#8220;Mighty Maulers&#8221; and the &#8220;Cosmic Showdown&#8221; were also available.

These twenty-eight packs were less popular than the modestly-priced four-packs, and are difficult to locate on the secondary market. More common are the ten-packs, which were sold in plastic garbage cans; a fair number of kids held onto these and used them to store their M.U.S.C.L.E . collections. Additional expressions for the line enhanced the wrestling theme, with a &#8220;Hard Knockin' Wrestling Ring&#8221; playset, a wearable &#8220;Battlin' Belt&#8221; that could house a small number of M.U.S.C.L.E . figures, and the &#8220;Mega Match&#8221; game, which created turn-based gameplay for wrestling matches between the figurines. The &#8220;Hard Knockin' Wrestling Ring&#8221; had play mechanics similar to Rock &#8216;Em, Sock &#8216;Em Robots : the players clipped one figure each into a plastic stand controlled by a joystick, enabling them to &#8220;fight&#8221; until one figure was knocked loose from the plastic clip. The playset and game are relatively rare, and command triple-digits price-points mint in box.

A promotional mail-away from Mattel threw down the gauntlet for collectors: a poster featured all two hundred and thirty-three M.U.S.C.L.E . figures, emblazoned with the rhetorical question of &#8220;Can you collect them all?&#8221; As mentioned in the introduction, the randomized nature of M.U.S.C.L.E &#8216;s distribution meant that the answer to this was &#8220;no&#8221; for all but the most diligent, obsessive fan &#8212; and that doesn't become much easier for them, even as an adult. The lack of agreed upon names adds another degree of difficulty, as seeking out individual characters on auction sites is hampered by trying to figure out which name the seller might have the character listed under. Some American M.U.S.C.L.E . collectors have taken to using the Japanese names, while other stick with the informal nicknames adopted by the American collecting community. As an example, &#8220;the Claw&#8221; advertised by Heritage's M.U.S.C.L.E . auction listing is a collector nickname for a character known in Kinnikuman as &#8220; Sunigator Shotai .&#8221; Some eBay listings for the figure use both names, some one or the other, some use neither.

Beyond the incredibly wide character selection, Mattel introduced another challenge to assembling a complete set of M.U.S.C.L.E . figures: different colored rubber. Later waves of M.U.S.C.L.E . came in eye-catching blues, reds, oranges, and greens. Including color variants, there are more than a thousand M.U.S.C.L.E . figures, though most collectors content themselves with trying to assemble a complete set of characters. M.U.S.C.L.E . is an easy line to collect, and a tough line to complete; each of these characteristics makes it attractive to different kinds of collectors. For some, M.U.S.C.L.E . is a fun line to informally gather a handful of from conventions, flea markets, and collectible stores. They're cheap, readily available, and full of creative, strange designs like the Moai-inspired &#8220; Amoiman &#8221; or the shark-headed &#8220; Jawsman .&#8221; Patience and research might be needed to track down the line's most elusive releases, and there is nothing hardcore collectors love more than a challenge.
