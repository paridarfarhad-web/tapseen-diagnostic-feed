Avengers: Age of Ultron is a very good, highly entertaining movie that just so happens to have a lot of competition to be the best in its series of films.

The movie sits at a strong 75% on Rotten Tomatoes and did a lot of work in setting up the future of the MCU. The Infinity Stones were showcased, characters like Wanda and Vision were introduced, Steve's wielding of Mjolnir was teased, and more. However, despite all those things, Avengers: Age of Ultron is often viewed as the worst of the series, yet it does something that no other entry in the franchise has managed to pull off. Avengers: Doomsday is on the horizon and it looks like it also won't do what Avengers: Age of Ultron did so well, making that 2015 film all the more fun to look back on.

It sounds so simple, but Avengers: Age of Ultron gives us a look at the titular team actually working as a unit. Hearing that it's the only Avengers movie to truly do that sounds odd, since each film has them team up, but the way Avengers: Age of Ultron did it was different. Right from the opening scene, we're watching our favorite heroes work as a legitimate team. There is no in-fighting and they aren't only coming together because some world-ending threat like Ultron or Thanos is upon them.

The opening scene of Avengers: Age of Ultron features Captain America, Iron Man, Thor, Black Widow, Hulk, and Hawkeye fighting as a unit to retrieve Loki's scepter. It's a somewhat simple mission, yet the scene plays out like they've been working together often since the events of The Avengers . They're pulling out tandem moves like Thor slamming Mjolnir down on Cap's shield to take out a bunch of bad guys and they have a plan already set in motion for turning Hulk back into Bruce Banner through Natasha's "lullaby." The whole thing is a joy to watch.

The shot of every member flying into action feels like it was pulled straight out of a comic book and that's what longtime fans have always wanted to see. The scene also allows each hero a moment or two to shine while they work together. Natasha drives Clint around while both are shooting enemies, Cap is throwing his motorcycle at people, Iron Man is going after the scepter, Thor is just dominating foes, and Hulk is smashing. When you add in the fun banter between the team and the afterparty, this feels like the most harmonious iteration of the Avengers in the history of the MCU.

Again, it sounds odd to consider that other Avengers movies don't feature the team at their best, but it's true. In The Avengers , the characters didn't know each other, so they spent a lot of the movie at each other's throats. They do finally come together to save the day in the end, but they weren't a team in the same way that they are in Avengers: Age of Ultron . Unfortunately, the events of Captain America: Civil War prevented that team from ever truly being together again. Although Thor and Hulk were absent from that film, it shattered bonds for most other members.

One of the main reasons that Thanos was able to defeat the heroes in Avengers: Infinity War was because of how scattered they were. We see Cap, Nat, and Bruce working together, but Clint is nowhere to be found, Thor only joins his friends late in the game, and Tony is on another planet. The team never actually gets together. Avengers: Endgame sees them brought back together after Thanos' snap , but they are a much different team by that point.

Thor's confidence is shot, Clint has become more vengeful, and the rift between Cap and Iron Man was never truly settled. Also, they were all there with the stakes at their highest, while Avengers: Age of Ultron allowed them to just be a team on a regular mission. There's something simple and somewhat wholesome about that. Granted, there wasn't really room in the stories of Avengers: Infinity War and Avengers: Endgame to have missions like that, but that just makes the dynamic in Avengers: Age of Ultron more special.

The upcoming Avengers: Doomsday feels like more of the same, as the team is clearly not firing on all cylinders. Thor even says in the trailer that he doesn't know many of them and we've already seen them fighting amongst themselves, whether it's Shang-Chi against Gambit or Cyclops against Sam Wilson. The original team is also no longer around after the deaths of Tony and Nat, meaning we'll never see what Avengers: Age of Ultron gave us again.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google Superman: The Movie is considered the grandfather of superhero cinema for a reason. Even though superheroes had been around for 40 years by then, a major Hollywood feature film starring one finally hit the screen. And even until the late 1990s, DC Comics was among the only major publishers getting its characters into movie theaters. Aside from Howard the Duck and Blade , Marvel had mostly kept its characters on television screens with both live-action and animated series. So in many ways, 2000’s X-Men created the template for the modern Marvel movies and eventually the MCU would follow.

Despite the creative liberties that first movie took with the source material , fans were willing to overlook the black leather outfits, absent character backstories, and reduced screen time for characters like Cyclops. Both X-Men and especially 2003’s X2: X-Men United treated the Marvel mutants with such reverence that fans largely accepted them with open arms. Nightcrawler’s opening assault on the White House, Wolverine’s berserker rage during the attack on Xavier’s school, and hints at Jean Grey’s latent Phoenix Force gave fans just a taste of the epic moments the MCU would ultimately deliver. But 20 years ago, fans’ goodwill toward Fox’s X-Men films ran out.

The first sign of trouble came when director Bryan Singer — who helmed the first two films — opted out of returning for the then-untitled X-Men 3 . Instead of staying with the franchise, Singer went off to Warner Bros. to make Superman Returns , taking composer John Ottman, writers Dan Harris and Michael Dougherty, and actor James Marsden with him. Enter Brett Ratner, director of the Rush Hour films and the Hannibal Lecter sequel Red Dragon . That change in hands then led to a new script by Simon Kinberg and Zak Penn, one that combined the Dark Phoenix Saga teased in X2 with the “Gifted” arc centered on the creation of a mutant cure.

Despite its grand scope, X-Men: The Last Stand somehow clocked in at just 104 minutes, the same length as the first film. And while it became a box office smash overall, fans and critics were not having it. Marsden’s Cyclops was killed off-screen early in the film, Juggernaut became a joke, and the rushed story failed to do justice to either Dark Phoenix or the mutant cure. Even though the movie delivered a Danger Room sequence (finally) and gave fans Kelsey Grammer’s beloved take on Beast, the film failed to live up to its predecessors. Instead, it became just another trilogy-ender that let audiences down, breaking the series’ streak of winning over fans.

Unfortunately for the franchise, X-Men: The Last Stand was only the beginning of a series of baffling creative choices. With its core trilogy over and done with, Fox intended to shift its focus to a series of origin stories focused on individual X-Men characters. So, despite being essentially the main character of the preceding trilogy, the first one produced was X-Men Origins: Wolverine . But bad visual effects, a weak script, and an embarrassing depiction of fan-favorite Deadpool sunk the movie among fans, proving once again that Fox just didn’t get it. The X-Men Origins concept was abandoned in favor of a reboot film, X-Men: First Class , which was ultimately retconned into a prequel to the original trilogy with X-Men: Days of Future Past .

After First Class , The Wolverine , and Days of Future Past — the latter of which marked Singer’s return to the franchise — it seemed like the series was back on track. The time-travel shenanigans in Days of Future Past not only prevented a dystopian future but erased both X-Men Origins: Wolverine and X-Men: The Last Stand from the timeline. But that goodwill was once again squandered with two unsatisfying sequels, X-Men: Apocalypse and Dark Phoenix , the latter of which again tried (and failed) to bring the beloved storyline to the screen. While Logan and the Deadpool films helped salvage what was left of the Fox X-Men universe, The New Mutants certainly ended things on a sour note. Now 20 years after X-Men: The Last Stand marked the series' first wrong turn, it’s exciting to see Marsden and other key original X-Men cast members get one final moment to shine in Avengers: Doomsday .

One thread connected their stories: all three had encountered the expectations of commercial Hindi cinema before finding the conviction to make something different.

Gowariker and Bhandarkar already had films behind them before "Lagaan" and "Chandni Bar" established their identities as filmmakers. Akhtar, making his directorial debut with "Dil Chahta Hai," said he had not been interested in making a film that conformed to the prevailing Bollywood potboiler template.

For Gowariker, who is also the IFFI festival director this year, the turning point came after his first two films failed. Having initially tried to make commercial entertainers, he realized that "my next film had to be something I believe in, something that I want to make first, and then hope to God that the audience also likes it. So that's how the genesis of 'Lagaan' happened."

The idea was an unlikely proposition: a period drama built around a cricket match, with villagers taking on the British. Gowariker wanted to make a film about "a group of heroes." He researched the British Raj, 19th-century life, cricket and village communities before taking the script back to star Aamir Khan, who had initially rejected the premise. Gowariker asked Khan for just three hours to hear the narration of "Lagaan" before deciding. "He heard it. Bhandarkar's route to "Chandni Bar" was rooted in observation. A visit to a dance bar with a friend gave him the subject for the Tabu-starring film, which he researched by visiting bars across Mumbai for six months. "Even if, in retrospect, 'Chandni Bar' had not done well at the box office, I would still be happy because this is the cinema which I wanted to make, and this is the world which I've seen," he said.

His interest in underdogs has continued in films such as "Page 3," "Corporate" and "Fashion," which examined the worlds of the media, business and fashion respectively. He has also resisted pressure to turn women-centered stories into male-led films for commercial reasons. From Tabu's vulnerable character in "Chandni Bar" to Raveena Tandon's politically ambitious woman in "Satta," he said he is drawn to complex women rather than idealized portrayals. "My movies are basically a mirror to society," he said.

With "Dil Chahta Hai," a buddy movie starring Aamir Khan, Saif Ali Khan and Akshaye Khanna, Akhtar set out to bring a more recognizable version of friendship to the screen. He felt Hindi films' depictions of male friendship did not reflect how people actually spent time together. "The relationship had become cheesy, filmy," he said. He cited Mansoor Khan's "Qayamat Se Qayamat Tak" (1988), which he watched as a schoolboy, as an important influence.

Asked specifically about the older-woman romance in "Dil Chahta Hai," he said the inspiration for the relationship between the characters played by Akshaye Khanna and Dimple Kapadia came "from many crushes that I had on older women, that's the honest truth."

The filmmakers also reflected on how cinema is made and judged. Akhtar described a screenplay as "a living, breathing thing," evolving through contributions from actors and crew members, sometimes well into the edit. Gowariker, discussing acting, said, "The true performance lies in the pauses where you're not saying anything."

Asked what they would change if they could revisit one another's films, Akhtar said he would leave "Lagaan" untouched, but if he were the producer, he would ask Gowariker to direct it again. Gowariker said he might add a soulful song to "Chandni Bar," while Bhandarkar said he would find it difficult to remake either "Lagaan" or "Dil Chahta Hai."

On AI and visual effects, Bhandarkar said, "AI and VFX can certainly enhance the visual experience and expand the possibilities of filmmaking, but they can never replace the human touch." Akhtar's advice to younger filmmakers was to draw on their own unique experiences.
