&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google It is impossible not to have heard about the release of Grand Theft Auto VI , as gamers across the globe are now playing one of the most highly anticipated video games of all time. Rockstar's newest release has received critical acclaim and is currently claiming hours of attention from anyone who has purchased it.

No matter how incredible the game is, a binge session can only go so long. Eventually, fans are going to want a break. Luckily, these six crime films will scratch the same itch as GTA VI , keeping viewers in the zone before they drop back into Vice City.

Michael Mann's 2004 film Collateral is one of the greatest movies of the 2000s, and it still holds up nearly two decades (and several GTA games) later. The film follows Jamie Foxx's Max Durocher, a Los Angeles cab driver who is given an incredibly high fare to drive to several locations with Tom Cruise's Vincent. Eventually, Max discovers that Vincent is a hitman, making the driver an accomplice to his crimes. So, Max must play his cards right in order to survive the night, escape Vincent, and avoid any trouble from the fallout of the killings.

Collateral has all of the key elements that make a GTA game, such as automotive crime, violent killings , and a seedy criminal underworld. The film takes place in LA, which is parodied in the GTA V setting, Los Santos. Plus, GTA VI actually references Collateral , as players can have an encounter where an NPC inspired by Vincent takes Jason hostage and forces him to drive a taxi around. So, before getting to this moment in the game, it's a good idea to check out Collateral .

This isn't the first or the last time that a Michael Mann film will appear on this list, but 1995's Heat is arguably the director's best film , and it fits perfectly into the vibe of GTA VI . Set in Los Angeles, the film follows the cat-and-mouse game between Al Pacino's LAPD detective Vincent Hanna and Robert De Niro's aging career criminal Neil McCauley. The near-three-hour epic kicks off with an iconic sequence in which an armored car is robbed, and the ball doesn't stop rolling until the credits.

Heat features intricately planned, large-scale robberies and a cast of criminal characters, and it's not hard to see how the film inspired the GTA franchise. On top of that, the film hinges on a tense chase of a cop trying to catch a criminal. Much of the fun of the GTA games is trying to help your criminal player character evade the police, and while Heat is a bit more dramatic, it perfectly captures this conflict.

Directed by Ridley Scott, the mastermind behind Alien and Blade Runner , 1991's Thelma & Louise is one of the director's most underrated films. The film stars Geena Davis as Thelma and Susan Sarandon as Louise, two friends who decide to go on a weekend fishing trip. While on the way, the duo kills an attempted rapist at a bar. So, they decide to flee to Mexico, with them driving their 1996 Ford Thunderbird convertible across the country as the FBI pursues them. The film was critically acclaimed, with it being nominated for six Oscars and winning Best Original Screenplay. Tragically, the film hasn't received the same mainstream notoriety as some of Scott's franchise films.

Like some of the other films on this list, Thelma & Louise is about characters running away from the police. However, the chase is far more literal in this film, as the police are always just one step behind the duo. Like in GTA , the main characters have to evade police cars and helicopters, and they occasionally have to survive encounters with the authorities while keeping their identities hidden.

Released only a year before Jaws , 1974's The Sugarland Express is one of legendary director Steven Spielberg's lesser-known films. However, it is just as good as any of the filmmaker's other masterpieces. The film stars Goldie Hawn as Lou Jean and William Atherton as Clovis, the latter of whom begins the film incarcerated. Lou Jean pays Clovis a visit to inform him that their son will soon be placed in foster care. So, they steal a car, take a police officer hostage, and go on the run from the authorities as they attempt to reach their son.

Players of GTA VI should immediately be able to see the parallels. Like The Sugarland Express , GTA VI follows a criminal couple who, after one of them gets out of prison, attempt to regain custody of their young child. Both the film and the game feature a massive amount of police vehicles getting involved in chases, and a world of crime centered around a surprisingly tender relationship.

When it comes to movies centered around a police chase, it is impossible to think of one more iconic than Hal Needham's 1977 film Smokey and the Bandit . The film follows two criminals, Burt Reynolds' Bo "Bandit" Darville and Jerry Reed's Cledus "The Snowman" Snow, who are tasked with illegally transporting a large supply of Coors beer from Texarkana to Atlanta for some wealthy benefactors. Knowing that law enforcement will be on their tail, they devise a plan. Snowman will drive the truck full of beer, while the Bandit will act as a blocker, using his iconic 1977 Pontiac Trans Am to distract police. However, they never could have expected how persistent Jackie Gleason's Sheriff Buford T. Justice would be.

Smokey and the Bandit is a film about transporting an illegal drug while evading law enforcement by way of reckless driving. It is hard to think of a film that could be more GTA than that. Plus, the film's Southern setting makes it not too far off from GTA VI 's South Florida setting, with the film's cast resembling some of the side characters in the game.

Michael Mann is a master of the crime genre, and no film from throughout cinematic history could be a better companion piece to GTA VI than the director's 2006 take on Miami Vice . Based on the iconic 1980s TV show, Miami Vice stars Colin Farrell as Sonny and Jamie Foxx as Rico, two officers from the Miami-Dade Sheriff's Office who work together to take on the world of illegal narcotics.

Miami Vice is all over the DNA of GTA VI , with the game's setting even paying tribute to the franchise by being named Vice City. The game's blue and pink color scheme is directly ripped from the original show, and while this isn't as present in the 2006 film, it occasionally pops up. A game based on GTA VI would feel exactly like Miami Vice , and until one gets made, this is the closest to a GTA movie adaptation that we have.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google Action figure collectors today sometimes forget how good we have it. Today’s world of collector-focused toylines means any property, no matter how obscure, is fair game for an action figure line. But going back a few decades, this was hardly the case: conventional wisdom held that the primary target for any toys were children, and as such, any toyline had to be something manufacturers could see kids wanting. Doctor Who , despite being a decades-running staple of British television, somehow avoided almost any toy merchandising during its original, twenty-six year lifetime. Denys-Fisher (Mego’s U.K. affiliate) released a small range of Doctor Who figures in 1977 and 1978; several companies offered Daleks; but for many collectors, the dream was an extensive, Star Wars -style line of characters from across eras and Doctors.&nbsp;

Model train manufacturer Dapol finally delivered in 1987, although with perhaps underwhelming results. That didn’t stop Doctor Who fans from turning out in force to collect the line – at least at first. The Dapol Doctor Who line was plagued with problems, both with the figures themselves and with their production. The line weathered the cancellation of the series, constant manufacturing delays, and a factory-destroying fire before Dapol lost the Doctor Who license in 2002. Their figures are badly painted, oddly articulated, and sometimes questionably accurate to the series. But like Kenner’s Star Wars line or Mego’s Star Trek , collectors look back fondly on the Dapol era, a time when there was thrill to just having a figure of a character like the Third Doctor or the Master. Today, the Dapol line may have been surpassed by Character Options’ still-running 5” Doctor Who line, but Dapol’s figures are still collected passionately by long-time Doctor Who fans, evidenced best by the nearly four-digit asking price for this 1987 TARDIS set .

Film and television tie-in toylines were largely avoided by manufacturers up until the 1970s . While this isn’t to say they didn’t happen, they usually consisted of low-cost, low-risk items, and not the kinds of elaborate action figure ranges we’ve come to expect. Doctor Who was no exception: while the series was immediately popular with children, British toy companies capitalized modestly. In the 1960s, Louis Marx manufactured several Dalek toys , but no accompanying Doctor or companions. The Marx Dalek was ubiquitous on U.K. shelves for years, and actually ended up in the series on two occasions. For the serials Evil of the Daleks and Planet of the Daleks , scores of the Louis Marx figures were put in the background of shots to make Dalek armies look larger. The Marx Dalek was the only figural Doctor Who toy produced for nearly fifteen years, with Denys Fisher providing the first-ever articulated action figure of the Doctor himself in 1978 .&nbsp;

The Doctor was based on Tom Baker’s iconic incarnation of the Time Lord, complete with his signature scarf. He was accompanied by then-current companions Leela and robot dog K-9 , still fan-favorites fifty years on. The assortment of “villain” characters included the perennial Dalek, a Cyberman, and the K-1 Robot from Baker’s inaugural episode, the creatively titled “Robot.” Boxed or loose, the Denys Fisher figures and TARDIS are incredibly rare and collectible, with the Dalek and companion Leela valued at up to $1k mint in box. The Denys-Fisher TARDIS operated under the same principle as Mego’s Star Trek transporter playset, making figures “disappear” inside with the push of a button. Around the same time, Palitoy manufactured a talking Dalek and talking K-9. K-9 is the rarer of the two, and is a surprisingly detailed and screen-accurate item for its era. Despite arriving at the show’s arguable ratings peak (as well as its high-water mark creatively, if you ask many fans), neither the Palitoy nor the Denys-Fisher range were especially popular.

Dapol inherited the license at a ratings and creative nadir. Their line came to market in 1988, just in time for the series 25th Anniversary. In celebration, Dapol incorporated not only the series’ current Doctor (Sylvester McCoy), companion (Bonnie Langford’s Mel), and adversaries, but an assortment from across its history. By 1988, the series was running on fumes, having narrowly evaded cancellation in 1985. The first wave consisted of the 7th Doctor, Mel, K-9, the bat-like alien Tetrap, and four different Daleks. Dapol’s Daleks would be the longest-running and most expansive aspect of the line, with some two dozen Dalek variations produced in the fourteen years they held the Doctor Who license. The second wave arrived in 1989, just before the cancellation of the original series. It included the 4th Doctor (sans-scarf, a complaint of collectors for thirty-seven years and counting), companion Ace, a new Cyberman, and first-ever figures of Dalek creator Davros and the 2nd and 3rd Doctor foes the Ice Warriors. Bizarrely, the Ice Warrior came with an accessory reused from Hasbro’s G.I. Joe line.&nbsp;

Two subsequent waves of new figures followed, bringing fans the 3rd Doctor (Jon Pertwee) and Roger Delgado’s incarnation of the Master, the “Melkur” statue from The Keeper of Traken , and four more aliens: a Time Lord, a Silurian, a Sea Devil, and a Sontaran. The alien sculpts are regarded as Dapol’s best work, detailed and fairly screen-accurate. Their human (and Time Lord) characters were criticized for soft likenesses and poor paint applications. Plastic quality and articulation were concerns across the line, the figures were brittle and prone to breakage. The line was rounded out by a TARDIS playset, at first just capturing the “police box” exterior of the vehicle, later resculpted to fold-out into the TARDIS’s interior, complete with plastic console.

While the fourth wave marked the end of the articulated line, a few unarticulated figures were produced in roughly the same scale as the articulated figures. These consisted of the 2nd Doctor (Patrick Troughton) and a Troughton-era Cyberman as seen in episodes The Moonbase and Tomb of the Cybermen . A planned “Whomobile” vehicle for the 3rd Doctor was cancelled following a devastating 1994 fire at their primary factory, this also ended production for most of the figures. Prototypes for a 1st Doctor figure and a Yeti (from the 2nd Doctor serial The Web of Fear ) were also cancelled; numerous rumored additional companions and aliens also failed to materialize.&nbsp; Dapol continued to sell a limited number of characters (mostly surviving product from before the fire) and new Daleks up until 2002, when they lost the license. Character Options partnered with the BBC to take over the dormant license ahead of Doctor Who ’s 2005 return to screens, and still produce Doctor Who figures to this day.&nbsp;

Character Options lived up to their name, releasing extensive product support for the 2005 season and the years after. For the first five seasons of the modern series, fans could expect that virtually every monster, alien, or adversary they saw on-screen would have an accompanying action figure; as well as a generous helping of companions, guest stars, and variants of the Doctor. In 2008, they expanded the range to include characters from the original series, and became the first-ever line to issue figures of every canon Doctor up to that point (the exception, of course, being Peter Cushing's Doctor from the 1960s theatrical films Dr. Who & The Daleks and Daleks Invasion Earth: 2150 A.D) . As Doctor Who ’s popularity has ebbed and flowed, the quantity of releases coming from Character Options has changed accordingly, but that same dedicated collector base that supported the Dapol line have kept the Character Options range running. This year, Character Options has finally issued the Yeti as a build-a-figure, making good on the promise of both Dapol’s prototype and their own long-delayed figure of the villain.

As one of the most intense and unforgettable psychological thrillers ever made, Misery may not get you in the mood for the festive holidays, but it will make you think twice about those dreams of becoming famous.

The story of Misery is a simple one. Author Paul Sheldon (James Caan) is injured in a bad car crash but that is the least of his worries when he is "rescued" by Annie Wilkes (the impeccably unhinged Kathy Bates), a woman who claims to be his number one fan. The appeal of these characters is simple, as they attack a hero's heart and judgment instead of his body, and most can't fight that.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google Star Trek: Deep Space Nine has a certain reputation with Star Trek fans. The third Star Trek show, it debuted in 1993 and ran until 1999. It was the first Trek show to experiment with serialized storytelling, while incorporating the standard monster of the week episodes (in Trek parlance, they're sometimes called "anomalies of the week") that were just as much about building the characters up for the lore episodes that would be the anchor of each season. Deep Space Nine has become a favorite of Trek fans for numerous reasons, but the biggest was that it was different than any other Trek we'd gotten. The connections it built between the cast and the audience made everything hit that much harder, allowing the show's episodes to reach some amazing heights, even if not all of them get the recognition they deserve.

Every era of Star Trek has amazing episodes , but Deep Space Nine has a surplus of them. Episodes like "Duet", "The Visitor", "Far Beyond the Stars", "In the Pale Moonlight", and "Under the Paper Moon" get the lion's share of the praise, but there's one episode that deserves way more praise than it gets. The seventh season of the show has its ups and downs (the last seven episode stretch is one of my favorite finale build-ups ever, but there are too many Ezri episodes earlier episodes earlier in the season), but there's one episode that blew me away when I first saw it – "The Siege of AR-558". This episode is one of the most harrowing episodes in Trek history and an underrated gem.

Star Trek: Deep Space Nine was different from other Trek in a variety of ways. To begin with, it was set on a space station and not a starship. Captain Sisko was the first black Captain and the show played heavily with religious themes related to him and the Bajoran people. The show was more character-focused than science-focused, and it told different kind of stories. It also went in a direction that previous Trek really only toyed with – the Federation at war.

The inciting incident of the show was the discovery of the Bajoran wormhole leading to the Gamma Quadrant and in the second season we were introduced to the Dominion, a mysterious power from the far side of the galaxy. Season Three would reveal that the Founders were the Changelings, Security Chief Odo's race, who ruled the Dominion, leading to a cold war throughout the season. The fourth season would see a war between the Cardassians and the Klingons covertly started by the Dominion, with Starfleet eventually fighting the Klingons, before the truth was revealed. The Cardassians joined the Dominion, and the Klingons and Federation teamed back up.

Seasons Four, Five, Six, and Seven gave us something that we'd never seen in Trek history. Sure, we had seen ship to ship battles for years, and the Original Series revolved around the ongoing conflict between the Federation and the Klingons, but we'd never seen a shooting war. Deep Space Nine gave us that and it was awesome. However, most of the show's battles were still ship to ship. We barely got to see the actual ground war against the Dominion and their genetically engineered soldiers the Jem'Hadar until the eighth episode of Season Seven, "The Siege of AR-558".

The episode saw the U.S.S. Defiant bring supplies to a Starfleet outpost on AR-558 in the Chin'Toka System. The position had been under siege for months with no relief troops sent in. They were surrounded by cloaked Houdini mines – invisible explosives that would chase you down – and constantly under attack. Jem'Hadar ships end up attacking the Defiant , with Sisko ordering the ship away and volunteering to take command of AR-558 until it can return.

For years, we were used to Starfleet as the smiling good guys, always bringing succor and aid to anyone who needed it. They were clean and civilized, their ships basically flying luxury hotels in the stars, especially by the time we got to the 24th century of the TNG/DS9/VOY era of the show. We saw them struggle at times, but we had never seen like this. I think the best way to describe this episode is that it's a Vietnam movie.

The Starfleet officers are like the naive young recruits from 'Nam movies at the halfway point. They had experienced horrors the likes of which they never imagined and it changed them. One of the officers, Reese, had begun to take the ketracel white tubes – the addictive drug that kept the Jem'Hadar under the Founders' control – which is a direct reference to every crazy soldier who collected body parts of the people they killed in Vietnam movie. We get to spend time with these stressed out, injured, mentally beaten men and women, seeing what happened when Starfleet met a situation they couldn't just technobabble their way through.

That said, technobabble does end up helping to save the day, when Sisko and company are able to suborn the Houdinis and turn them against the Jem'Hadar, but this isn't your typical "monster of the week" episode. It technically fits into the overarching arc of the season, but it is a standalone episode as far as the story it starts ends in the same episode. The episode, though, does have repercussions for the main cast, in the form of Nog. Or, in this case, Nog's leg.

The Ferengi Nog's arc from vaguely criminal kid to Starfleet officer is one the best in the show. His uncle Quark was completely against his nephew joining Starfleet, believing it not a place for a Ferengi. However, Nog was always able to come through without a scratch, something that would end in this episode. His hearing made him an amazing scout and while on patrol, he was shot in the leg. Jem'Hadar weapons were designed to be especially damaging and his leg had to be removed. It's a harrowing moment, watching this young character who had grown so much so gravely injured.

The episode's final battle scene is the heartbreaking center of the story. We had grown attached to these soldiers throughout the episode and the final charge of the Jem'Hadar would be the end of several of them (Reese survives; sci-fi legend Bill Mumy's character Kellin doesn't). Star Trek actions scenes always seem so safe; sure we see red shirts die all the time, but there's never really a sense of danger. Deep Space Nine had several ground combat scenes and they were always able to impart that sense of death around the corner that the best battle scenes could. "The Siege of AR-558&#8243;'s final scene is everything you could want from that perspective. The ending, with the soldiers being relieved as a new group takes over the outpost, is the perfect denouement for this episode, leaving viewers with the sense of more meat being loaded into the meat grinder.

Deep Space Nine was always a cut above the others . It was unique back then in the pantheon of both Trek and sci-fi TV in general, and no other Trek series has ever reached the same level. The show combined many of the best writers from TNG and was able to show that Star Trek didn't have to just be science-based monster of the week; it could tell a long coherent story over the course of the entire series. I just did a rewatch last year and it's one of the few Trek series that is basically great the entire time; even TNG was bad for most of the first two seasons (seriously, don't watch Season One at all after "Encounter at Farpoint"; there's not much good there).

The show is able to stand up so well because of how well it built its characters and the unique stories that you could only do with Deep Space Nine . "The Siege of AR-558" took the idea of the Federation at war to its ultimate extent – what happens when the scientists and ambassadors and engineers have to get down in the filth with the foot soldiers. I missed the first run of the last season of DS9 . I was 18 and had others things to do at 7:00pm on a Sunday in 1998 and 1999. I caught a rerun of it a few months after the show ended and was blown away. It was completely different from any other Trek I had ever watched and it stuck with me in a way few others episodes have. Nog's screams are unforgettable, the death knell of the innocence of Starfleet. War is hell, even when you have replicators and holosuites back home.

For all its serious moments, the story features characters with food names, an afterlife where bad guys are tortured by fairies and Mr. Satan changing to Hercule in the English dub and more amongst other numerous funny bits . One weird bit that should've received another edit before making the final cut, however, is Android 17 and 18's name choice.

Android 17 and 18 are introduced as killing machines in Dragon Ball Z . They were kidnapped and created by the Red Ribbon Army's Dr. Gero to kill Goku and take over the world. Their almost emotionless violence and super-powered abilities gave them a robot feel. Once Cell is defeated, the human part of them is restored, but their human names are not. It was revealed later by franchise creator Akira Toriyama that their names are Lapis (17) and Lazuli (18 ), however they were never brought into the manga or anime.

Android 17 becomes a park ranger after the events of the Cell Saga and falls off the radar. Even though this point applies to him too and he deserves to have his name be something other than a number, the plot doesn't awkwardly point it out time after time. Android 18 on the other hand marries Krillin, gives birth to Marron and becomes a member of the Z Fighters. And yet, her name stays simply, "18." Anytime Krillin addresses her it's that same number. An unceremonious way to address one of the anime's fan favorite characters .

Plot-wise it makes sense to change it. The androids went from humans to bio-mechanical beings, so the number applies and fits their evil personas. The issue introduced readers not only the Scarlet Speedster , but the original Golden Age incarnation of Hawkman , and beloved Justice Society of America mainstay Johnny Thunder and his Thunderbolt. Plus, the issue introduces Shiera Sanders, the original Hawkgirl — though she doesn't take on her heroic alter-ego until the following year's All-Star Comics #5. This is where DC began to build a universe, debuting heroes who would soon become charter members of the Justice Society of America. Before Flash Comics #1, the heroes of the DC Universe consisted of Superman , Batman , Zatara , and the Crimson Avenger. By the end of 1940, that roll call would expand to include Robin , the Spectre , Hourman, Doctor Fate, and the Green Lantern. The epicenter of that super-hero explosion, which would earn the "Golden Age" its name, began here. It's no wonder, then, that the Overstreet Price Guide lists this as the twelfth most valuable comic of the era — unrestored, of course.

Comic book restoration is a long-standing and controversial collecting practice. For decades, it was acceptable to pay a professional or to attempt at home to improve the condition of a vintage comic book. As comic book grading became common practice, restoration came to be seen as "cheating" the grade, artificially inflating the book's value. As a result, restored books are often worth a mere fraction of the price of an unrestored copy, even when the issue in question is a Golden Age mega-key like Flash Comics #1. The CGC (Certified Guarantee Company) lists less than one hundred copies of Flash Comics #1 on their population report. In an unrestored 8.0 grade, Heritage Auctions quotes a value of $234k . But the copy on auction through Heritage later this week was the recipient of extensive restoration, making it an "apparent" rather than a true 8.0. At the time of writing, this looks to be a $200k difference, as the current price sits just shy of $27k.

While DC's "big three" (Superman, Batman, and Wonder Woman) are products of the Golden Age, they transcended the era, evading cancellation at its tail end and evolving to suit a changing medium . If you say "Golden Age" to a DC Comics fan, they'll instead think of the heroes of the Justice Society, rooted in and defined by the era that spawned them. These characters emerged from the culture and the conventions of the 1940s, embodying the courage and determination of an America in the midst of World War II. After their initial retirement at the decade's close, a conscious decision was made by subsequent creators to keep these characters frozen in time. Gardner Fox created "Earth-Two" for them to inhabit, and though time passed there and its heroes aged (Earth-Two's Superman is distinguished by his graying temples, after all), it always felt like the Golden Age never ended. Even Crisis on Infinite Earths did little to change this, collapsing the multiverse but retaining the World War II-era for the heroes of the Justice Society.

Much of this owes to the storytelling, with the origins of Jay Garrick's Flash, Carter Hall's Hawkman, and Johnny Thunder feeling distinctly old-fashioned. As written in Flash Comics #1, college student Jay Garrick is experimenting with "hard water" (water with a high mineral concentration, for those of us who didn't pay attention in science class) when he accidentally gains super-powers from minerals in the water's vapor combining with the chemicals in the cigarette he is smoking. Ret-cons would change this from "hard water" to "heavy water," a key component in atomic bomb research. Taking on the identity of "the Flash," Garrick first dismantles a protection racket operating in his home of New York City, then foils a spy ring that have kidnapped his girlfriend's army major father. Garrick would headline the Flash until its 1949 cancellation, as well as All-Flash Quarterly from 1941 to 1947. He also appeared in Justice Society stories in All-Star Comics beginning in 1941 and lasting until 1951. After All-Star Comics ' cancellation, Garrick would not appear again until Gardner Fox's landmark The Flash #123, when he would team up with his Earth-One successor Barry Allen for the first time.

The Carter Hall iteration of Hawkman drew influence from Universal's The Mummy with its themes of ancient Egyptian reincarnation. Hall was an archaeologist and the reincarnation of Egyptian Prince Khufu, murdered along with his lover Chay-Ara by jealous priest Hath-Set. When Hall touches the ancient Nth metal dagger Khufu used to kill Khufu, he regains his memories of his past life. Creating the costume and Nth metal anti-gravity belt of "Hawkman," Hall rescues Chay-Ara's reincarnation, Shiera Sanders, from Hath-Set's reincarnation, Anton Hastor. This became the basis for a notorious continuity headache after Crisis on Infinite Earths , which had to square the "reincarnated Egyptian prince" Hawkman with the Silver Age "alien policeman" Hawkman introduced by Gardner Fox in 1961's Brave and the Bold #34. As well as joining the Flash in founding the Justice Society, Hawkman served with distinction in Roy Thomas's fan favorite All-Star Squadron , a World War II-set love letter to the Golden Age heroes and villains of DC Comics.

Johnny Thunder is a comparative footnote, but a fondly remembered one among JSA fans. The character is kind of ridiculous, and his origin is laden with poorly-aged Orientalist stereotypes. Johnny is the seventh son of a seventh son, kidnapped by mystics of "Badhnesia" for an occult ritual on his seventh birthday. Instead of bringing power to the Badhnesians, the ritual causes Johnny to gains control of a genie known as the Thunderbolt — though he can't seem to remember the magic incantation to summon the genie. After years (and twenty issues of Flash Comics ) of summoning the Thunderbolt by accident, he discovers that the magic words are "cei-u," pronounced phonetically exactly the same as the English language phrase "say you." Like Snapper Carr and the Justice League or Rick Jones and the Avengers, Johnny Thunder was in the right place at the right time, chosen to be the first new member to join the Justice Society in All-Star Comics #6. As a result, the character was revived alongside the rest of the team in Gardner Fox's Silver Age Justice League-Justice Society team-ups, cementing him as one of DC's major Golden Age heroes in the eyes of long-time comic fans.

Before the end of its Golden Age hundred-and-four issue run, Flash Comics would spawn one of the era's other most iconic characters, Black Canary , as well as fan-favorite villains like Gentleman Ghost and the Shade. Retitled "The Flash," Flash Comics was revived in 1959 as the adventures of the Barry Allen Flash, retaining its original numbering and beginning with issue #105 (also the first appearance of Mirror Master). Jay Garrick would return in his former comic two years later, in the aforementioned Flash #123. Reader response (and sales) were so overwhelmingly positive that, over the next several years, all of the classic Justice Society heroes were revived, many fighting alongside their Silver Age counterparts. Such was the case for Carter Hall's Hawkman, who switched cowls to avoid visual confusion with the Thanagarian Katar Hol. Annual Justice League-Justice Society team-ups became a tradition, lasting up to the early eighties.

Crisis on Infinite Earths changed this status quo and the Golden Agers' history, but did nothing to reduce their role in the DC Universe — if not strictly because comic readers wouldn't have it any other way. These heroes have become firm cult favorite for true DC fans, their old-fashioned heroism a reminder of the genre's earliest days. If any book were to serve as the emblem of those beginnings, it would be Flash Comics #1. And while the $27k bid on the Flash Comics #1 up for auction through Heritage is a far cry from the eye-watering $234k that the book could sell for, it is still a staggering sum. Shy of Action Comics #1 or All-Star Comics #8, it's difficult to imagine a better book for the centerpiece of one's collection.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google For all the memorable characters Grand Theft Auto has introduced over the years, there's always been something a little off about how the series approaches its protagonists. They're often larger-than-life personalities with enough baggage to fill an entire criminal enterprise, yet their personal stories can feel surprisingly disconnected from the chaos happening around them. GTA 5 made an ambitious attempt to address this by giving players three protagonists with wildly different personalities. It was an entertaining experiment, but it was a hard sell to get you to feel genuine compelled in their actual lives. This is especially apparent when you recall Rockstar didn't really bother expand upon their stories after the credits. That's where Grand Theft Auto 6 gets the narrative right.

Jason and Lucia are exactly the kind of protagonists I've wanted to see from this franchise. Rather than splitting attention between three people with competing ambitions, Rockstar builds its latest crime saga around a relationship that gives the entire adventure a much stronger emotional foundation. They're partners whose futures are tied together, for better or considerably worse, which means their actions take on an inherent emotional risk, adding an extra layer to literally everything they say and do. This shift makes an enormous difference, as it gives players a natural core to care about the about them, in one of the most dangerous professions: being a criminal.

One of the biggest strengths of GTA 5 is undoubtedly its three-protagonist setup. Michael, Franklin, and Trevor each bring something different to the table, and switching between them creates opportunities for comedy and conflict that wouldn't work nearly as well with a single lead. But that structure comes with a trade-off. The story constantly has to divide its attention between three separate lives, each with their own problems and motivations. Their paths intersect in entertaining ways, but the relationships between them rarely become the emotional foundation holding the entire experience together. They're memorable individuals first and a unified group second. I would not call that a flaw, as that's what GTA 5 set out to accomplish, but it does leave its narrative feeling less intimate than it could have been.

Jason and Lucia's relationship isn't some subplot that Rockstar occasionally revisits between heists. It's the connective tissue running through the entire experience. Their criminal ambitions are intertwined because neither character is simply pursuing some grand scheme in isolation. They're trying to build a future together, even when their circumstances make that prospect seem increasingly ridiculous. That gives their decisions a stronger weight. A dangerous job isn't just another opportunity to make money or create some spectacular mess. It's something that could affect the person standing beside them, that's a natural concept for any human to feel, whether its a spouse, a family member, or anything between. I find that considerably more compelling than simply having another protagonist to switch between.

Jason and Lucia don't need to constantly agree with each other to make their partnership interesting, nor does their connection depend on turning every interaction into some grand romantic declaration. Their chemistry comes through in the smaller moments, the banter, the disagreements, and the sense that these two people have developed a life together despite everything working against them. Rockstar has always been capable of writing memorable personalities, but giving those personalities a relationship worth investing in is a different challenge altogether. GTA 6 understands that distinction, and it makes Jason and Lucia considerably more interesting as a pair than either protagonist would be in isolation.

One of the things I find most interesting about Jason and Lucia is that their story doesn't have to stop whenever a mission ends. Rockstar has been building open worlds for years, where players can lose entire afternoons doing practically anything that catches their attention. Yet those diversions have often existed separately from the narratives unfolding in the main story. GTA 6 makes a more concerted effort to bring those two experiences together. Spending time with Jason and Lucia outside missions can reveal more about their personalities through conversations and interactions that respond to what's happening around them. Rather than relying entirely on cutscenes to develop its protagonists, Rockstar gives players opportunities to get to know them simply by spending time in their shoes.

This approach apparent in the smaller interactions between the two. Jason and Lucia can hold hands while exploring the world together, wait for one another when getting into a car, and even hold doors open for each other. None of these actions is particularly spectacular on its own, but that's precisely why I like them. They're the sorts of things that make a relationship feel… true, rather than something that only exists when the story needs an emotional moment. Rockstar's developers have discussed how these interactions evolved from individual mission ideas into features players could experience throughout the open world. It's a surprisingly thoughtful use of the freedom that makes GTA 6 so appealing in the first place.

I think this is an important step forward for the series because it makes the time between major story beats feel more meaningful. The best open-world games understand that their protagonists shouldn't disappear as characters whenever players decide to ignore the main objective for a while. I'd argue that's one of the greatest faults of the sub-genre, so its great to see GTA 6 take a stab at tackling this long-running problem. You aren't always watching Jason and Lucia's story happen. Instead, sometimes you're simply driving around together, and the game finds ways to make even that feel like part of it. It's ultimately a clever way to strengthen the central narrative and bring the open-world design closer together in unison. Personally, I hope Rockstar carries that philosophy into whatever comes next.
