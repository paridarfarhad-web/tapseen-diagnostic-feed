While the Bond movies were popular, the Dr. No adaptation didn't make as much of a splash and Bond comics were a thing of the past.

His signature film was, by its very narrative design, an encapsulation of his virtues as a performer: In a room of 12 angry men, you could count on him to be the calmer, quieter one, a bastion of sense and reason. Upstanding integrity may not be be the sexiest thing to be known for as a movie star, but Fonda was never stodgy with it. In half a century of stardom, Sylvester Stallone has had two just roles that connected with audiences on the mythological level out of which movie stardom is sculpted. But that turned out to be just enough. The first role was, of course, Rocky Balboa, who Stallone played with hooded eyes and a voice of muffled molasses and a groggy valor that came to life. The beauty of “Rocky” is that it connected the glory of Frank Capra to the grittiness of the New Hollywood. And what made it work was the shabby majesty of Stallone’s presence, the way he believed in the character — the way he made Rocky, in his tattered dreams, an echo of us all. Consider “Rocky” and “Rambo” the yin and yang of a star with old movies in his (first) blood. — O.G.

But the Zendaya moment that gave us that first star tingle came in a musical that never got the love or respect it deserved: “The Greatest Showman.” As she sings “Rewrite the Stars,” hanging from a hoop trapeze, her face lights up with the pleasure of knowing that a forbidden love might be stronger than prejudice. In “La La Land,” the movie that solidified her place as one of the stars of our time, Emma Stone plays a struggling actress who becomes a movie star. In a career of dizzying and daring choices, she can play vintage heroic (“Battle of the Sexes”), saintly or scheming or both at once (“The Favourite”), a cawing lascivious Frankenstein monster (“Poor Things”), a ruthless corporate sociopath you start to root for (“Bugonia”), or she can turn a kids’ movie into a thrilling ride for adults (“Cruella”). The ugly presence of “The Birth of a Nation” in their joint filmography can’t be ignored, but it shouldn’t cancel out the technical and thespian achievements of “Intolerance” or “Broken Blossoms,” with its haunting “closet scene,” a master class in confined emotional torment. Sound didn’t end Gish’s career — eventually delivering her an Oscar nod for “Duel in the Sun” and an all-time classic in “The Night of the Hunter” — but hers remains a legacy defined by silence. — G.L.

Outrageously brash and uproariously full of fury, she seems to be channeling Groucho Marx, Richard Pryor and Medea. Yet what made Reynolds just about the biggest movie star of the 1970s was his mixture of macho bluster and teasing self-mockery. He was perhaps the first hunk of Hollywood beefcake who refused to take himself seriously (a quality he honed on the late-night talk-show circuit) — and that lends his performances in everything from “The Longest Yard” to the chicken-fried drive-in juggernaut of “Smokey and the Bandit” a quality of insolent swagger marked by his irresistible wink at his own magnetism. — O.G.

Rose, an heiress fallen on hard times who insists on pursuing love over security, etched into box office history what Winslet does as well as anyone: She shows us a woman who understands the rules of society and then attempts, fiercely, to claw through the veneer of propriety. Whether Winslet, onscreen, is blowing up her marriage (“Little Children”) or her career (“Steve Jobs”) or her entire existence (“Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind”), she seems always to be making decisions utterly in the moment — and surprising even herself. Little wonder that with “Titanic,” Winslet became the onscreen avatar for untold millions of teen girls. Perhaps no other actress might have so perfectly merged earthy realism with, just a millimeter beneath, a dreamer’s sense of possibility. — D.D.

No one becomes a movie star without trying to. But after fashioning himself into the moody pinup heartthrob of a generation in the “Twilight” films, Robert Pattinson made a radical left turn. He’d become a star, but he almost seemed to leave stardom at the altar when he took on a series of edgelord roles in defiantly niche art films (&#8220;Good Time,&#8221; “The Childhood of a Leader,” “The Lost City of Z”), spending the better part of a decade doing the character-actor-in-a-leading-man’s-body thing. We don’t mean to make light of it; he was honing his chops, showing the world the kind of star he wanted to be. And when he was cast in “The Batman,” the best of both worlds finally came together: Behind the gruff voice, Pattinson created a Batman who was more complexly dynamic and riveting than any who had come before him. As his daring performances in “Primetime” and “The Drama” attest, he’s a new kind of movie star: hushed and saturnine, plugged into the dark side of his time. Will that be reflected in his upcoming role as James Bond? We're betting that Pattinson leaves us as shaken as we are stirred. — O.G.

It’s no accident that he became the voice of God. Though if you chart his career path, that was hardly his destiny. In the midst of a thriving New York theater career, Freeman became a regular on the '70s children’s TV series “The Electric Company.” But he didn’t explode into the spotlight until he was nearly 50, when he starred in “Street Smart” (1987), giving the most authentic performance as a wily, vicious street operator that the movies had ever seen. As he quickly proved, though, he could play deep-grained benevolence as complexly as he did ferocity. He became a humanistic everyman, as embodied by his memorable performance in “The Shawshank Redemption.” For this immense orator, the leap to voicing the Almighty came all too naturally, since that voice emerged from his inner quality — a higher wisdom that envelops you in its warmth. — A.T.

The actresses in Ingmar Bergman’s films weren’t just accomplished artists. Like Bergman himself, they were groundbreakers, expressing sides of women’s experience that had never been seen onscreen before, and doing it with a lacerating intimacy that took on an almost confessional quality. But even within that gallery of acting genius, Ullmann stands apart. In “Persona,” the movie that introduced her to the world, she gave a nearly silent performance as a theater actor so traumatized she had decided to stop speaking. Yet Ullmann spoke volumes with her face — the gaze that could be furious or beatific or full of fear. And as she became Bergman’s most complex and impassioned surrogate, what you saw in her was the charisma of unbridled expression, culminating in her virtuosic performance in “Scenes From a Marriage,” which she turned into nothing less than “A Doll’s House” for the postwar age. What’s a movie star? Someone who makes the truth shine as brightly as Liv Ullmann did. — O.G.

MUST SEE: &#8220;Scenes from a Marriage&#8221;

You knew he was a movie star the moment he charmed the world into a collective giggle fit in “Fast Times at Ridgemont High,” playing the stoner surf dude Jeff Spicoli with a blitzed bravado that made every line sound like a stupido catchphrase. From the moment he played a juvenile con in “Bad Boys,” you knew you were seeing the next-generation De Niro, and he knew it too. That’s why he was drawn, compulsively, to playing criminals, losers, lowlifes, renegades and ugly-mustache freaks. Yet in every case, he found the character’s underlying humanity. The shaved head that’s like an angry egg, topped by a wave of synthetic hair. The giant dagger eyebrows and mouth that curls into an evil leer. The body that makes her look like Jayne Mansfield crossed with Godzilla. It would be hard to think of an image in movie history as magnetic, scary, hilarious and flat-out insane as Divine in “Pink Flamingos.” In that 1972 midnight classic, she plays “the filthiest person alive,” who is also a homicidal demon-clown maniac with the personality of the Wicked Witch of the West on PCP. It’s a performance that’s pure drag Dada, but as Divine proved in every movie she made with director John Waters (the von Sternberg to her deranged Dietrich), she was no one-trick pony. He’ll always be Atticus Finch because of how large he made that character loom in the American imagination. It all started, of course, with Harper Lee’s great novel, but in the 1962 film adaptation of “To Kill a Mockingbird,” Peck turned Atticus into such an intractable yet homespun figure of relatable valor that it’s almost as if he was playing Abe Lincoln. The performance earned him an Oscar while spotlighting America’s racial discord and racial criminality at a time when the issue of race was starting to rip the country apart. Peck tended to play thoughtful and rather reserved characters, notably opposite Audrey Hepburn in “Roman Holiday,” but that made him the perfect actor to step into Alfred Hitchcock's psychologically twisted “Spellbound,” which seemed to be stress-testing the limits of his charmed rationality. — A.T.

It’s easy to forget that Gosling began his career on Disney Channel’s “All New Mickey Mouse Club” in the ’90s — before becoming one of the rare child stars to rise above the chaos of the industry to become an acclaimed actor in his own right. While the hit tearjerker “The Notebook” catapulted Gosling to superstardom in 2004, it was his performance as a drug-addicted teacher in “Half Nelson” two years later that showed his range and landed him the first of three Oscar nominations. A millennial dreamboat with a singing voice to match, he has thrived in everything from indies like “Lars and the Real Girl” to genre blockbusters like “Blade Runner 2049” to the glittery showbiz epiphany that was “La La Land,” playing everyone from Neil Armstrong to, well, just Ken. — A.T.

Few stars have hit the jackpot with their film debut in quite the way Lancaster did: with a leading role in a hell of a movie — Robert Siodmak’s icily hardboiled 1946 noir “The Killers” — that also just happened to encapsulate his whole deal as an actor. As an ex-boxer embroiled in an underworld scheme, ultimately undone by obsessive love, Lancaster’s character is a bruiser with a soul. It’s a tender-tough archetype that served the actor well in such films as “The Birdman of Alcatraz,” “Atlantic City” and, of course, “From Here to Eternity.” The enduring cultural memory of the latter is of him rolling around in the surf with Deborah Kerr, his brawny physique made barely decent with a tight swimsuit, but that scene rather obscures the portrait he etched in the film of military alpha masculinity with a stout liberal conscience. The action star was a phenomenon that rose up in the 1970s, and it took years for the concept of the “female action star” to seem anything more than a counterintuitive anomaly. She was fast and furious and decades ahead of her time, but she came up in the world of blaxploitation films, which were highly successful and influential movies that were marginalized as an “underground” happening. The reality, however, is that they were cultural bombshells, and Grier was nothing less than a revolutionary movie star. In the raw funk revenge thriller &#8220;Coffy,&#8221; she's sly-eyed and insolent, proudly sexual, rivetingly spontaneous in her violence (she wields a sawed-off shotgun like James Bond in an Afro), with a ruthlessness that feels rooted in experience. Even in the ’80s, when he was just an overgrown kid, Robert Downey Jr. never stopped talking. And in “Iron Man,” something fantastically ironic happened. He was starring in a comic book movie — a genre that tends to swallow up stars, even when the movies are hits. The hottest tickets at the most recent edition of Il Cinema Ritrovato, the classic film festival in Bologna, Italy, were for the Stanwyck retrospective.
