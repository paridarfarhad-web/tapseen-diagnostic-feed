[50] Can Peter Rice Fix Netflix? New Content Boss Has a Daunting To-Do List
Source: The Hollywood Reporter | Status: expanded | Words: 1493
Published: 2026-11-18T13:05:00+00:00
URL: https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/business/business-news/peter-rice-netflix-1236758024
------------------------------------------------------------
SUMMARY:
Ted Sarandos’ handpicked consigliere is tasked with reinventing the streamer — while his boss promises Wall Street that nothing is changing.

CONTENT / EXTRACTED DETAILS:
Reed Hastings found about Netflix 's big shake-up the same time everyone else did. The Netflix founder (he officially exited the board over the summer) told a rapt audience at the Masters of Scale Summit in San Francisco in late October that the mass layoffs, the exit of content chief Bela Bajaria and the surprise announcement of Peter Rice as its new head of content caught him just as off guard as the rest of Hollywood. "I didn't know those were coming, and it's hard because I'd like to be in the fray helping," Hastings told the audience in the Presidio Theater.

Still, the man who shaped Netflix into today's behemoth had thoughts on what Rice and co-CEOs Ted Sarandos and Greg Peters need to do. "The strategy is doing more and better television shows, more and better movies," Hastings said. "The core brand property is about being thrilling. We realized &#x2014; it took a long time &#x2014; that we could be distrusted, we could be disliked, but we can never be boring."

Now the burden will be on Rice to find those thrilling additions to Netflix's menu, perhaps retiring the safe but aging gourmet cheeseburger of the Bajaria era.

Rice helped forge Fox into an entertainment giant and was one of only a few Fox veterans brought into the Disney fold when the assets were acquired under Bob Iger (Disney exec Dana Walden was another). His taste and relationships at both Fox and Disney were, in the words of one source, "exquisite," with a knack for fare that was both commercial and high-end. At Disney, he helped build a general entertainment business from near-scratch, leaning on both FX and Hulu to help it do so.

But he has been out of the corporate mix since Bob Chapek unceremoniously ousted him in 2022, just a few short months before Chapek himself would get the boot.

Those post-corporate gigs, however, shed light on what he can bring to Netflix: He helped the L.A. Olympics Committee develop the entertainment for the opening and closing ceremonies, critical events that will be seen by millions (and perhaps billions) before passing the baton to Kathleen Kennedy. And perhaps more intriguingly, he inked a deal with A24. As A24 was revving up its original productions &#x2014; thanks to several rounds of investment that ended with a $3.5 billion valuation &#x2014; the insular New York indie studio signed Rice to a film and TV pact.

It's a testament to Rice's talent relationships that the notoriously director-driven studio tapped him to help shepherd some big swings. His credits at the studio included the Alex Garland-produced Warfare and Chris Rock's return to the director's chair with Misty Green . When he left for his Netflix post, his development slate included A24's biggest production to date, Garland's adaptation of the fantasy video game Elden Ring . In other words, he sought to bridge the highbrow and the mainstream with fare that thrills. It's a skill set that Sarandos seems to admire. "His creative instincts and business acumen are remarkable," Sarandos said to analysts Oct. 20. "Most importantly, Peter Rice understands talent relations."

But Rice will have his work cut out for him thanks in no small part to Sarandos, who keeps a tight leash on Netflix's content output and who already reiterated that the company's content strategy isn't changing. "The business and the business flywheel are working. There's no need for a fundamental shift in strategy," he said, even as he lamented the "doubles and singles" that the company delivered this year. Sarandos wants home runs, and he thinks Rice can do that, with a particular emphasis on the U.S. market.

"These big moves, after a disappointing 2026 in which programming expense grew by 10 percent while U.S. engagement per sub roughly stagnated, admit a problem that is increasingly obvious," Wolfe Research analyst Peter Supino wrote Oct. 20. "Ask a friend whether they are watching more or less Netflix these days &#x2014; in our experience, they'll say less."

If Rice makes an impact at Netflix in the near term, it may be with the company's film strategy, sources say, with the top job now open with the exit of Dan Lin. Rumors have circulated that ousted Warner Bros. film co-chiefs Pam Abdy and Mike De Luca could be in line to succeed Lin to lead Netflix's film division. But Rice has been telling staff that he isn't hiring a film boss for now. He wants to be in the trenches creatively, multiple sources say.

"We view [the executive changes] as a signal that management is focused on improving content output and creative hit rates," Bank of America analyst Jessia Reif Ehrlich wrote Oct. 21, adding that "management appears increasingly focused on generating larger scale hits in [the U.S. and Canada]. We believe it will take some time before any of the new management changes impact the film or TV slate."

In the meantime, junior executives at Netflix have been calling his former colleagues, asking how to thrive in a Peter Rice meeting. He has strong opinions but can be hard to read. "He'll make you work for it," notes one former colleague of his style, which involves leaving subordinates to figure out the solution without him spelling it out.

While Netflix is a major player in the movie business, it has largely avoided theatrical. Sarandos has said the company has not changed its streaming-first mentality, but rival film executives believe that a year from now, many more of the company's movies will have a theatrical component, &#xE0; la Amazon.

That's easier said than done. The company has relied on its algorithm &#x2014; not its marketing &#x2014; for the vast majority of its successes. But the company has conspicuously changed its tune of late: Netflix users have noticed that it now runs teaser trailers for its shows before other Netflix programming, a classic TV marketing move it has long shunned. And the company launched a major brand campaign dubbed "Find the Thing You Never Knew You Needed" on connected TVs, YouTube, TikTok and other platforms &#x2014; as well as in movie theaters. The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth (Nov. 25) and Narnia: The Magician's Nephew (Feb. 12) will be key tests to see if Netflix's marketing department can get people to theaters. Rivals believe they'll need to make more hires with theatrical marketing experience if they want to make a real go of it.

After Netflix failed to acquire Warner Bros. and its library, including DC Comics, Rice will also need to beef up its homegrown IP, particularly on the film side. "Netflix is weak in long-lasting franchises and universes," notes one veteran film exec known for IP plays. "Ted says he wants to have big, culturally impactful successes &#x2014; and maybe universes. What's then the next chess moves on that? They don't have Marvel or DC, and there's not one big acquisition to do it. How do they navigate that and show Wall Street and the entertainment world they can build what they weren't able acquire?""

Of course, Comcast is set to imminently spin off NBCUniversal, raising buzz in Hollywood as to whether the streaming giant may make one more acquisition play in search of franchises and film expertise. In a note to NBCUniversal staff Nov. 16, CEO Mike Cavanagh touted the "open, partnership-driven approach" that the company has, which "allows us to go even further, bringing great content to consumers wherever and however they choose to engage."

Indeed, one critique of Netflix over the past few years is that some of its biggest hits caught it by surprise, leaving it scrambling to catch up when Squid Game and KPop Demon Hunters hit big. In both instances, the company scrambled to secure Halloween costumes or merchandise for fans, deals that typically would be locked up well before a debut of a series or film. It's a sore spot Sarandos is well aware of. Earlier this year, the co-CEO oversaw the launch of a branding effort dubbed "The Netflix Effect," which attempted to calculate the economic and cultural impact of the company.

"Over the last decade, Netflix shows and movies have consistently shaped what people read, buy, listen to, eat, wear and play. We've pushed old songs back up the musical charts, helped niche sports go mainstream, and boosted sales of everything from chess sets, to Halloween costumes, to home storage," Sarandos wrote at the time. "Now we have a responsibility to keep that flywheel going."

Rice may be able to manage that flywheel better, but "let's only make the hits" is a running joke in Hollywood. Still, Hastings may be on to something. Rice may not be able to guarantee hits, but his track record suggests he can guarantee buzz. Whatever happens next, it won't be boring.
