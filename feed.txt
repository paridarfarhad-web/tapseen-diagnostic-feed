The '90s classic that became a franchise and is now reportedly getting another sequel with Macaulay Culkin has entertained generations of viewers, but despite its popularity, it still raises a pretty big question. The whole premise revolves around Kevin being left home alone while his family travels to Paris for Christmas, but you've probably wondered how the McCallisters managed to fly to another country without realizing they'd left an eight-year-old child behind.

It's a ridiculous situation when you actually stop to think about it , after all, we're talking about a kid, not a suitcase that someone could accidentally forget in all the holiday chaos. And that's why some people have come to consider this a plot hole, assuming it's just something the movie expects audiences to overlook so they can enjoy the story. But here's the thing: there's actually a scene that answers this question and makes it way easier to understand everything. The problem is that it happens very quickly, right in the middle of a chaotic moment, which makes it also easy to miss.

Early in the movie, we see the McCallister family getting ready for their trip, and the house is in a complete mess: relatives are arriving, everyone is busy packing and making last-minute preparations, and Kevin is having trouble getting along with his brother, Buzz (Devin Ratray). During dinner, he teases the protagonist about a cheese pizza, and Kevin doesn't take the jokes so well. Peter (John Heard), the family patriarch, tries to clean up the mess but accidentally makes things worse without realizing it. In the middle of all the commotion, while gathering the wet napkins and cleaning the table, he also picks up Kevin's plane ticket and throws it in the trash. The camera briefly shows the ticket inside the garbage can, with the boy's name visible, before more napkins cover it. The shot lasts less than a second, and because you're focused on the argument and all the chaos surrounding it, you can easily overlook. There's also no reaction from anyone to suggest that Peter has just made a mistake, so the movie simply moves on. You can see it at the 2:51 mark in the video:

And that little mistake Peter makes is relevant the following morning. During the night, a storm causes a power outage, resetting the house's alarm clocks. Therefore, the McCallisters oversleep and go into full panic mode as they try to make it to the airport on time. Kevin, who was sent to sleep in the attic after his fight with Buzz, is still asleep while everyone else gets ready to leave. And when Heather (Kristin Minter), one of Kevin's cousins, counts all the children before they get into the van, she accidentally includes a neighborhood boy who happens to be standing nearby with his back turned.

Now, imagine if Kevin's ticket had still been with the other documents. It would be natural to check everyone's tickets before boarding and wonder why there was one for a child who wasn't there. The difficulty of buying into the Home Alone 's premise isn't just something audiences started complaining about years later, as Heard acknowledged that the situation wasn't exactly easy to believe. In an interview with EDGE Magazine , the actor was asked how a family could board a plane to Europe without noticing they'd left one of their children behind. "Between the two of us, she had the more difficult part. So, all in all, the moment when Kevin's plane ticket gets thrown away isn't exactly a new discovery. Still, it's a detail that keeps on surprising most people, especially during the holiday season, when the film gets another round of rewatches . Is it a convenient chain of events? Sure, but at least it makes for a pretty entertaining comedy. Home Alone is a classic after all.

However, the original plan would have made this masterpiece legitimately groundbreaking.

Star Wars: Andor was a prequel to Rogue One: A Star Wars Story . While that movie is probably the most praised of Disney's tenure (although, The Last Jedi is actually the better movie, come at me in the comments), fans didn't really know what to expect from Andor . The show took things in a more mature direction, using the setting of the pre- A New Hope era to tell a story about fascism and how it affects everyone. However, there was a chance it could have been way mature, as Tony Gilroy has talked about the things Disney didn't let him say , including Maarva Andor dropping a certain F-shaped bomb in the greatest Star Wars scene of all-time.

So, going back to A New Hope , one of the things that appealed to fans so much about Star Wars was that it wasn't "pretty" sci-fi. This wasn't the Enterprise , with its spacious crew quarters and rounded off lines, the universe of Star Wars was gritty and jagged, with a lived-in quality that made it all seem real. The prequels changed that, as the Republic was more high tech and clean, but Andor brought it back. Most of the first season took place on Ferrix, a small colony world out on the periphery, a world where scrapping Clone Wars-era tech paid the bills.

Andor was a story about how the various social classes deal with fascism. Cassian, Bix, Brasso, Wilmon, and Maarva were in the lowest class, having to deal with a social system where they had no rights or power. There was a sense of tension throughout the first season (I mean, through the show in general, but it was one of the key parts of the first season's narrative), as everyone was pushed closer and closer to the breaking point by the circumstances of the galaxy. Maarva's funeral was an orgiastic moment of release for our main characters and the people of Ferrix, and it's one of the reasons the first season is so beloved.

Andor Season One was like communist theory, in a lot of ways, showing you why the system of the Empire was bad. A lot of people talk about The Trail of Political Consciousness , the anti-Imperial manifesto written by Karis Nemik, but the first season is basically his manifesto in macrocosm and it's why the F-bomb would have been perfect. "F-k the Empire" would have been the perfect way to illustrate where the characters were.

Andor was a lot like old Star Wars Legends , using ideas from the West End Games sourcebooks (I still have a bunch of those; they're great lore companions for Legends). One of the interesting thing about Legends is that the creators came up with their own curse words. The F-word equivalent was usually "kriff". The canon Expanded Universe does still use them and they are fun little Easter Eggs for people like me, who read all of that stuff when they were teenager. However, they never would have got the point across because only the diehards know them.

Adding the "F-k the Empire," line to Andor Season One would have been the perfect way to show how much pressure and stress everyone was under. The first season – and the show in general – worked so well because it was able to create this flawless atmosphere of tension; adding this line to the season would have worked out so well for several reasons

To begin with, it's never been done in Star Wars and it would get an immediate attention. You can look at like this – even if this is the first time we've heard that word, that means it does exist but it's one of the worst things you can say, and they could have played it like a certain curse word that is used a lot in Great Britain and Australia, but you can't say in the United States. We talk about a lot about "aura" and "vibes" in the 2020s, and the F-bomb's use in Andor would have had unassailable aura and immaculate vibes (I'm too old to say that, aren't I?).

It also would have another affect, something that show already did so well – make the audience understand how the characters felt. Andor wouldn't have been nearly as successful without its ability to connect the audience with the struggle onscreen . Having Maarva end her funeral speech with, "F-k the Empire!" would have made the scene that much more powerful because we've all been there. We've all been in that place where staying polite in the face of monstrous injustice is just too much and you need to get it out. That's what we could have had and it would have been glorious.

There are few scenes in Star Wars history as good as Maarva Andor's funeral. Even pulling back from its place in the story, it was a perfectly set-up, choreographed, acted, and shot scene. It was an example of everyone in the cast and crew putting their best work forward. It's a scene that still gives people goosebumps four years later. The only way it would have been better is if Maarva Andor got to end her speech with a posthumous F-bomb.

Star Wars has often struggled with how it is perceived. On the one hand, some of its original diehard fans are on Social Security right now, or about to be, and they've stayed with it all these years because there is a certain maturity to the franchise that still appeals to them. However, it's also a franchise where the creator was able to buy back the film rights because of toy sales. It has always had a little something for everybody; the younger fans got stuff like Star Wars Visions and The Ninth Jedi (anime is all the rage with the kids, he says, as he rubs his aching back) and the older fans got stuff like Andor (some of them will say the same about Revenge of the Sith , but that's because they were 8 when they first watched it and it felt dark and mature to them; it's just like my generation with Return of the Jedi ).

Putting the F-bomb in Andor would have a great moment. It definitely fit the show's tone and would have been an amazing moment that broke new ground in Star Wars . It also would do the one thing that the creators had done so well across the length of the show – further build the world of the show, making it more real. Honestly, I don't think really think there will ever be a more perfect moment to bring the F-bomb to the galaxy far, far away than this one. It was a missed opportunity.

The Bronze Age was an era of growing pains for DC Comics, who were struggling to adapt to a changing marketplace and increasingly stiff competition from Marvel. For readers, there was one clear benefit: the publisher understood themselves to be losing their grip on what was popular or appealing to its readers, and was willing to take risks. However, few Bronze Age titles are as iconic or as sought-after as the original Len Wein and Bernie Wrightson issues of Swamp Thing .

Besides Kirby's aforementioned Fourth World titles ( The New Gods especially), few Bronze Age DC titles are as collectible as Swamp Thing 's first volume. Wrightson lent interiors and stunning covers to the first ten, while Wein scripted the first thirteen. Changing censorship standards in the early seventies allowed for horror comics — after being functionally banned for more than a decade — to make their return to newsstands. A generation of creators who had grown up on the controversial E.C. titles of the 1950s were entering the field, and were eager to lend their talents to the genre. Anthology titles like House of Mystery and House of Secrets were converted into horror books, and strong reader response supported an explosion of new horror titles like The Witching Hour, The Phantom Stranger , and The Demon . Swamp Thing was spun-off from a short story in House of Secrets #92, also by Wein and Wrightson. For the ongoing series, the duo retold the Southern gothic origin of the character, updating it for the modern era. While House of Secrets ' Alex Olsen became Swamp Thing because of a fire set by a romantic rival, Swamp Thing 's Alec Holland was killed for refusing to give over the secrets of his world-hunger ending bio-restorative formula to a shady criminal cabal. What didn't change was Wrightson's striking design for Swamp Thing, which resembles the artist's later interpretation of the Frankenstein Monster .

A more direct take on the Frankenstein Monster would come the following issue with the "Patchwork Man," a demented experiment by the mad Anton Arcane, who revives his brother Gregori from the dead after he's blown apart by a forgotten World War-era landmine. For Len Wein's initial thirteen issues especially, Swamp Thing existed in a dark, gothic world that — but for a team-up with Batman in Swamp Thing #7 — felt totally outside the DC Universe.
