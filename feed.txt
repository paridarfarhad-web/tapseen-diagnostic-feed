No, the history of Daredevil's identity is simultaneously one of Marvel's strangest and funniest pieces of history, as Matt truly can't catch a break. Half of these reveals aren't even his fault, but somehow his reactions only get funnier over time. Today, we're diving into the newest identity reveal and Matt's various other accidental identity oustings throughout the years, going over exactly how it happens and why it works so well as a storyline for Matt specifically. After all, a guy doesn't own an &#8220;I'm Not Daredevil&#8221; Christmas sweater without having ten times more reasons than anyone should have.

Matt's newest slip of the mask came when Mister Fear launched a brand-new assault on New York City. The villain developed a drug that could completely kill fear in people's minds, and there was a drug lord bidding war to control it. However, the classic villain had also long since figured out Matt's dual life on his own, and given that he'd always been jealous of the Man Without Fear since he was outshone in law school, he decided to make things personal. He created a new strain of Fear Toxin that could target Matt's memories, dousing him with it while he was teaching his college course. Being the fearless hero he is, Matt eventually overcame this toxin, but affirmed his confidence by shouting that he was Daredevil, unaware that he was tied to his co-worker and new love interest, Sari Ellison.

Now, while this is definitely an unfortunate turn of events for everyone's favorite devil, it's actually one of his more dignified identity reveals. So, with the context of the newest take out of the way, let's dive into the hilarious number of times this has happened before, not even counting the times when Matt has chosen to do so. The first time was in Daredevil (1964) #9, but Dr. Van Eyck died in the same issue he uncovered it, funnily enough, just by putting two and two together by looking at both men's physiques. The second, and personally funniest, came in Daredevil (1964) #24, when Karen and Foggy learned Matt's identity because Spider-Man sent Matt a letter assuring him his identity was safe with him, having deduced it in issue #17.

To make it as clear as humanly possible, Spider-Man figured out Matt Murdock, a blind man, was Daredevil, and sent a handwritten letter to the place he worked explicitly saying this. There are at least three layers of stupidity to that, but what takes it from hilarious to downright side-busting is that Matt, in a fit of panic, insisted that it was actually his secret twin brother, Mike Murdock, who was Daredevil. Mike, of course, wasn't real, meaning that Matt had to occasionally take of fhis glasses and pretend to be a gangster type who could see to sell the illusion. This identity proceeded to be a cover for several years, even developing into his own person, at one point.

And trust me, that's far from the last of the shenanigans around this. Starr Saxon, a different Mister Fear, puzzled out his identity by hitting him with a pheromone trail he followed to Matt's home. A San Francisco news station put it together because Matt and Daredevil both moved to the city at the same time and had ties to Black Widow, which is probably his most embarrassing reveal, given it wasn't even anyone close to him or a villain. It was just a couple of reporters. Speaking of reporters, Ben Ulrich figured it out, and his notes were leaked by his assistant, forcing Matt to fake his death. Karen famously sold his name for drug money , which would eventually lead to the CIA learning it from Kingpin's files decades down the line.

The point is, Matt couldn't keep his identity a secret to save his life. These aren't even all of the examples, or counting when Matt gave it away willingly. He's had to employ some truly insane tactics to cover his tracks over the years, Mike Murdock notwithstanding. He's had numerous superheroic allies cover for him in his costume, from Iron Fist to Black Panther, who also figured it out on his own, FYI. Most recently, the Purple Man's children used their power to wipe Daredevil's identity from the world, letting Matt return to anonymity until about now. The cast of characters who know on both the heroic and villainous side is still kind of absurd, but it does reveal exactly why so many writers keep returning to this conflict, and why they keep undoing it.

Daredevil is, at his core, a man of juxtapositions. He's a deeply Catholic man who dresses like the Devil to inflict punishment on the unjust. He's a blind man who sees the world in more detail than anyone else could imagine. He's a lawyer who takes the law into his own hands to save others. It's that last one that draws on the inherent drama of his secret identity the most. All superheroes walk on the wrong side of the law to do what they do, but it's especially relevant to Matt because of his career and deep belief in the judicial system. Despite breaking it to be a hero, he still legitimately believes in it, and unlike someone like Spider-Man, his entire life crumbles apart at a fundamental level when that hypocrisy is dragged into the light.

If Spider-Man's identity is public, then his villains target his loved ones. If Matt's identity is public, then he's forced to defend the code of ethics he's used to justify his actions for years, all while fighting the ramifications of being a lawyer who regularly breaks the law. This singular event makes Matt's life topple like a house of cards in a wind tunnel and provides more than enough drama for an entire run between just the threat of it and the fallout. That's not even mentioning all of the inherent drama of his identity being revealed, which includes, as mentioned above, the danger it puts all of his loved ones in.

Status quo is king in comic books, yes, so whenever Daredevil's identity is revealed, you know that he's going to return to a secret identity at some point. That's just the way of the world with superhero identities. Plenty of Marvel's big names have cast their secret identities aside over the years, from Iron Man and Captain America to the X-Men, but for characters like Daredevil and Spider-Man, their masks hiding their true names are an iconic, expected part of their character. However, while the status quo is a rightfully cited reason Marvel undoes character changes and development, I truly can't say that is the main factor for Daredevil.

Simply put, the threat of Daredevil's identity being revealed is so much more interesting than a status quo where everything is public knowledge. The fallout of his reveal can sustain an entire run as he adjusts, but after the ground settles and Matt and the world have grown comfortable with his status as Daredevil, that drama is gone with nothing to replace it. Like I said, Matt is a character built around countless themes of duality and conflicting realities. Just as countless stories draw on his dual identity for drama, so do they use his faith and the violence he revels in, or the inherent tragedy of his abilities. Matt's best stories, his most impactful ones, always generate tension from one or more of these aspects of his character. Do away with any one and a vital piece of Daredevil is lost .

At the end of the day, Daredevil's secret identity is an essential aspect of his character, and stories that challenge it will always be in ample supply. The revelation to Sari is sure to put their connection in a new light, especially considering how much Matt likes her and how much she despised him for his apparent laziness and fear. If there's anything that Matt's torrid history with this secret has taught us, it's that this relationship is about to get turned up to eleven, and I cannot wait to watch it turn into an absolute dumpster fire, which is another core tenet of Daredevil's stories.

Which of Daredevil's identity reveals and recoveries is your favorite? For me, it has to be the physical letter Spider-Man sent to a blind man leading to a fake twin, because nothing written in the past sixty years of comics has come close to making me laugh that hard. Actually, scratch that, as the &#8220;I'm Not Daredevil&#8221; sweater is just insane enough to follow it up. Let us know your vote in the comments below!

Here are five sci-fi series from the 1990s that are not only great, but they got better with each season. These are the shows that we keep coming back to all these years later because of how good they are, but also for how they built on themselves to deliver from start to finish.

Sure, Star Trek: The Next Generation isn’t the first Star Trek series, but it is in many ways the absolutely definitive one. The series was solid from the start, debuting in 1987, but it was the third season that really saw the series make a massive leap and after that, it was only better from there. The first two seasons of The Next Generation were, admittedly, a little campier than the others, but they also had a lot of heavy lifting to do. They had to establish this iteration of the franchise, the characters, and really get us invested but starting in Season 3, the stories became even more evolved, the characters more richly developed, and saw not only the series reach its full potential every season after that but also expanded the Star Trek universe in a way that made it one of the most beloved IP in not just sci-fi but entertainment on the whole. The Next Generation is an example of a series that never diminished in quality, which is rare.

I feel like we don’t give Babylon 5 nearly the appreciation it deserves, but the series, which ran from 1993 to 1998, is one of the genuinely great sci-fi series of all time and a huge part of that comes down to how it was planned. Babylon 5 , unlike most sci-fi series of its era, started with a complete five-year plan. Series creator J. Michael Straczynski knew going into the very first episode where he wanted things to go. This meant that there was a path forward for each story, each character, each arc. The first season set things up, specifically the politics and organization of the world and lore and then was able to use that basis to create something that had not only thorough storytelling, but rich emotional development. The result is a series that simply got better and better because it knew where it was going. There’s really not been anything quite like it since.

The X-Files ran for 9 seasons in its original run between 12993 and 2002, but the series varies wildly. Seasons 1 through 5 are considered the best of the series and if you look just at those five seasons, it’s definitely a situation where the show just got better and better. The first season of The X-Files set the stage but by the time you get to Season 3, you have a series that has built on its conspiracies as well as the monster of the week format to deliver something intriguing. Even now, if you ask a fan about the best episodes of the series, they’re going to be found here. Things shift by the time we get to the sixth season — there are some production changes and the conspiracy elements of the show start to get a little unfocused here — and the whole thing declines after that, but those first five seasons just kept building on each other making The X-Files a classic.

If you’re not familiar, 3rd Rock From the Sun follows four extraterrestrials who come to Earth as part of a research expedition and, to carry this out, they pose as a normal human family in Ohio. But what about his best horror performance? A case could be made for War of the Worlds , although its action-adventure elements still dominate. This leaves only Cruise’s 1994 role in Neil Jordan's adaptation of Anne Rice's novel Interview with the Vampire as his most notable horror performance. Cruise stars as the charismatic vampire Lestat alongside a young Kirsten Dunst as Claudia and Brad Pitt as Louis de Pointe du Lac, who recounts his centuries-long life as a vampire to Christian Slater’s journalist. Yet despite the stacked cast, Cruise remains the film's standout, particularly because he takes his leading-man reputation and turns it on its head.

Before Interview with the Vampire , Cruise had already established his reputation through performances in Top Gun , Rain Man , Born on the Fourth of July , A Few Good Men , and The Firm . In the early 90s, he was increasingly moving towards serious dramatic roles but still favored sympathetic leading characters. Not only was Lestat de Lioncourt a substantial departure from the good-guy image, but it didn’t fit squarely into either action or prestige drama. At the time of his casting, the disconnect was so great that author Anne Rice publicly objected to Cruise playing Lestat. She considered him an incorrect choice for the aristocratic, flamboyant vampire she’s created. She roasted the decision in the press, calling it &#8220;the worst crime in the name of casting since The Bonfire of the Vanities&#8221; and &#8220;so bizarre, it's almost impossible to imagine how it's going to work.&#8221;

Yet Cruise shocked the world by not only giving us a fantastic Lestat, but also delivering the best performance in the film. Even Rice came around after seeing the movie, placing a self-funded two-page ad in The New York Times apologizing for her earlier public criticism of Cruise's casting , stating, “I never dreamed it would turn out this way.” As Rice and many critics of the time noted, Cruise’s Lestat works so well not despite his public persona, but precisely because of it, using his leading-man charm to make Lestat more convincing to both the characters and the audience. Lestat’s handsome confidence and humor are offset beautifully when he is revealed as a remorseless killer who treats human beings as disposable, using seduction as his weapon of choice.

The complexities Cruise communicates through Lestat are incredible. When Lestat offers Louis the opportunity to become a vampire, he promises him the choice he himself was denied, and the restraint with which Cruise delivers the line lets the character's loneliness peek through the veneer. Simultaneously, the offer is manipulative: Lestat wants a companion and sees Louis as someone who can fill that role. Is the vulnerability real or part of the manipulation? Therein lies the brilliance of Cruise’s performance, which only becomes more openly cruel as Louis struggles with his new existence. Perhaps most unsettling is when Lestat turns Dunst's Claudia (another standout performance) into a vampire and presents her as their new daughter, intending the unconventional family arrangement to solve their problems. Cruise's enthusiasm and excitement at having condemned a child to an eternity is genuinely horrifying.

Though critics were initially mixed upon the film’s release, the movie received two technical Oscar nominations: Best Art Direction and Best Original Score. Interview with the Vampire has since accrued a dedicated cult following among genre movie lovers. For all its camp and melodrama, the movie remains genuinely emotionally devastating, thanks in part to the nuance Cruise brings to the screen. A satisfying mix of horror, romance, and drama with doomed blood-suckers at its center, Interview With the Vampire deserves recognition among the genre's finest films.

While Cruise's performance is a major reason Interview with the Vampire holds up, the film's timelessness also comes from its execution. Neil Jordan whips up an elaborate gothic world stretching from New Orleans to Paris with help from production designer Dante Ferretti, cinematographer Philippe Rousselot, and composer Elliot Goldenthal. As for the ensemble, Pitt gives Louis a melancholy that plays beautifully off Lestat's flamboyance, while 11-year-old Dunst delivers an extraordinary early-career performance as Claudia, even earning her a Golden Globe nomination (she apparently lost the win by a single vote). Antonio Banderas, Stephen Rea, and Christian Slater round out the stellar cast.

In 2022, the AMC TV adaptation of Interview with the Vampire also received critical acclaim. Starring Sam Reid, Jacob Anderson, and Bailey Bass, the series supposedly took substantial liberties with Rice's original story . Rice and her son Christopher were involved in the series' development. However, she died before its premiere, making it impossible to know how she would have responded to the changes or finished adaptation—or whether another smear campaign or two-page apology ad would have been in order. Both versions have their merits, but with the Rice seal of approval, Neil Jordan's 1994 film remains the definitive adaptation of Interview with the Vampire in which Tom Cruise gives one of the most distinctive performances of his career and, to date, his finest work in horror.

As a result, depending on who you ask, Vampirella is an alien from planet Drakulon, the daughter of the Biblical Lilith, a scream queen, a secret monster-hunting agent for the Vatican, and the bride of Dracula. For the past seven years, Christopher Priest has been at the helm of Dynamite’s Vampirella, and has somehow taken all of these disparate elements and made them (mostly) work in one canon.

To begin with, yes, really: Drakulon. Vampirella’s classic comic book origin is absolutely ridiculous, though her real-world origins carry a surprising pedigree. The character is credited, broadly, as emerging from the imagination of Famous Monsters editor Forrest J. Ackerman. Apocryphally, the name came from Ackerman combining “vampire” and Barbarella , the Roger Vadim film adaptation of which was a major cult hit the previous year. Underground comic writer and artist (and later comic book historian) Trina Robbins designed Vampirella’s costume and overall look, some of her first major mainstream work. Tom Sutton illustrated her first appearance, while Frank Frazetta contributed the cover. Ackerman wrote the issue, which introduced Vampirella as an alien from Drakulon, planet of the vampires, where blood flows from great rivers. Faced with a great catastrophe as Drakulon’s rivers dry and its people starve, Vampirella is forced to flee the planet for Earth, hitching a ride aboard a crash-landed space probe. On her journey to Earth, Vampirella discovers that the blood she needs to survive flows in the veins of these alien “humans,” forcing her to kill for sustinence.

Vampirella disappeared to the background of her magazine for its first few years, serving as a hostess to the anthology horror stories within, introducing them like the Crypt-Keeper or DC’s Cain and Abel. Sporadically, she’d get a short feature story. Readers responded well to her stories, and she gradually took over the book. Naturally, this eventually included a confrontation with Dracula, revealed as a member of her own species, the “Vampiri.” Eventually curing herself of her thirst for blood through the Necronomicon-esque book of magic the “Crimson Chronicles,” Vampirella became a force for good and a hunter of monsters, teaming with characters like the sorcerer Pendragon and Adam Van Helsing, descendant of Dracula’s nemesis. The Warren run lasted one hundred and thirteen issues, ending in 1983 when Warren Publishing filed for bankruptcy. Harris Publications bought their assets at auction, this included Vampirella. Harris reprinted the entire Warren run throughout the eighties, finally launching a new Vampirella title in 1991 – naturally coming in the midst of the era’s comic speculation boom.

Kurt Busiek revamped Vampirella’s origins for Harris Publication, introducing the alternate backstory that she is the daughter of Lilith, the first wife of Adam and the “mother of demons” in traditional Judeo-Christian myth. Repentant, Lilith trained her daughter Vampirella to battle the demons she had spawned. Warren Ellis incorporated “Drakulon” back into canon with his Vampirella Lives mini-series, which establishes it as Vampirella’s birthplace and a realm of Hell. Grant Morrison and Mark Millar’s work on the Vampirella ongoing title is considered a high water mark for the Harris era, featuring religious conspiracies, vampire gangsters, and a new nemesis for Vampi with the lich Von Kriest. Characters and continuity from Harris were kept roughly intact for the early Dynamite era, which also reintroduced Warren-era characters like Adam Van Helsing and Pendragon. A reboot reimagined her as an agent of a B.P.R.D-esque monster-hunting organization run by the Vatican; another reboot put her in a dystopian future. For the better part of a decade, Christopher Priest has been trying to make all of these interpretations make sense together.

For the most part, Priest has succeeded: his Vampirella run has been upheld by readers as one of the best iterations of the title, with more complex characterization and intricate storytelling than many of its precursors. That it’s managed to fly under the radar is a reminder that Vampirella remains a cult character, maintaining a dedicated fanbase while never cracking most readers’ pull list. It’s that niche status that enables writers like Priest, Morrison, or Alan Moore (who penned a 1997 Vampirella/ Dracula cross-over) to exercise unrestrained creativity when writing for Vampirella: they aren’t subject to the same pressures and oversight that comes with working on a mainstream title.

Tying together science fiction, horror, and a traditional super-hero comic, Vampirella is a surprisingly versatile character. While the combination of these genres doesn’t work for every reader, for those who it resonates with, it really resonates , making Vampirella an ardent favorite of theirs. It’s those hardcore fans that drive the high aftermarket prices on her classic Warren appearances, with Vampirella #1 seldom selling for less than $500. The copy on auction at Heritage has already attained a price of more than $3k – higher than average for the book in high grade condition, but explicable given its exceptional 9.6 Near-Mint condition. With more than a week left to bid, the price is likely to climb even higher, cementing Vampirella’s status once again as an underrated icon, far more than an outrageous costume and a campy name.
