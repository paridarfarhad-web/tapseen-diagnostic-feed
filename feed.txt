The history of the anime industry dates back several decades, but the medium finally began becoming popular in Japan during the 1950s and 1960s, thanks to the color theatrical features and the rise of TV animation. Several classics across all demographics were released during that era, and it goes without saying that Shonen was more popular than others even back then. However, while those series were pretty popular during the time of their broadcast, most of them couldn't stand the test of time when it became increasingly difficult for fans to watch them later on.

This is especially true for fans in the U.S. since several older classics are difficult to watch even now. However, Criterion made it possible to stream Speed Racer , one of the most legendary anime shows of all time. While it isn't an obscure show anymore, thanks to the Hollywood live-action adaptation by the Wachowski sisters in 2008, the anime itself has been difficult to watch for a while now. The official Instagram handle of the service revealed its December 2026 lineup, where Speed Racer was also listed. The handle confirms that the complete series will be available on the platform in both Japanese audio with subtitles and the English dub as well.

Based on an iconic manga written and illustrated by Tatsuo Yoshida, Speed Racer premiered in 1967 and ran for 52 episodes. The story centers around Go Mifune, who pilots the Mach 5, the name of the technologically advanced racing car built by his father, Pops Racer. The series doesn't shy away from portraying the brutality of the world of professional racing as Go gets entangled with all kinds of underhanded plots of corrupt businessmen and obstacles set by his rivals.

The visuals and color palette might give the impression that it's a kid's show, but it handles mature themes that are suitable for viewers of all ages. Not to mention that the best part of the anime was that, unlike typical racing stories, it blends family drama, political intrigue, and automotive fantasy.

Established in 1962, Tatsunoko Production eventually became one of the oldest animation studios in Japan that's relevant to this day. Over the decades, the animation studio has released more than 200 anime, including films and series. It's known for several acclaimed shows, such as Ping Pong the Animation , Psycho-Pass , and many more.

The studio thrived during the 1970s and 1980s, when there wasn’t an endless stream of anime TV shows being released for global fans. However, its legacy is mostly thanks to the success of Speed Racer , which helped the studio gain a strong foothold in the industry not long after its establishment. The studio even released a reboot and spin-off in an attempt to keep this series relevant for the next few decades.

Of course, she only appeared for one issue, and it's clear that this version served as a testing ground for the conceit of the character, but therein lies a lost art of comic book character introductions. Super-Girl is one of the most pivotal characters in DC, and she's the embodiment of this forgotten style of superheroic introductions.

This version of Super-Girl made her singular appearance in Superman (1939) #123. Jimmy Olsen, as he is wont to do, came into possession of a wish-granting staff, and his first thought was to create a female companion who could fight alongside Superman to keep him safe and give him companionship. Super-Girl fit the bill perfectly, proving she had all of Superman's many marvelous powers. However, she lacked the experience he did, so she wound up getting in his way more often than she did help him. She even nearly revealed his identity to Lois after X-raying through his outer outfit. In the end, however, she wound up saving the Man of Steel, sacrificing herself to save Superman from a Kryptonite attack. As she succumbed to Kryptonite poisoning, she asked that Jimmy undo his wish, making her cease to exist.

It's a morbid end to this introduction, but it did what it had to do, as it posed the question of whether fans would want a Supergirl in the first place. By creating a character made to expire by the end of the issue, the creators were able to gauge fans' reactions to Super-Girl as a concept. If they hated her, they could sweep this under the rug as a one-off adventure. But since they loved Super-Girl, they were incentivized to create the proper version just nine months later. Obviously, introducing a character like Kara wasn't something they could walk back from, and the heads at DC were likely very nervous about the whole thing, so using Super-Girl as a test was a perfect middle ground. On top of that, she serves as the perfect example of how characters used to be introduced .

Super-Girl's origin is definitely strange, but back in the day, the idea of introducing a superhero in the main issue of another character's ongoing was the premier way of debuting. Often, these heroes would join in on a story and then race off to their own back issues or side adventures in an anthology book, before eventually claiming their own place in an ongoing, if they proved popular enough. Supergirl would take that path, but she more often served as a main and side character in Superman's adventures as well, with other characters like the Elongated Man also fitting this role.

Nowadays, however, breakout heroes don't tend to be introduced in this same way. Instead, more often than not, they debut in team books. Don't get me wrong, plenty of new heroes grace heroes' main book pages every month, but they aren't typically designed as breakout stars. Jade Tiger swung onto the pages of Batgirl (2025) and Marilyn Moonlight in Superman (2023), but these characters more serve as supporting cast members rather than heroes designed to lead their own adventures. Meanwhile, team books like Teen Titans (2026) and New Titans (2026) introduce entire casts of new characters, from the bionic Eva to the bad-luck-inducing Wildcard.

It makes sense to throw a new hero designed to break away onto a team, as it gives them a reason to stay in the public eye for a longer time as they battle alongside established heroes instead of relying on one or two issues for them to build an audience. New characters still do show up in solo books, but it's much rarer, as it takes a much bigger chance that the character will make a scant few appearances before being relegated to event-only limbo, or even worse, back-up feature-only jail. Still, DC testing the waters with more heroes debuting in solo adventures could be something to explore once again, as it keeps the focus squarely on one or two heroes instead of an entire team, which can easily be spread too thin.
