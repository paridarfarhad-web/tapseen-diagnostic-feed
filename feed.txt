&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google The Xbox console series has released two systems on November 22nd, once in 2005 with the highly celebrated Xbox 360 and again in 2013 with the more controversial Xbox One. While definitely a marketing strategy that didn't apply to the 2020 launch of the current Xbox Series X|S console on November 10th, the last two devices in the Xbox line-up couldn't have been received more differently by audiences. In many ways, the lasting legacy of the Xbox 360 and Xbox One represent the highs and lows of Xbox as a gaming company, with their mistakes from both eras still existing as something players feel today.

The next Xbox console is currently called "Project Helix" according to internal sources, with predictions estimating that it could come out around the 2027 or 2028 holiday season. With Xbox laying off over 3,000 employees in their gaming division during a massive "reset" of the company, the 2028 date is far more likely as studios close down and scrutiny of Xbox is higher than ever before. Industry trends such as the skyrocketing costs of manufacturing parts of any electronic device will also play a key part in Project Helix's release, marking a much different atmosphere to the Xbox 360 and Xbox One's time in the sun.

The time periods of gaming when the Xbox 360 and Xbox One released couldn't be more different, with each marking a different kind of evolution of console quality and exclusivity. Microsoft likely released the Xbox One on the same day as the 360 once did due to the former console's celebrated status, as it still remains one of the most influential home systems in the industry to this day. Without the Xbox 360, the modern landscape of online play simply wouldn't exist, from Xbox Live and Xbox Game Pass to PlayStation+, Nintendo Online, and similar features. Across eight years, the Xbox 360 became a gaming cornerstone for a variety of reasons.

At the time, the Xbox 360 was widely considered much better than even the Nintendo Wii, whose strange motion control systems were associated more with family or party games. Meanwhile, the PlayStation 3 was lacking the innovations of the Xbox 360 when it came to accessibility and quality-of-life, making it feel like another version of the PS2 rather than something new. While PlayStation 3 games were better by comparison, the compact controller of the Xbox 360 was almost perfect, setting a standard other consoles have tried to follow years later. For that reason, it's no wonder why the Xbox One wanted to capture that nostalgia by following the 360's release date.

The beginning of the Xbox 360's reign is hardly as celebrated as you might think by the time the console's life cycle ended, mainly due to one infamous hardware problem. The "red ring of death" was an issue for the earliest version of the Xbox 360, where players' entire consoles bricked after a red ring showed up on their power button. This caused tons of recalls, refunds, or big wastes of money of some fans' parts, leading to massive frustration around the console. However, this didn't take away from the easier time developers had actually making games that appeared on the console itself.

The really easy controller design (both in style and connectivity) helped any player become familiar with the Xbox 360 in some way, with standout games like Halo: Reach , Gears of War , and Forza being great exclusives on the console too. The hardware eventually became reliable enough to host large multiplayer groups, creating LAN parties for games like Halo 3 that are legendary among players who remember that era. Speaking of multiplayer, the Xbox 360 was revolutionary in the way that it pioneered online functionality, allowing players to engage in a variety of social features other consoles simply didn't have.

For example, the Xbox 360 allowed groups of players to add each other as friends in an online ecosystem, which in turn set up online messaging chat rooms for better communication. Players could jump into party calls as a whole group or just with one individual friend, primarily to coordinate jumping into a specific game together for multiplayer experiences. Unique profiles through gamertags helped players establish online identities here too, creating social circles that created lasting memories for years to come.

By contrast, the Xbox One was leagues below the Xbox 360 in terms of overall impact. While the Xbox 360 didn't have to compete much against other consoles, the Xbox One had to deal with the PlayStation 4, which was a far more robust device with a greater number of high-quality exclusives. Everything from Insomniac's Marvel's Spider-Man , the new Norse God of War games, and other great titles that didn't appear on the Xbox One at all, making the console feel like it was a step behind. However, games weren't the only reason the Xbox One failed, as business practices surrounding it also put it at a disadvantage.

The Xbox One released during a time when the console was extremely overpriced compared to other consoles on the market, being sold as a "complete entertainment system" for movies, TV, and streaming alongside games. The multimedia approach wasn't necessarily a bad thing in the long run, as new consoles like the PS5 have that setup now. For the time, though, the Xbox One's price didn't justify that strategy, especially when players figured out the console was planning on having daily online checks and a lending of physical games rather than ownership of them. Long before the PS5 announced the end of physical game production, the Xbox One was doing it first.

Higher pricing also applied to the Xbox One's strange side piece of hardware — the Xbox Kinect. The Kinect was a strange way Microsoft was trying to compete with Nintendo, mimicking the Nintendo Wii's motion control systems with voice activated commands and players' physical actions being mapped to the games they played. Needless to say, this was not the right move, often leading to glitches and strange games that players had to pay hundreds of dollars for. This also started Microsoft's era to buy up various game studios at seemingly random, while not putting out any great exclusives that helped sell the console as part of those partnerships.

Constant struggles to create first-party games is ultimately what sank the Xbox One, even if players praised the Xbox Game Pass library that offered game catalogs for a subscription price. Weaker hardware options and damaged player trust caused many people to switch to the PS4, and even more to the Nintendo Switch when it eventually released during the Xbox One's time. While the Xbox Series X|S is a step in the right direction, the missteps of the Xbox One are a shame, as the evergreen popularity of the Xbox 360 once marked Microsoft as on top of the competition before its big collapse.

&nbsp; Share on X (Opens in new window) X Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Add Us On Google Animation has given us many classic movies over the years. Naturally, the name Disney is one of the first evoked by any mention of the genre, but there are several other movie studios that have built remarkable reputations for success off the back of their animated films. Pixar, DreamWorks, Studio Ghibli — some of the best animated movies ever made have come from various different studios and filmmakers. Considering the genre built its success on family-friendly fare, it's perhaps unsurprising that this is the most common perception of its movies: that they're mostly just for kids. However, that stance dismisses and diminishes the quality of some truly exceptional films.

Throughout the genre's history, there have been many great animated movies that are all too quickly dismissed by general audiences. The perception that animated films only have something to offer to the youngest members of their audiences is the sort of rash, sweeping generalization that glosses over some truly great movies. Each of the following is an exceptional film that also happens to be animated, but the notion that they were made just for kids ultimately hurt their chances for the success they deserved.

The Iron Giant is fondly remembered today by the many '90s kids who were emotionally traumatized by it upon its original release. Brad Bird's 1999 movie told the story of a young boy who befriends a huge, alien robot, set against the backdrop of a particularly paranoid 1950s America. The Cold War subtext was naturally lost on many of the movie's younger audience members, but most adults were quick to dismiss the movie due to its animation style, which evoked the Disney movies of the era. This led to the awesome sci-fi movie being a box office flop in spite of its incredible quality.

In the years since, the brilliance of The Iron Giant has been more widely recognized, but that doesn't do anything to change its lack of initial success. The movie's deeper narrative themes and emotional weight gave it many qualities that only older viewers could truly appreciate, and it has since come to be considered one of the best animated movies of its time. The idea that The Iron Giant was just for kids might have since been dispelled, but that can never change the fact that the movie struggled at the box office as a result of that misguided perception.

Atlantis: The Lost Empire was released during an incredibly interesting period of Disney animation. After spending the 1990s making the animated musicals that are most commonly associated with the studio, 2001 saw them try something decidedly different with an animated adventure movie inspired by classic pulp fiction and the sci-fi works of writers such as Jules Verne. Unfortunately, Disney underestimated just how much their movies were perceived as being specifically for kids, and Atlantis: The Lost Empire struggled as a result.

Now remembered as one of Disney's most underrated movies , Atlantis: The Lost Empire has come to be considered a cult classic. However, the scope of its fantastical sci-fi adventure was something that, as well as being one of Disney's most ambitious experiments, deserved to secure a much wider audience. Sadly, the Disney label, combined with marketing that failed to communicate the movie's universal appeal, saw Atlantis dismissed as just another kids movie for far too long.

Titan A.E. is perhaps the most obscure title on this list, but it's probably also the one with the biggest cult following. The year 2000 saw Don Bluth turn his hand to making a sprawling space opera using the animation techniques that had seen him deliver numerous successful movies, resulting in Titan A.E ., an animated movie with a cast packed with huge Hollywood stars . Its story followed Cale Tucker, a young man in the 31st century tasked with protecting a giant spaceship that could save humanity after the destruction of Earth.

Despite the strength of its epic sci-fi premise, Titan A.E. was unable to secure the success or attention it deserved. The style of its animation led to many people dismissing it as another adventure movie made for kids, but the level of violence and the complexities of its plot meant that it also struggled to appeal to young children. This unfortunately led to the movie becoming a box office bomb that effectively killed Fox Animation Studios, which is especially tragic considering just how excellent Titan A.E. actually is.

There are a handful of Disney box office failures that eventually became fan-favorite movies , but none as well-known as Treasure Planet . A particularly ambitious adaptation of Robert Louis Stevenson's literary classic Treasure Island , the movie sought to transplant its story into outer space. A breathtaking combination of traditional hand-drawn animation and computer-generated imagery, Treasure Planet remains one of Disney's most original adaptations of a previously existing story, and has since come to be considered one of the best animated movies of the 2000s.

Treasure Planet 's underwhelming marketing campaign did nothing to help dispel the notion that it was purely for children. Its appeal wasn't at all communicated by promotional material, with adults who would have enjoyed its sci-fi reimagining of a familiar story instead seeing it as just another animated Disney movie. This was one of the major factors in the movie becoming a financial disappointment, and while Treasure Planet proved to be one of the best Disney movies of all time, it took years to build that reputation because of how it was initially dismissed by so many.

Shane Acker's 9 is one of the strangest and most interesting animated movies of the 21st century so far. The post-apocalyptic sci-fi story is set in an alternate version of the 1940s and follows the titular rag doll who awakens shortly after the uprising of machines, which in turn led to the eradication of humanity. The only resistance against the machines is the rag doll Stitchpunks, setting a particularly bleak backdrop for the animated movie. Perhaps unsurprisingly, 9 struggled to find its audience.

Many thought the film seemed too dark for young children, but a lot of older audiences saw the animated movie as being aimed at another demographic. Existing in this liminal space between the two, 9 's brilliance wasn't recognized widely enough, and it passed into relative obscurity. With the movie's potential sequel seeming destined to go unmade, it is now remembered as a great standalone animated movie that deserved much greater success. It's now clear that it was never truly intended for a particularly young audience, but that its distinct visual identity and complex premise should have been better targeted at more mature viewers.

The bizarre Max Headroom incident was actually two hijackings in one night and it started on Chicago’s WGN-TV. At 9:14 p.m. during the sports segment of the station’s The Nine O’clock News, the regular broadcast went black for viewers at home for roughly 15 seconds before returning only it wasn’t the sports they got back. The truth is we might never know who was behind this wild, weird moment in television history — but it is also something that can’t happen again. The '90s had shows like X-Files, Oz, Star Trek: Deep Space Nine , while future classics like The Sopranos, Sex in the City , and Six Feet Under began. These shows formed the basis of what has been called the Golden Age of Television, which began in the '00s. There are some brilliant shows from the first decade of the 2000s, but there's one show deserves the title of best TV show of the '00s: Battlestar Galactica .

The original Battlestar Galactica was network TV's answer to Star Wars , a show that definitely looked and felt like it was from the late '70s in the worst ways. While it had its fans, it just never found a big enough audience for how much it cost to make and would become one of many lost sci-fi series. However, Ronald D. Moore, one of Star Trek 's best writers, and David Eick would end up bringing the show back on the then-Sci-Fi Channel in 2004, starting with a four-part miniseries. Battlestar Galactica isn't a perfect show – some fans find its last season disappointing – but it really is the best show of the decade, beating out numerous amazing shows for the title.

The early '00s were a great time to be watching TV. HBO Sundays were becoming the home of the best TV shows around, the networks were putting out shows like 24 and Lost , and Adult Swim on Cartoon Network was making adult animation into a force to be reckoned with. The Sopranos was the 500-pound gorilla in the room, but it felt like there was something amazing on TV every night. The '90s had been the decade of cable, with many new channels popping up, as well, broadening the mixture of shows.

The Sci-Fi Channel launched in 1992, playing reruns of old sci-fi and genre shows. Eventually, they started to make their own original series and Moore and Eick pitched the network the reboot. Sci-Fi wanted the show and casting would begin in Canada. The show was meant to be the focus of a new programming slate, using a recognizable old name to get fans old and new through the door.

The original Battlestar Galactica saw the titular ship escape the destruction of the Colonies at the hands of Cylons, leading a fleet of civilians ships into the stars to find the original home of humanity – Earth. The new series would copy this idea, using many of the same names of characters, ships, and places, but changed everything else. The original Battlestar Galactica was a Star Trek/Star Wars style sci-fi universe, but the BSG reboot would take place in a more grounded universe. Gone were the weird alien races, the lasers, and the bizarre clothes. In their place were the Greek Gods, a whole lot of ammunition, and fashion that looked like it stepped out of 2004 Vancouver (because it did).

Moore and Eick wanted to use the series to talk about the then-current War on Terror. The Cylons were the creations of humanity who had rebelled. The first war almost destroyed humanity, but the Cylons retreated out of nowhere leading to decades of detente. The miniseries takes a page from the 9/11 playbook, as the Cylons launch a massive sneak attack on the Colonies of Man, slaughtering billions, with only the Galactica and a fleet of civilian ships escaping. The ship rearmed and then set course past the Red Line – the edge of known space – as they searched for the homeworld of mankind, Earth.

The show starred Edward James Olmos, Katee Sackhoff, Mary McDonnell, Jamie Bamber, Michael Hogan, James Callis (Baltar is the best), Tricia Helfer, and numerous others. The original series was the brainchild of Glenn Larson and it was very Mormon. Moore and Eick went in a completely direction, with the Colonials being polytheistic worshipers of the Lords of Kobol, our Greek Gods, while the machine Cylons were monotheistic, worshiping the one "true" god. This focus on religion and culture was different from any other sci-fi show out there, giving it a unique flavor.

The show was military sci-fi with a religious edge. Back in the early '00s, the right was selling the War on Terror as a clash of civilizations and Moore and Eick took the show in that direction, while also showing how foolish that was by the end of the story. The characters were able to inhabit their roles perfectly, and as the episodes dug into them, their past, and the trauma of the destruction of mankind, we watched them change and grow. It felt like nearly every character had an arc of some kind; they all started in one place and ended up in a complete other (in more ways than one).

Of course, there are some complaints that the show can't duck. To begin with, Moore and Eick had no plan on how to end the series and when you rewatch it, it shows. The last season is an interesting beast because it does tie up all the plot threads, but the way it did it made a lot of fans angry. Without getting too into the weeds of it all, the show went to places that sci-fi fans, one of the atheist/agnostic fandoms out there, didn't really like. The final episode is one you either love completely or don't like at all (I personally love it and have watched it more than any other finale, but I get why other people don't like it). However, I think the flaws of the show make it what it is. It's one of the best rides in TV history, and was able to be more interesting than anything else in the decade.

The '00s had three of the best TV shows of all-time – The Sopranos, Mad Men, and Breaking Bad . The shows all had the same throughline – a badly behaving patriarch – and gave viewers some of the best performances imaginable. You can point to all of them as nearly flawless examples of everything that TV should be. However, I think that even though it definitely had its flaws, Battlestar Galactica was better than all of three of them.

To begin with, it became appointment TV in a way those shows didn't. Friends would get together to watch it every week. The fandom was so engaged by the show, helped along by Moore and Eick using the Internet to communicate more with them. It honestly felt like a true fan community

The show had a lot to say about the moment in time that was the '00s, letting us take a step back from the goings on of the day and see it from another perspective. It had characters that you fell in love with; sure, those other shows all had great characters, but could you love them? Most of them were monsters of some kind. The characters of BSG were flawed, stressed out, traumatized, and exhausted, but they were good people in a terrible situation. TV reboots can be all over the place , but Battlestar Galactica was able to create an amazing narrative peopled by brilliant characters in a way that no other show of the decade could.
